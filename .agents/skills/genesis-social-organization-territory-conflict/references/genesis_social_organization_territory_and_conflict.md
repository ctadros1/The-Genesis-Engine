# The Genesis Engine: Social Organization, Territoriality, Cooperation, and Conflict

**Engineering-oriented scientific review for a deterministic artificial-life simulation**  
**Prepared:** 2026-08-04  
**Encoding:** UTF-8  

> **Scope.** This document asks what *minimum embodied mechanisms and ecological conditions* could make grouping, kin bias, cooperation, territoriality, coalition formation, and recurrent inter-group conflict possible without defining factions, political systems, diplomatic states, group objectives, or a technology tree. It is a narrative engineering synthesis rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. Foundational results, major counterarguments, artificial-system demonstrations, and falsifiable implementation tests are emphasized.

> **Core rule.** The simulation should contain individuals, bodies, cues, memories, actions, resources, signals, artifacts, spatial constraints, and consequences. It should not contain a privileged fact saying “these individuals are a group” or “this polygon belongs to faction X.” Group, culture, tradition, territory, species, and era labels should be generated by offline analysis and must never be fed back into organism controllers.

## 1. Executive summary

### 1.1 Principal conclusion

An explicit group identifier is **not required** for stable aggregation, affiliative clustering, kin-biased interaction, local cooperation, territorial occupancy, or even coalitionary action. In fact, a globally readable group ID would create an overly direct route to factions: a controller could implement `help same_id`, `attack different_id`, and `follow leader_id` without solving recognition, learning, partner choice, communication, or coordination. That would make many headline outcomes true by construction.

The least scripted viable route is a dependency stack:

1. **Embodied local interaction:** bounded perception, collision avoidance, approach/withdraw/follow behavior, occlusion, and spatially structured encounter opportunities.
2. **Ecological aggregation:** renewable resources, shelters, hazards, reproductive opportunities, and structures whose locations are uneven enough to create repeated co-presence.
3. **Perceptible social cues:** heritable phenotype components, movement styles, emissions, carried objects, and location history—never direct access to genotype distance, pedigree, or observer labels.
4. **Limited associative memory:** familiarity, recent treatment, local place value, and optionally learned individual fingerprints with deterministic capacity limits and decay.
5. **Costly social affordances:** carrying, dropping, transfer, joint manipulation, shared structures, defense, and public-good production whose donor costs and recipient benefits can be measured.
6. **Low-bandwidth communication:** observable movement and actions first; later, costly local signals and decaying environmental markers with no predefined semantics.
7. **Spatial memory and site value:** learned expectations about resources, safety, neighbors, structures, caches, and prior contests.
8. **Damage and injury only after earlier stages work:** attack actions, energetic costs, recovery, impaired performance, and death make aggression consequential, but do not themselves create organized conflict.
9. **Observational or social learning only after behavioral variants exist:** this allows conventions and traditions to persist across turnover without embedding “culture” as a state variable.

### 1.2 What can arise from simple rules, and what usually cannot

Local attraction, repulsion, alignment, following, and shared response to environmental gradients can produce cohesive motion and spatial aggregation without identity, memory, signaling, or leadership. Models and experiments on collective motion show abrupt group-level transitions from small changes in local rules or density (Couzin et al., 2002; Buhl et al., 2006). A minority with directional information can guide a group even when others do not know who is informed (Couzin et al., 2005). These results justify starting Genesis with local embodied rules rather than a social hierarchy.

Stable cooperation is a separate problem. It can arise through several mechanisms that should not be collapsed into one:

- **Mutualism and by-product benefits** can require almost no social cognition.
- **Kin-selected helping** requires positive assortment among heritable variants, but not necessarily explicit kin recognition.
- **Partner choice and exit** require recognizing profitable interactions but can be simpler than calculating reputations.
- **Direct reciprocity** requires repeated interaction plus enough memory to condition behavior on a partner's past conduct; evolutionary-robotics results warn that reciprocity may be difficult to bootstrap even when it would be advantageous once established.
- **Indirect reciprocity and reputation** require observation, identity resolution, information transfer, and assessment rules; they are not minimal mechanisms.
- **Punishment and policing** may stabilize public goods, but create their own costs, errors, retaliation, and second-order free-rider problems.
- **Stigmergic cooperation** can emerge through persistent environmental changes without identity or symbolic communication.

Territory also should not be a privileged world object. The recommended initial model is:

- the world stores resources, structures, caches, occupancy traces, and decaying markers;
- organisms learn spatial values and expectations;
- repeated use, site fidelity, avoidance, patrol, marking, and defense produce spatial regularities;
- the observer infers home ranges, core areas, boundaries, and defended regions.

This is closer to the biological distinction between a **home range** (an area used) and a **territory** (an area actively defended) and permits graded systems with defended cores and overlapping peripheral zones. Hard polygons with owners should be reserved as a positive control showing how much behavior is being short-circuited.

### 1.3 Organized conflict requires a stack, not an attack button

The research strongly rejects the shortcut “scarcity + kin bias + damage ⇒ war.” Those ingredients may generate crowding, avoidance, theft, predation, dyadic fights, or population collapse. Organized inter-group conflict typically also depends on several of the following:

- persistent interaction communities or repeated spatial associations;
- recognition through familiarity, phenotype, location, signals, or shared history;
- target discrimination and sustained attention;
- coalition recruitment or action-following;
- a way to estimate or exploit numerical advantage;
- sufficiently low expected participant risk, or sufficiently high direct/indirect benefits;
- concentrated, defensible, renewable, or capturable value;
- spatial memory and recurrent encounters;
- mechanisms reducing participation free-riding, such as mutualistic group augmentation, direct loot, strong social ties, partner choice, punishment, or reputation;
- benefits from occupying, capturing, or denying resources rather than merely destroying competitors;
- enough demographic resilience that conflict does not immediately extinguish the population.

Chimpanzee studies associate lethal coalitionary attacks with strong numerical asymmetry and often inter-community targets, while other primates show that encounter location, residence, social structure, or resource value can outweigh simple head counts. Human archaeological and ethnographic evidence is heterogeneous: organized violence existed among some foragers and early farmers, but other samples show low warfare or predominantly interpersonal homicide. The Genesis experiments must therefore treat the origins and prevalence of war as disputed and condition-dependent, not inevitable.

### 1.4 Required analytical separation

The event pipeline must classify, in ascending evidentiary order:

1. **Individual aggression:** one organism intentionally directs a harmful action at another.
2. **Group-correlated aggression:** offline-inferred social communities predict who attacks whom, but attackers may still be independent responders to the same stimulus.
3. **Coordinated coalitionary behavior:** two or more organisms temporally couple complementary or convergent actions against a target, with coordination above an opportunity-matched independent-action null.
4. **Repeated organized inter-group conflict:** persistent inferred communities repeatedly conduct coordinated hostile episodes against one another, with cross-episode memory, participant reuse, territorial or resource consequences, and recurrence beyond matched null expectations.

A count of simultaneous attackers is not enough. Genuine organization requires evidence that timing, target choice, trajectories, signaling, role allocation, or response to partners changes the outcome relative to independent behavior.

### 1.5 Recommended experimental policy

Do not activate all social mechanisms at once. Use a dependency-ordered program and admit a stage only after its causal criteria survive ablations and matched null models. Use paired seeds across treatments, independent named RNG streams, pre-registered metrics, and ensembles large enough for rare-event statistics. Early aggregation experiments can begin with tens of seeds per cell; coalitionary and cultural-conflict stages will usually need hundreds, plus event-exposure stopping rules and pilot-based power analysis.

The most important factorial distinction is **resource scarcity versus resource concentration**. Scarcity is the ratio of demand to supply. Concentration is the spatial clustering and monopolizability of supply. A world can be scarce but diffuse, abundant but concentrated, or both. Concentrated renewable patches and persistent structures often create repeated co-presence and defendable value more directly than aggregate scarcity. These variables must be manipulated independently.

### 1.6 Minimal recommended Genesis social stack

| Order | Mechanism | Why it belongs | Why it is not a faction system |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Local embodied sensing and movement | Enables aggregation, following, avoidance, and opportunity structure | No identity or membership field is required |
| 2 | Tunable resource patchiness, renewal, and carrying | Creates repeated encounters, transfer, and defendable value | Resources have locations and properties, not owners |
| 3 | Heritable perceptible phenotype cues | Makes recognition evolvable and fallible | Cues do not state kinship or group membership |
| 4 | Familiarity and bounded associative memory | Supports partner choice, reciprocity, retaliation, and neighbor recognition | Stores experience with entities/cues, not faction labels |
| 5 | Drop/transfer/joint-action affordances | Makes cooperation measurable through real costs and benefits | No “cooperate” action; only physical actions |
| 6 | Shared structures, caches, and persistent modifications | Enables stigmergy and environmental inheritance | Anyone can perceive and interact with them unless physically excluded |
| 7 | Costly local signals | Enables alarms, recruitment, conventions, and deception | Channels have no authored meaning |
| 8 | Decaying markers | Enables low-cost space advertisement and stigmergy | Marker content is a perceptible vector, not an owner token |
| 9 | Spatial memory | Enables site fidelity, route learning, patrol, and learned ownership expectations | Territory remains in the organism's model and observer analysis |
| 10 | Damage, injury, recovery, and death | Makes aggression risky and consequential | No war state, kill reward, or enemy class |
| 11 | Observational learning | Enables behavioral transmission and traditions | Copies observed action tendencies, not observer labels |

### 1.7 Central falsifiable prediction

If groups, territories, coalitions, and traditions are genuine emergent products rather than classifier artifacts, then:

- they should be recoverable by more than one reasonable offline method;
- their measured effects should survive controls for shared location, resource exposure, kinship, and opportunity;
- targeted in-simulation ablations of memory, cues, signals, structures, or spatial value should selectively remove the predicted behavior;
- shuffled, time-shifted, and independent-hazard nulls should not reproduce the same coordination scores;
- observer labels must be removable with no change to a replay hash or organism action sequence.

## 2. Definitions and conceptual distinctions

### 2.1 Aggregation, association, group, and community

**Aggregation** is spatial co-presence caused by any mechanism, including independent attraction to the same resource. It does not imply affiliation.

**Association** is repeated proximity or interaction between particular organisms beyond what their movement opportunities predict.

**Affiliative group** is an offline-inferred, temporally persistent set of organisms with elevated within-set association or positive interaction relative to opportunity-matched nulls. Membership may be graded, overlapping, and time-varying.

**Interaction community** is a network-analytic label for a cluster of organisms that interact more frequently or strongly with one another than with others in a defined time window. It is an observer construct, not necessarily a psychologically represented unit.

**Coalition** is an episode-specific set of two or more organisms whose actions are coupled toward a common social target or outcome. A coalition can form without a stable group, and a stable group can exist without coalitions.

**Population** is the demographic set defined by the simulation or analysis boundary. It should not be casually equated with a social group.

### 2.2 Kinship, recognition, assortment, and green beards

**Pedigree relatedness** is genealogical relation derived from ancestry. It can be computed exactly or approximately offline, but organisms should not read it directly.

**Genetic similarity** is similarity of inherited sequence or traits. It is not the same as pedigree relatedness in a recombining, mutating population.

**Kin recognition** is behavior that uses perceptible cues or learned associations to condition action in a way correlated with kinship. Mechanisms include familiarity, phenotype matching, spatial association, and learned family cues.

**Assortment** means that interactants are non-randomly matched with respect to a trait or genotype. Kin-selected effects can arise from limited dispersal or shared natal sites even with no recognition.

**Green-beard mechanism** links a perceptible trait, recognition of that trait, and preferential behavior toward bearers. It can create cooperation cheaply, but is vulnerable to recombination, mimicry, false beards, and suppression unless genetic architecture tightly links the components.

### 2.3 Cooperation, mutualism, altruism, and coordination

**Cooperation** is an interaction that produces a benefit to another participant or a joint outcome, generally with a measurable opportunity cost, risk, or resource expenditure by the actor. The term should not be assigned from visual appearance alone.

**Mutualism** gives immediate net benefits to all participants. It may be stable without kinship or memory.

**By-product benefit** occurs when one organism's self-serving action incidentally helps another.

**Altruism**, in the evolutionary sense, imposes a direct fitness cost on the actor and a fitness benefit on another. Measuring it requires a counterfactual or experimentally estimated cost and benefit; a transfer is not automatically altruistic if it buys protection, mating access, future repayment, or reduced harassment.

**Coordination** is statistical and causal coupling among actions. Organisms independently moving toward the same food are correlated, but not necessarily coordinating. Coordination evidence strengthens when an organism changes timing, direction, or target conditional on another's behavior.

**Division of labor** is persistent or context-sensitive differentiation of roles that improves joint performance. It may emerge from morphology, thresholds, experience, spatial position, or reinforcement without a centralized role assignment.

### 2.4 Dominance and leadership

**Dominance** is a repeated asymmetry in contest outcomes or priority access. It should be inferred from dyadic outcomes, not represented by an authored rank number unless used only as an experimental control.

**Leadership** is disproportionate influence on collective direction or action. It can be transient, distributed, and context-specific. Couzin et al. (2005) showed that informed minorities can guide collective movement without explicit leader signaling or follower knowledge of leader identity. Genesis should therefore log influence and information flow rather than designate leaders.

### 2.5 Home range, territory, boundary, and ownership convention

**Home range** is the area an organism or inferred group repeatedly uses.

**Core area** is a high-use subset of a home range.

**Territory** is a used area in which occupancy or access is actively defended, advertised, or made costly to intruders. Mere exclusivity is insufficient if it arises from habitat partitioning or avoidance.

**Boundary** is a spatial region where interaction, movement, marking, or contest behavior changes. It may be diffuse, moving, seasonal, and asymmetric.

**Ownership convention** is a learned or evolved rule that resolves contests based on residence or prior possession. A “resident wins” pattern can reduce injury without any legal or political ownership state.

### 2.6 Aggression and conflict ladder

The engine and observer should maintain the following distinctions:

| Level | Definition | Minimum evidence | Common false positive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accidental collision | Contact caused by movement geometry | No target-directed harmful control | Congestion near a resource |
| Resource competition | One organism reduces another's access through depletion or occupation | Resource-mediated negative effect | Treating every co-use event as hostility |
| Individual aggression | Target-directed harmful action | Target selection and harmful act | Predation or defensive reflex mislabeled as social aggression |
| Retaliation | Increased harmful response after prior harm by the same actor | Dyadic temporal dependency | Both organisms responding to the same contested patch |
| Dyadic fighting | Reciprocal harmful actions in one episode | Back-and-forth targeting | Two independent strikes at a resource |
| Group-correlated aggression | Inferred community predicts attack direction | Between-community excess after opportunity controls | Spatial segregation alone |
| Coalitionary aggression | Two or more actors coordinate against a social target | Timing/trajectory/target coupling above independent null | Simultaneous independent attack on a weak target |
| Raid | Coordinated incursion into another community's repeatedly used area | Directed travel, coalition, target area, hostile or capture actions | Routine migration through overlap zone |
| Coordinated defense | Multiple actors respond to intrusion or attack in coupled fashion | Recruitment or conditional convergence | All fleeing from the same danger |
| Repeated organized inter-group conflict | Persistent communities repeatedly conduct coordinated hostile episodes with cross-episode consequences | Recurrence, group persistence, coordination, opponent specificity, consequences | Recurrent crowding at a resource hotspot |

### 2.7 Culture, convention, and tradition

**Social learning** is learning influenced by observing or interacting with another organism or its products.

**Convention** is one of multiple behaviorally equivalent or near-equivalent variants that becomes locally predominant because organisms respond to or copy others.

**Tradition** is a socially maintained behavior variant that persists across time and at least partial demographic turnover.

**Culture**, for observer purposes, is a set of group-correlated, socially transmitted behavioral variants not adequately explained by genes, individual learning, or ecology alone. This is an evidential classification, not a simulation state. Claims should be graded because animal-culture research debates how much ecological and genetic exclusion is required.

### 2.8 Species, group, culture, and era labels

These labels may be useful in visualization and offline science, but they must remain outside the organism-facing state. The strict rule is:

> Removing every observer label and every clustering result must leave the simulation's next-state transition, action choices, replay hash, and checkpoint bytes unchanged except for optional observer-only metadata.

## 3. Group-formation mechanisms

### 3.1 Local spatial rules

The lowest-complexity path to grouping is a controller that balances:

- short-range repulsion to avoid overlap;
- intermediate-range alignment or following;
- longer-range attraction to visible conspecifics;
- environmental gradients such as food, shelter, temperature, or hazard;
- locomotion cost and collision constraints.

Couzin et al. (2002) showed that these local interaction zones can generate swarms, toroidal milling, and polarized groups, including hysteresis-like collective memory. Buhl et al. (2006) experimentally found a density-dependent transition to ordered marching in locusts. These systems demonstrate group-level order, but not necessarily affiliation, cooperation, or identity. Genesis should use them as the **Stage 1 aggregation baseline**, not as proof of social organization.

Minimal implementation is a bounded neighbor query plus controller inputs for relative bearing, distance, velocity, and perhaps body orientation. It is unnecessary to supply neighbor identity. To prevent traversal-order artifacts, neighbor lists must be sorted by stable entity ID or spatial key before aggregation, and vector sums must use a deterministic reduction order.

### 3.2 Ecological co-location

Groups can arise because independent organisms repeatedly select the same place. Important attractors include:

- concentrated renewable resource patches;
- shelters or safe resting locations;
- nesting or reproductive sites;
- water, temperature, or terrain bottlenecks;
- predator-free refuges;
- persistent structures, caches, and modified environments;
- migration corridors and seasonal resources.

This mechanism is computationally cheap and ecologically plausible, but it is a major interpretive confound. A cluster around one food patch is an aggregation until the analysis shows partner-specific association, coordinated movement away from the patch, or persistence after the ecological attractor changes.

### 3.3 Familiarity and social attraction

Repeated exposure can produce partner-specific attraction without kin detection. A minimal implementation stores a bounded familiarity score updated from co-presence, affiliative contact, tolerated proximity, or successful joint outcomes. Familiarity can support stable groups, neighbor recognition, reduced aggression, and later direct reciprocity.

Familiarity is evolvable because the memory update rate, decay, attraction response, and generalization across cues can be inherited. It is also fallible: migrants can become familiar, offspring can be separated, and deceptive individuals can exploit trust. These failures are useful rather than defects because they prevent a perfect faction channel.

### 3.4 Kin-biased grouping without direct kin access

Kin-biased groups can form through:

- limited dispersal from natal locations;
- parent-offspring following;
- early-life imprinting on caregiver or nest cues;
- phenotype matching against self or familiar templates;
- shared heritable signals;
- shared structures and inherited home ranges.

Hamilton's theory concerns assortment and fitness effects; it does not require a magic kin sensor. Genesis should log pedigree and genetic distance for analysis but expose only phenotype and experience. A direct `relatedness(actor, target)` controller input would make kin selection trivial and obscure the evolution of recognition.

### 3.5 Assortative interaction and partner choice

Assortment can arise from:

- homophilic attraction to similar phenotypes or behavior;
- avoidance of prior defectors or aggressors;
- selective joining or leaving;
- spatial sorting caused by locomotion or habitat preferences;
- repeated use of the same structure or resource;
- signal-response matching;
- different activity times or migration routes.

Partner choice is a powerful stabilizer because cheaters can be excluded by lost opportunities rather than punished. It can operate with a simple learned value per cue class or individual. However, a model in which agents directly compare an arbitrary tag and automatically cooperate can manufacture “ethnocentrism” by design. Tag-based ABMs are valuable positive controls and theoretical demonstrations, but Genesis should let cue use, tolerance, and response coevolve independently.

### 3.6 Mutualism, predator defense, and cooperative foraging

Groups may be directly beneficial through:

- dilution of predation risk;
- collective vigilance;
- alarm information;
- coordinated capture or processing of resources;
- defense of offspring or structures;
- thermoregulation or shelter sharing;
- improved navigation through distributed sensing.

These mechanisms can stabilize grouping with little memory. To avoid scripting cooperation, Genesis should define physical tasks with nonlinear returns—for example, an object that moves faster when forces align, a resource that is safer to process when another watches, or a structure that reduces local hazard for any occupant. The controller should receive no `team_task` flag.

### 3.7 Reproductive opportunities and cooperative breeding

Reproduction can drive grouping through mate access, offspring care, protection, and helpers-at-the-nest dynamics. These behaviors require careful interpretation because helping may be kin-selected, mutualistic, coercive, mating effort, or a route to future reproductive opportunity. A minimal system needs offspring that impose real care costs and can benefit from feeding, guarding, transport, or environmental modification. It does not need authored family roles.

### 3.8 Shared structures and ecological inheritance

Persistent nests, shelters, caches, paths, barriers, and work sites can anchor groups across individual turnover. They create:

- repeated encounter locations;
- public or club goods;
- opportunities for defense and maintenance;
- spatial memory targets;
- stigmergic coordination;
- environmental inheritance.

A structure must not contain a privileged group-owner field. Access can still become exclusive through physical occupation, locks or barriers organisms construct from general physics, local defense, familiarity, or learned conventions.

### 3.9 Reciprocity, reputation, dominance, and leadership

These mechanisms can increase group stability but should be introduced in order of cognitive cost:

1. **Outcome reinforcement:** repeat interactions that produced personal benefit.
2. **Partner-specific familiarity/value:** remember who helped, harmed, or tolerated proximity.
3. **Direct reciprocity:** condition help on partner's prior behavior.
4. **Observed reputation:** condition behavior on witnessed third-party actions.
5. **Transmitted reputation:** use signals from others about a third party.

Dominance can reduce repeated contest costs by creating stable asymmetries, but may also generate reproductive skew, coercion, and coalition incentives. Leadership should be measured as influence on movement or action timing, not assigned. A small informed minority can create leadership-like effects without signaling or rank (Couzin et al., 2005).

### 3.10 Environmental constraints and fission-fusion

Group size and persistence depend on encounter topology. Narrow corridors, isolated patches, sleeping sites, seasonal routes, and carrying capacity can create fusion. Resource depletion, crowding, conflict, reproductive competition, and patch splitting can create fission. Genesis should allow group boundaries to be porous and time-varying rather than forcing permanent membership.

A useful world must support:

- temporary aggregations;
- overlapping affiliations;
- nested scales, such as dyads within bands;
- migrants and peripheral individuals;
- fusion and fission;
- extinction and replacement of social clusters.

### 3.11 Is an explicit group identifier needed?

No, except as an experimental positive control or debugging treatment.

| Design | Advantages | Scientific cost | Recommended use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global explicit group ID visible to controllers | O(1) recognition; easy visualization; stable coordination | Directly enables factions, perfect in/out-group behavior, and hidden objectives; blocks recognition evolution | Positive control only |
| Local badge assigned at birth | Cheap, partially observable | Still creates authored categories and inheritance channel | Green-beard control, not default |
| Heritable continuous cue vector | Evolvable, noisy, recombining, deceptive | Can still become a tag; requires cue costs and independent response genes | Recommended |
| Familiarity and individual identity memory | Supports flexible relationships and migrants | Memory cost; may create cliques | Recommended after spatial grouping |
| Interaction-network inference | No behavioral shortcut; captures dynamic groups | Offline computational cost and classifier uncertainty | Primary observer method |
| Shared location/structure inference | Cheap and interpretable | Confounds ecology with affiliation | Supporting observer feature only |

The strongest design is to keep immutable entity IDs for engine bookkeeping while preventing controllers from reading those IDs directly. An organism may learn a perceptual fingerprint from morphology, signal profile, or repeated encounter, but recognition must be noisy, capacity-limited, and produced through perception.

## 4. Recognition and relatedness

### 4.1 Recognition mechanism comparison

| Mechanism | Evolvability | Deception/mismatch risk | Memory need | Runtime cost | Deterministic implementation | Risk of faction shortcut |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Direct pedigree relatedness input | None needed; perfect by fiat | None unless noise authored | None | O(1) or kinship query | Easy | **Extreme**; do not use |
| Natal spatial association | High through dispersal and following traits | Adoption, brood mixing, migration | Low | Low | Stable co-location counters | Low |
| Familiarity | High through update/decay parameters | Familiar cheaters; strangers who are kin | Moderate | O(memory slots) | Fixed-capacity deterministic map | Low |
| Phenotypic matching | High if cues and templates evolve | Mimicry, environmental effects, recombination | Low–moderate | Cue-distance calculation | Fixed-point vector distance | Moderate |
| Shared heritable signal | High | False signaling and linkage breakdown | Low | Signal sensing | Deterministic channel model | Moderate–high |
| Location/home-range cue | High through site fidelity and learning | Visitors, migrants, boundary ambiguity | Spatial memory | Map-dependent | Quantized spatial memory | Low |
| Behavioral history | High | Strategic deception, sparse observations | Moderate–high | Event updates | Ordered event processing | Low |
| Shared artifact use | High through repeated co-use | Theft, open access, abandoned artifacts | Low–moderate | Object-interaction memory | Stable object IDs observer-side; perceptual signatures organism-side | Low |
| Learned individual identity | High | Recognition errors, disguises, memory eviction | High | O(k) sparse records | Deterministic fingerprint matching and eviction | Low |
| Group-specific convention | Cultural rather than purely genetic | Mimicry, convergence, migration | Learning and observation | Moderate | Deterministic learning updates | Moderate if conventions become badges |
| Green-beard-like linked trait | High under tight linkage | False beards, recombination, suppression | None–low | Cue test | Straightforward | High if one-bit exact match |

### 4.2 Genetic relatedness should be observer-visible, not organism-visible

The engine should preserve complete lineage and genome records for analysis. Offline, it can compute:

- pedigree coefficients over bounded depth;
- realized genome similarity;
- identity by descent where the genome design supports it;
- maternal, paternal, sibling, and cohort relations;
- local relatedness within inferred groups.

Organisms should receive none of these values directly. The causal path should be:

`genotype → perceptible phenotype / early association / signal → perception and learning → behavior`.

This permits false negatives, false positives, mimicry, drift, migration, adoption-like associations, and cue evolution. Those are central scientific phenomena, not implementation noise.

### 4.3 Familiarity

A minimal familiarity record can contain:

- perceptual fingerprint prototype;
- exposure count or exponentially decayed familiarity;
- last encounter tick;
- learned valence from received benefits/harm;
- confidence or recognition variance;
- optionally context-specific values for feeding, resting, mating, and conflict.

Use a fixed-capacity memory table with deterministic replacement. A recommended eviction ordering is lowest absolute learned value, then oldest last-use tick, then stable slot index. Do not use hash-map iteration order. Serialize every slot, decay accumulator, and fingerprint prototype.

Familiarity supports stable groups without making ancestry the only basis of social structure. It also permits a “dear enemy” pattern in which known neighbors receive less aggression than unfamiliar intruders, provided repeated peaceful boundaries make this adaptive.

### 4.4 Phenotypic matching

Phenotypic matching compares an observed cue vector against a template derived from self, parents, nestmates, or early companions. Candidate cue dimensions include:

- body proportions and coloration;
- odor-like scalar/vector emissions;
- movement rhythm;
- call spectrum or pulse pattern;
- surface pattern on carried or constructed artifacts;
- developmental timing.

Cue genes and response genes should be separable and recombining. If one locus directly encodes both a badge and unconditional aid to matching badges, the system becomes a green-beard model. That is scientifically interesting as a treatment, but not a neutral default.

### 4.5 Shared signals and conventions

A signal can support recognition if agents learn a mapping between signal patterns and expected partners or behavior. To avoid direct factions:

- signals should be local, noisy, and costly;
- channel values should be continuous or multi-dimensional rather than a single exact group integer;
- signal production and response should be independently mutable;
- mimicry and withholding must be possible;
- semantics must arise from context and learning;
- newly learned conventions must be able to cross genetic lineages.

Group-specific signal conventions become plausible when repeated partners coordinate, learners observe local responses, and migration is limited enough for local equilibria to persist. They are cultural only if genetic and ecological controls cannot explain the pattern.

### 4.6 Location and shared artifacts

Location can act as a recognition cue through learned expectations: “individuals encountered near this nest have often been safe” or “this marker pattern near this cache predicts attack.” Shared structures can likewise create association through repeated co-use.

Neither mechanism should identify an owner. The controller perceives spatial features, recent occupants, marker vectors, and interactions. The observer may later infer that a set of individuals predominantly builds, maintains, or defends a structure.

### 4.7 Behavioral history, reputation, and identity

Behavioral history can be represented at three levels:

1. **Cue-class value:** cheap generalization, no individual identity.
2. **Individual episodic summary:** partner-specific help/harm/tolerance history.
3. **Third-party reputation:** observed or signaled information about others.

Level 1 can produce broad biases but is vulnerable to stereotyping and deception. Level 2 supports direct reciprocity, partner choice, and retaliation. Level 3 is expensive and should be delayed because it requires reliable identity, observation, memory, and assessment rules. Indirect reciprocity models are sensitive to information quality and processing costs; a reputation system should not be added as a free global ledger.

### 4.8 Green-beard-like traits

Green-beard systems are useful because they demonstrate that identity-linked cooperation can evolve without genealogical inference. Empirical examples and models also show their fragility and complexity. False beards can display the cue without cooperating; recombination can separate cue and behavior; suppressors can exploit recognition; multiple tags can sometimes stabilize conditional altruism (Keller & Ross, 1998; Jansen & van Baalen, 2006; Gruenheit et al., 2017).

Genesis should include green-beard treatments only through ordinary genome architecture:

- a heritable emitted cue;
- a receptor or learned matcher;
- a response tendency;
- linkage/recombination parameters;
- costs of cue production and response.

No engine-level green-beard primitive is needed.

### 4.9 Recommended recognition sequence

1. Begin with no identity memory: only body/motion perception.
2. Add natal association and familiarity.
3. Add heritable perceptible cues and phenotype-template learning.
4. Add bounded individual fingerprints.
5. Add local signals with evolvable production and response.
6. Add third-party observation and socially learned conventions.
7. Add reputation transmission only if simpler mechanisms have been exhausted.

This sequence permits causal attribution and prevents a sophisticated social-memory system from masking simpler ecological or kin-assortment effects.

## 5. Cooperation and cheating

### 5.1 Mechanisms are alternatives and combinations, not stages of one theory

The major cooperation frameworks describe different causal structures and sometimes different mathematical decompositions of the same population process. Genesis should implement the underlying ingredients—assortment, costs, benefits, dispersal, partner switching, memory, signaling, reproduction, and competition—rather than a “kin selection,” “reciprocity,” or “group selection” switch.

| Mechanism | Core condition | Minimum cognition | Key vulnerability | Genesis implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| By-product benefit | Helping another is incidental to self-benefit | None | Benefit disappears if ecology changes | Start here to test whether apparent cooperation is merely correlated self-interest |
| Mutualism | Joint action immediately benefits each participant | Local action-response | Coordination failure and unequal division | Use physical tasks with nonlinear joint returns |
| Kin selection | Benefits are preferentially delivered to genetically correlated recipients, directly or through assortment | None if relatives cluster; low if cue matching | Local kin competition, migration, recognition error | Keep pedigree offline; manipulate dispersal and perceptible cues |
| Direct reciprocity | Repeated encounters, sufficiently large future value, contingent response | Partner recognition and memory | Noise, mistaken retaliation, bootstrapping | Add bounded social memory only after simpler cooperation tests |
| Indirect reciprocity | Reputation affects future treatment | Identity, observation, assessment, information | Cost/error of information, norm disagreement, deception | Do not use a global reputation score |
| Partner choice | Cooperators can select, retain, or leave partners | Learned partner value or cue-class value | Search costs, market thinness, deceptive entrants | Provide exit and alternative partners before punishment |
| Mutualism plus partner choice | Joint gains vary by partner quality | Low–moderate | Monopolization and coercion | Strong candidate for early stable cooperation |
| Punishment | Defectors suffer a cost imposed by others | Detection and harmful response | Punisher cost, retaliation, second-order free riding | Introduce only after measurable public goods and cheating exist |
| Policing | Third parties suppress conflict or cheating | Observation and intervention | Bias, capture by dominants, policing cost | Treat as an evolved action, not a role |
| Public goods | Contributions create non-excludable or partially excludable benefit | Contribution control; possibly memory | Free riding and overuse | Implement through environmental state changes, not a “public goods game” button |
| Division of labor | Different actions or phenotypes are complementary | Response thresholds may suffice | Credit assignment, role lock-in | Permit heterogeneity and measure role specialization, do not assign classes |
| Cooperative breeding | Helpers increase survival of offspring not solely their own | Offspring recognition or local association | Reproductive conflict, coercion, misdirected care | Requires real offspring needs and care actions |
| Stigmergic cooperation | One action changes the environment and guides later actions | Local perception of traces/artifacts | Misleading traces, vandalism, saturation | High-value low-cognition mechanism for Genesis |
| Multilevel selection | Between-cluster covariance offsets within-cluster selection against cooperation | No special cognition | Migration, cluster fusion, local competition | Analyze selection at multiple levels; do not create group reproduction by fiat |

### 5.2 Kin selection and assortment

Hamilton's rule, in its simplest form, asks whether a recipient-weighted benefit exceeds the actor cost. In embodied populations, the difficult part is not writing `rB > C`; it is producing and measuring the assortment and fitness effects that make the terms meaningful. Limited dispersal can cluster relatives, but if relatives also compete for the same local reproductive slots, local competition can cancel part of the benefit. Genesis experiments must therefore vary:

- natal dispersal distance;
- resource and breeding-site saturation;
- scale of competition for replacement slots;
- whether benefits affect survival, fecundity, offspring development, or group resource capture;
- recognition accuracy and cue linkage.

A strong kin-bias result requires more than relatives standing near one another. Costly help should be disproportionately directed toward kin after controlling for encounter opportunity, familiarity, location, age, and recipient need. Cross-fostering or cue-scrambling treatments should separate pedigree from learned association.

### 5.3 Direct reciprocity

Direct reciprocity requires:

- repeated encounters with identifiable partners;
- a benefit-to-cost and continuation structure that makes future returns valuable;
- memory of relevant conduct;
- strategies robust to implementation and perception error;
- a path by which conditional response can evolve from simpler behavior.

The last requirement is often neglected. Evolutionary robotics simulations found that reciprocity could fail to evolve because a useful reciprocating controller requires several coordinated functions—help, recognize, remember, and condition response—to arise together. This supports a staged Genesis design in which partner choice, familiarity, or kin-biased helping can scaffold later reciprocity rather than expecting tit-for-tat to appear from arbitrary neural mutations.

Recommended minimal memory is not a full event history. A partner record can store decayed estimates of:

- benefit received;
- harm received;
- failed or refused opportunities;
- uncertainty and exposure count;
- last encounter tick.

Reciprocity should be measured with lagged conditional effects and opportunity controls, not by symmetric lifetime totals alone.

Morozov and Feigel (2026) provide a recent theoretical result that broadens this design space: high cooperation can evolve in Prisoner’s Dilemma when willingness to cooperate is opponent-specific, without imposing explicit reciprocal arrangements, genetic assortment, or spatial structure. This is not a no-recognition result. Their mechanism requires consistent discrimination among opponents across repeated encounters, based on appearance or behavior. For Genesis, it strengthens the case for bounded perceptual identity and partner-conditioned policy as a potentially simpler precursor to explicit bookkeeping of favors. Its robustness must still be tested in an embodied ecology with recognition error, mutation, migration, variable encounter opportunity, and real resource costs.

### 5.4 Indirect reciprocity and reputation

Indirect reciprocity raises the cognitive and computational requirements substantially. An organism must identify an actor and recipient, observe what happened, assess whether the action was appropriate in context, retain or communicate the assessment, and use it in later partner choice or aid. Assessment rules can disagree, signals can be manipulated, and information processing itself can be costly. Models show that small information costs or errors can materially change outcomes.

Genesis should not expose a global “reputation” scalar. A plausible sequence is:

1. observe another's action;
2. update a local actor-context value;
3. optionally emit a signal based on that observation;
4. allow recipients to learn whether the informant is reliable;
5. let assessment conventions evolve or spread.

This makes reputation a distributed, inconsistent, and potentially deceptive phenomenon rather than a scoreboard.

### 5.5 Partner choice and biological markets

Partner choice can stabilize cooperation without punishment if organisms can leave bad partners and find alternatives. Biological-market reasoning predicts that cooperation changes with partner availability, demand, outside options, and the value of exchanged services. Paper-wasp experiments, cleaner-fish systems, and theory support the importance of partner choice and audience effects.

Minimal Genesis requirements:

- heterogeneous partner quality;
- optional interaction rather than forced pairing;
- search and travel costs;
- ability to decline, leave, or switch;
- delayed consequences of partner retention;
- no guarantee that the best partner is available.

A key falsifiable prediction is that increasing outside options should make exploitative behavior less successful and increase assortative matching among reliable cooperators. If cooperation remains unchanged when partner switching is disabled, partner choice was not the causal stabilizer.

### 5.6 Punishment, policing, and retaliation

Punishment should be distinguished from retaliation and defense:

- **Retaliation** responds to harm against the actor.
- **Third-party punishment** responds to harm or defection involving others.
- **Policing** suppresses conflict or cheating that threatens a shared system.
- **Defense** prevents or stops immediate harm or intrusion.

A damage action makes punishment possible but does not identify its function. Classification must use context and counterfactual payoff effects. Punishment creates costs for the punisher, retaliation risk, and second-order free riding by individuals who benefit without punishing. It can also become coercion: dominants may punish subordinates for withholding benefits rather than enforce a cooperative norm.

Recommended implementation is no special punishment action. The same harm, blocking, exclusion, signaling, or refusal actions should be available in all contexts. Offline analysis infers whether their contingent use suppresses cheating.

### 5.7 Public goods and resource sharing

A public good should exist as an environmental consequence of ordinary actions. Examples include:

- adding material to a shelter that reduces local exposure;
- clearing a path that lowers locomotion cost;
- maintaining a water or food-processing site;
- producing a signal or marker that reveals danger;
- guarding while others forage;
- carrying resources to a cache accessible to others.

Resource sharing requires a genuine transfer or tolerated access. Distinguish:

- intentional handoff;
- dropping in another's reachable area;
- abandonment;
- tolerated theft;
- spillage or accidental availability;
- reciprocal exchange;
- provisioning offspring or mates;
- coercive taking.

Garbus and Pollack (2024) found emergent resource exchange and tolerated-theft-like behavior in a multi-agent reinforcement-learning environment when a shared campfire created a period of congregation and interaction. The design implication is not to reproduce that scenario verbatim, but to recognize that **interaction opportunities and downtime** can be as important as an explicit transfer action.

### 5.8 Division of labor

Division of labor can emerge from simple local differences:

- morphology or sensor variation;
- different response thresholds;
- age, energy, injury, or experience;
- spatial position relative to a task;
- reinforcement from success;
- frequency-dependent returns;
- task switching costs.

A controller need not represent roles symbolically. Genesis should measure role differentiation using action distributions, task-state occupancy, transition rates, and contribution counterfactuals. A persistent split is not beneficial division of labor unless joint output exceeds matched homogeneous or randomized-role baselines.

Evolutionary-robotics studies show that genetic team composition and the level at which performance is selected can strongly affect cooperation and credit assignment. Genesis should avoid assigning team fitness; individual survival and reproduction should remain primary. Group-level effects may still emerge when organisms share environmental consequences.

### 5.9 Stigmergic cooperation

Stigmergy coordinates behavior through environmental traces rather than direct messages. Examples include:

- paths produced by repeated travel;
- decaying chemical-like markers;
- partially completed structures;
- moved objects that expose the next useful action;
- caches and work piles;
- worn terrain or cleared vegetation.

It is attractive for Genesis because it is local, embodied, persistent, and compatible with low cognition. It also supports delayed cooperation across non-overlapping lifetimes. The main design risk is creating marker channels with authored semantics or unlimited precision. Markers should have production costs, decay, diffusion or physical persistence, interference, and saturation.

### 5.10 Cheater resistance under population change

Cooperation should be tested against:

- **mutation:** cooperators produce lower-contribution variants;
- **migration:** unfamiliar strategies enter established clusters;
- **cheating:** individuals accept benefits while withholding costs;
- **population turnover:** reliable partners die and are replaced;
- **resource scarcity:** benefit/cost ratios change;
- **environmental change:** prior cooperative tasks lose value;
- **group fusion:** previously separate conventions meet;
- **group fission:** small daughter clusters lose diversity or enforcement capacity.

Potential stabilizers and their predicted signatures:

| Stabilizer | Signature under cheater invasion | Diagnostic ablation |
|---|---|---|
| Kin assortment | Cheaters spread mainly when relatedness falls | Increase dispersal or scramble pedigree-cue correlation |
| Partner choice | Cheaters lose interactions before being physically punished | Disable refusal/switching |
| Direct reciprocity | Aid to a cheater falls after observed defection | Reset dyadic memory |
| Reputation | Third parties avoid observed cheaters | Occlude third-party observation or corrupt identity |
| Punishment | Cheaters incur direct costs after defection | Remove harm/exclusion response while retaining memory |
| Spatial clustering | Cooperative clusters persist at boundaries | Randomize locations or increase mixing |
| Pleiotropy/task linkage | Cheaters lose another essential function | Decouple the linked traits genetically |
| Structure access | Maintainers receive preferential access | Make structure benefits fully non-excludable |
| Mutualistic interdependence | Withholding lowers the cheater's own immediate payoff | Remove complementary task dependency |

### 5.11 Migration, fission, and fusion

Migration can weaken kin assortment but strengthen cultural diffusion and partner markets. Its effect depends on whether migrants can learn local cues, whether residents can exclude them, and whether rare innovations are valuable. Fusion can destabilize conventions through coordination mismatch or create larger coalitions. Fission can preserve between-group variation but may make small groups vulnerable to demographic stochasticity.

Genesis should log migrant status only for observers. Organisms can perceive unfamiliarity, cue mismatch, or place history, not a migrant bit.

### 5.12 Multilevel-selection and inclusive-fitness debate

The dispute is partly conceptual and partly about generality. Inclusive-fitness approaches emphasize effects of an action on copies of genes in social partners; multilevel approaches partition selection within and between groups. In many models, Price-equation decompositions can describe the same evolutionary change at different levels. The 2010 *Nature* eusociality paper by Nowak, Tarnita, and Wilson challenged the necessity and generality of inclusive fitness; a large group of evolutionary biologists responded that the critique overstated its case. The debate remains technically important.

For Genesis, the practical response is neutral:

- record individual reproduction and survival;
- infer dynamic clusters offline;
- decompose trait change into within- and between-cluster covariance;
- separately estimate pedigree assortment and social effects;
- report both decompositions where valid;
- never add a “group selection force.”

The mechanism is the ecology and demographic structure. The theoretical vocabulary is an analytical lens.

## 6. Communication and coordination

### 6.1 Start with cues, not signals

A **cue** provides information without having evolved or been produced for that purpose. Movement direction, attack trajectories, resource carriage, injury, gaze/body orientation, and crowd motion can coordinate behavior without dedicated communication. Mitri et al. (2009) showed in evolved robots that inadvertent light cues near food could become useful social information and then be suppressed when competition made disclosure costly. This is directly relevant: information production and concealment should both be possible.

Genesis should therefore expose rich local action observation before adding explicit signal channels. Candidate percepts are:

- relative position, velocity, acceleration, and body orientation;
- current manipulation target;
- attack wind-up, impact, and target;
- held object category and visible properties;
- recent resource acquisition or loss;
- emitted sound/light/chemical-like values once signals are enabled;
- nearby marker fields and structure state.

### 6.2 Coordination from movement observation

Collective direction can emerge when individuals follow neighbors or weight their own preference against local motion. A small informed minority can guide a larger group without an explicit leader role (Couzin et al., 2005). Attack or defense coordination can likewise begin through action-following:

- approach where familiar partners approach;
- flee where others flee;
- orient toward a target others face;
- strike when local attack intensity crosses a threshold;
- follow a departing individual from a resting or gathering site.

These are simple rules, but the observer must test whether apparent synchronization exceeds a model in which all individuals independently sense the same external event.

### 6.3 Local signal channels

A minimal signal system can use two to four bounded analog channels. Each emission has:

- amplitude;
- duration;
- energetic or opportunity cost;
- propagation radius and attenuation;
- optional directionality;
- occlusion or terrain effects;
- production latency and cooldown;
- receiver-specific sensitivity;
- deterministic noise keyed to emitter, receiver, channel, and tick if noise is desired.

No channel is named “alarm,” “recruit,” “attack,” “kin,” or “territory.” Meaning is inferred from statistical context and receiver response.

### 6.4 Alarm, recruitment, and directional signals

An alarm-like convention exists when a signal reliably follows detection of a hazard and changes receivers' protective actions. A recruitment-like convention exists when a signal increases the probability that receivers approach, follow, or join an action. A directional signal requires either directional emission or a learned mapping from signal sequence to bearing/action.

Necessary tests include:

- signal playback or replay with the original stimulus absent;
- signal permutation across contexts;
- emitter-removed controls;
- receiver learning ablation;
- energetic-cost manipulation;
- deceptive-emitter invasion.

A signal-response correlation alone is insufficient because both may be caused by the same observed stimulus.

### 6.5 Shared attention

Shared attention need not imply a human-like theory of mind. Minimal shared attention can be implemented through visible body orientation, gaze proxy, target-bearing cues, or local action salience. An organism may learn that following another's orientation predicts resources or threats.

For coalitionary aggression, shared attention is a likely bridge between simultaneous arousal and target-specific coordination. Log each organism's perceptual target set and attention weights if the controller architecture has them; otherwise reconstruct possible targets from sensor geometry.

### 6.6 Persistent environmental markers

Markers can support:

- route reinforcement;
- resource recruitment;
- hazard avoidance;
- territory advertisement;
- individual or local convention recognition;
- construction sequencing;
- indirect retaliation or deterrence.

Recommended marker properties:

- local deposition action;
- finite material or energy cost;
- bounded vector channels;
- diffusion and/or advection if physically justified;
- deterministic decay;
- overwrite, mixing, masking, and saturation;
- no persistent emitter ID readable by organisms;
- optional perceptible chemical fingerprint generated from heritable phenotype.

The observer can retain the depositor ID in the event log for science, while the organism perceives only the resulting field.

### 6.7 Signal conventions, deception, and information ecology

Signals are subject to conflict of interest. Emitters may benefit from recruiting help, warning kin, bluffing strength, concealing food, luring rivals, or advertising occupancy. Receivers should evolve or learn skepticism when signal reliability falls.

Measure:

- mutual information between signal and context;
- receiver behavior conditional on signal and direct evidence;
- emitter fitness effects;
- receiver fitness effects;
- signal production cost;
- deception rate, defined against the receiver-relevant environmental state;
- equilibrium reliability under different relatedness and competition conditions.

Do not require honesty. Stable partial information or polymorphic signaling can be a legitimate result.

### 6.8 Imitation and observational learning

Social learning can range from low-fidelity stimulus enhancement to action-sequence copying. A staged hierarchy is:

1. **Local enhancement:** presence of others increases attention to a place.
2. **Stimulus enhancement:** observation increases attention to an object type.
3. **Outcome copying:** repeat an action associated with observed success.
4. **Action copying:** imitate a motor/action pattern.
5. **Sequence learning:** copy ordered multi-step behavior.
6. **Conformist or prestige bias:** preferentially copy common or successful variants.

Genesis should begin with outcome copying or attention modulation rather than full action-sequence imitation. Chimpanzee research shows both social diffusion of techniques and limits on what can be acquired, cautioning against an unrestricted “copy behavior” operator.

### 6.9 Communication compute and determinism

Signal propagation can dominate compute if every emitter checks every receiver. Use spatial indexing and cap local percepts. For fields, prefer fixed grids or sparse active cells with deterministic update order. Quantize amplitudes and decay in fixed-point arithmetic where exact cross-platform replay is required.

Never draw signal noise from a traversal-dependent stream. Use a named keyed draw such as:

`rng(signal_noise, tick, emitter_id, receiver_id, channel_id)`.

If simultaneous signals mix, define a stable summation order and saturation rule. Serialize channel state, emission cooldowns, learned signal-response parameters, and active marker cells.

## 7. Territoriality

### 7.1 Resource-defense theory and contest economics

Classic territoriality theory treats defense as economical when the expected value protected exceeds the costs of advertisement, patrol, missed foraging, injury, and exclusion effort. Resource distribution affects whether value can be monopolized. A uniformly diffuse resource may be scarce but not defendable; a small renewable patch may be abundant in aggregate but highly contestable. Brown (1964), Emlen and Oring (1977), and Maher and Lott (2000) emphasize variation in resource distribution, predictability, renewal, and social organization.

Genesis should independently vary:

- total resource supply;
- spatial concentration;
- patch size and separation;
- renewal rate and predictability;
- portability and storability;
- depletion by occupants;
- travel and patrol costs;
- visibility and marker persistence;
- injury cost and numerical asymmetry.

### 7.2 Home ranges and site fidelity

Site fidelity can emerge from learned place value and travel efficiency without defense. A minimal spatial-memory map stores bounded estimates for:

- expected resource yield;
- hazard;
- recent social encounters;
- attack risk;
- shelter/structure value;
- cache state;
- marker patterns;
- uncertainty and last visit.

Use coarse learned tiles, landmarks, or sparse place memories rather than giving organisms global coordinates if that violates embodiment. Exact implementation depends on the perception system, but memory updates and eviction must be deterministic.

### 7.3 Territorial markers

Markers can lower the cost of defense by allowing avoidance before physical contests. They can also bluff occupancy, be counter-marked, decay, or become stale. In many animals, scent and acoustic advertisement convey identity, state, or occupancy. A Genesis marker should be a physical trace with a learned interpretation, not a border declaration.

Marker experiments should vary:

- decay half-life;
- deposition cost;
- cue individuality;
- perceptual range;
- overmarking and erasure;
- false marking by nonresidents;
- delay between deposition and encounter;
- resource movement or patch extinction.

If stable boundaries remain when markers are disabled, direct encounter memory or physical structures may be sufficient. If boundaries vanish, markers were causal.

### 7.4 Patrol behavior

Patrol is costly repeated movement through low-immediate-return space that increases detection, advertisement, or defense of valuable areas. It should be inferred by comparing trajectories with optimal foraging and random movement baselines. Indicators include:

- repeated perimeter-biased routes;
- elevated marker deposition;
- reduced feeding during patrol;
- increased response to intruders;
- partner coupling or recruitment;
- return to core areas after circuit completion.

A circuit near a resource edge may be foraging, not patrol. The analysis needs context and counterfactual path-value estimates.

### 7.5 Boundary formation

Boundaries can arise from:

- repeated resident advantage;
- mutual avoidance after costly encounters;
- marker gradients;
- terrain barriers;
- resource partitioning;
- neighbor familiarity and “dear enemy” tolerance;
- learned ownership conventions;
- group-size asymmetry.

Biological evidence supports graded systems. Western gorillas, for example, show patterns consistent with stronger avoidance near neighboring home-range centers while retaining overlap and tolerance elsewhere (Morrison et al., 2020). Genesis should therefore represent and measure boundary probability surfaces, not only hard polygons.

### 7.6 Site, nest, structure, and cache defense

Persistent structures and caches create defendable value even when natural resources are diffuse. Defense can include:

- blocking access;
- occupying an entrance;
- alarm or recruitment signals;
- marker deposition;
- chasing or attacking;
- moving resources away;
- repairing damage;
- hiding or camouflaging access.

Structures are especially important because they support environmental inheritance: descendants or unrelated successors can benefit from prior construction. This can anchor groups and traditions across turnover without explicit ownership.

### 7.7 Migration and seasonal territory

Territory can be temporal. Organisms may defend:

- a breeding site during one season;
- a feeding patch only during renewal peaks;
- a sleeping site during vulnerable periods;
- a migration bottleneck during passage;
- a cache until depletion.

A static territory map would miss this. Event-log analysis should use rolling windows, season-conditioned utilization distributions, and time-varying boundary estimates.

### 7.8 Residence effects and ownership conventions

Prior residence can change contest outcome because residents know the terrain, value the site more, arrive in better condition, or follow a convention that residents escalate more. The “bourgeois” convention—resident asserts, intruder retreats—can reduce fighting costs, but it should not be coded directly. It can emerge if organisms learn place-contingent confidence and if repeated contest outcomes reinforce asymmetry.

Experiments should separate:

- physical advantage from familiarity with terrain;
- higher subjective value from better information;
- convention from actual fighting ability;
- current possession from long-term residence.

Swap organisms between mirrored sites and equalize condition to test these mechanisms.

### 7.9 Should territory be explicit world state?

| Representation | Scientific value | Risk | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| World polygon with owner/group ID | Easy access checks and visualization | Scripts ownership, borders, and factions; perfect information | Do not use in mainline simulation |
| World polygon with individual owner | Tests ownership conventions cheaply | Still grants legal-like state and hidden knowledge | Positive control only |
| Organism-learned spatial expectation | Embodied, fallible, dynamic | Memory/compute cost | Recommended |
| Repeated occupancy and active defense | Fully emergent behavior | Harder to classify | Recommended causal substrate |
| Decaying marker field | Supports advertisement and stigmergy | Can become a badge channel | Recommended with cost/decay/deception |
| Offline observer inference | No behavioral feedback; supports graded territories | Method uncertainty | Primary classification method |

The least scripted viable model is: **territory exists only as consequences in the world, expectations in organisms, and inference in the observer.**

### 7.10 Falsifiable territoriality criteria

A region should not be called a territory unless most of the following hold relative to opportunity-matched controls:

- repeated use or occupancy by the same organism/community;
- intrusions predict targeted advertisement, approach, blocking, chase, or attack;
- response intensity varies with location or site value;
- residents obtain priority access or intruders alter behavior;
- defense imposes a measurable cost;
- the pattern persists beyond a single resource event;
- boundaries or cores are reproducible across inference methods;
- removing spatial memory, markers, or defense capability selectively weakens the pattern.

## 8. Aggression and conflict

### 8.1 Damage is an affordance, not an explanation

A damage action permits harmful interaction. It does not specify motive, social scale, or organization. The same physical action may serve:

- predation;
- self-defense;
- offspring defense;
- resource defense;
- dominance;
- coercion;
- retaliation;
- punishment;
- coalitionary attack;
- accidental friendly fire.

Genesis should implement target-directed physical consequences and let context determine function. Avoid kill bonuses, enemy categories, war states, or attack goals assigned by the engine.

### 8.2 Minimal damage model

A defensible minimal model includes:

- attack direction, reach, wind-up, recovery, and energy cost;
- collision/occlusion and line-of-action constraints;
- impact based on morphology, momentum, tool properties, and target state;
- injury that impairs movement, sensing, manipulation, or reproduction;
- recovery or permanent damage;
- death when physiological state crosses a threshold;
- visible attack and injury cues;
- dropped or physically capturable resources after incapacitation;
- no automatic transfer of abstract points or territory.

Injury cost is critical. If attacks are nearly free, selection may saturate on indiscriminate aggression. If any hit is lethal, coalition experimentation may be too risky and populations may collapse. A graded injury system creates retreat, deterrence, opportunism, and numerical-advantage effects.

### 8.3 Accidental collision and competition

Before enabling damage, quantify non-aggressive interference:

- collision rate;
- blocking and displacement;
- lost feeding opportunities;
- local depletion;
- queueing around resources;
- object theft or displacement;
- reproductive competition.

These are ecological competition. They can produce negative fitness effects and spatial segregation without aggression. After damage is enabled, compare whether attacks merely track these preexisting competition hotspots.

### 8.4 Individual aggression

Individual aggression requires evidence of target selection. Useful indicators are:

- orientation and approach toward a particular organism;
- attack probability conditional on target properties;
- continuation after the original resource stimulus is removed;
- pursuit;
- repeated target-specific attacks;
- behavior change when the target is occluded or replaced.

Classify predation separately by target species/functional relation, consumption, and foraging context. Do not assume aggression toward conspecifics has the same payoff as hunting.

### 8.5 Retaliation, revenge, and deterrence

Retaliation is short- or medium-lag harm directed toward a prior attacker. Revenge is often used for longer-lag or socially mediated retaliation, but the term is cognitively loaded. Genesis should report the operational pattern: “target-specific attack hazard increased after prior harm, controlling for shared location and repeated contest.”

Deterrence exists when prior defense or retaliation reduces later intrusion/attack probability. It should be measured over repeated opportunities. Escalation can also occur if retaliation induces counter-retaliation.

Required components range from simple to complex:

- immediate counterattack needs no long-term memory;
- delayed dyadic retaliation needs identity memory;
- third-party revenge needs observation and relationship representation;
- collective deterrence needs shared response or transmitted information.

### 8.6 Dyadic fighting and dominance

Dyadic fights can create dominance-like asymmetries. Measure:

- win/loss matrices;
- direction consistency;
- transitivity, with uncertainty;
- context and location effects;
- resource priority after contests;
- avoidance and submission-like behavior;
- injury and energy costs.

Do not insert an Elo score into controllers. The observer may compute one; organisms should learn local opponent values through experience.

### 8.7 Group-correlated aggression

Group-correlated aggression exists when offline-inferred social communities predict attack direction after controlling for:

- spatial overlap and encounter rate;
- kinship;
- resource competition;
- size, age, sex, health, and morphology;
- prior dyadic history;
- shared external stimulus.

This is weaker than coalitionary aggression. A resource patch can cause members of one spatial cluster to attack members of another without coordination. Report it as a distinct level.

### 8.8 Coalitionary aggression

Coalitionary aggression requires at least two attackers and evidence of coupling. Candidate mechanisms include:

- action-following;
- alarm or recruitment signal;
- approach toward a partner's target;
- waiting for a local numerical threshold;
- encirclement or complementary positions;
- role differentiation, such as blocking and striking;
- shared memory of prior opponents;
- assistance to strongly bonded partners.

Empirical chimpanzee work shows that numerical asymmetry can reduce attacker risk and that strong social ties predict participation. Lion coalitions demonstrate that kinship can help but is not universally required; unrelated individuals can cooperate when direct reproductive or territorial benefits are large. Fiddler-crab coalitions show that helping a neighbor can be adaptive when a replacement rival would be more dangerous. Genesis should therefore avoid a single “kin coalitions” assumption.

### 8.9 Raiding and coordinated defense

A raid should require:

- directed departure from a usual area;
- multiple coupled participants;
- movement into another inferred community's repeatedly used area;
- target search, attack, theft, destruction, or displacement;
- return or dispersal after the episode;
- evidence that the destination was socially or materially valuable.

Coordinated defense requires multiple residents to converge, block, signal, chase, or attack conditional on intrusion or harm. The response can be self-organized; leadership is optional.

### 8.10 Repeated inter-group conflict

Repeated organized conflict is not simply many fights. It requires:

- persistent, independently inferred communities;
- opponent specificity across episodes;
- coalitionary coordination in a substantial subset of episodes;
- recurrence beyond encounter opportunity;
- cross-episode consequences such as avoidance, retaliation, patrol, displacement, boundary movement, resource capture, or demographic change;
- enough temporal continuity that traditions or strategies could be transmitted.

A recurring fight at a single food patch may still be resource competition. Stronger evidence includes conflict in multiple contexts, coordinated incursions, changing boundaries, or opponent-specific recruitment.

### 8.11 Environmental and social variables

| Variable | Predicted effect is conditional, not monotonic |
|---|---|
| Resource scarcity | Can increase competition, but extreme scarcity may disperse agents or cause collapse |
| Resource concentration | Increases repeated encounters and monopolizability; often more directly relevant than scarcity |
| Renewability | Persistent renewal raises long-term defense value; ephemeral resources favor mobility |
| Portability | Capturable resources make raids profitable; immobile resources make displacement valuable |
| Numerical advantage | Can lower attacker risk, but residence, terrain, and individual strength may dominate |
| Injury/death cost | Low cost promotes aggression; high cost favors avoidance, display, or asymmetric attacks |
| Reproductive skew | Can increase gains from coalition control, but also participation inequality |
| Coalition stability | Repeated partners reduce coordination uncertainty and can support reciprocity |
| Communication | Enables recruitment and target sharing but also deception and interception |
| Memory | Enables retaliation, deterrence, neighbor recognition, and recurrent opponent models |
| Leadership | Can accelerate coordination but is not necessary; may emerge from information or motivation |
| Territorial value | Raises incentive to patrol and defend concentrated sites or structures |
| Population density | Raises encounter rate up to congestion; can also reduce defendability |
| Migration | Mixes groups, imports strategies, weakens kin assortment, and may generate unfamiliarity bias |
| Interdependence | Makes partners valuable and can restrain lethal escalation |
| Revenge/deterrence | Can stabilize boundaries or create feud cycles |
| Capture vs destruction | Capture produces direct benefit; destruction may only reduce future competition and can be costly |

### 8.12 Is scarcity plus kin-biased grouping sufficient?

The claim is overstated.

**Supported component:** kin-biased spatial structure can increase within-cluster cooperation and between-cluster differentiation. Scarce or defendable resources can increase competition. Numerical advantage and low-risk attacks can make coalitionary aggression profitable in some systems.

**Missing mechanisms:** organized violence still needs opportunity, target recognition, coordination, benefits, risk management, and demographic persistence. Kin clusters may avoid one another, migrate, fuse, share, or compete nonviolently. Scarcity can reduce group size and encounter frequency. If resources cannot be captured or defended, attacking may waste energy. If injury is severe and numerical assessment poor, coalitions may not form.

**Required Genesis test:** fully cross kin-cue availability, actual relatedness, scarcity, concentration, renewability, damage, signals, and social memory in staged or fractional-factorial designs. Do not infer a causal “scarcity-to-war” path from one combined treatment.

## 9. Requirements for organized inter-group conflict

### 9.1 A dependency graph

Organized inter-group conflict is best treated as a high-order phenomenon with prerequisite layers:

```text
embodied movement and encounter opportunities
        ↓
repeated association / spatial communities
        ↓
recognition, familiarity, or cue-based assortment
        ↓
cooperation or mutual interdependence within some relationships
        ↓
shared attention, action-following, or recruitment
        ↓
target-specific aggression with real risk and benefit
        ↓
coalitionary coordination above independent-action nulls
        ↓
persistent opponent differentiation and cross-episode memory
        ↓
recurrent territorial/resource/demographic consequences
        ↓
organized inter-group conflict
```

Not every biological case follows this exact linear order, but skipping early layers makes later results difficult to interpret.

### 9.2 Minimum plausible mechanism set

For Genesis, a minimal coalitionary-conflict treatment likely requires:

1. **At least three simultaneously sustainable social clusters** so results are not forced into a binary partition.
2. **Local recognition** through familiarity, phenotype, signals, place association, or some combination.
3. **Target-directed damage** with graded injury and retreat.
4. **Observation of partners' movement and attacks.**
5. **A recruitment route:** action-following may suffice; a dedicated but semantically neutral signal channel is the next treatment.
6. **Concentrated persistent value:** renewable patch, cache, structure, nest, route, or reproductive opportunity.
7. **Spatial memory** sufficient to revisit and distinguish high-value regions.
8. **Asymmetric encounter opportunities:** local numerical advantage, surprise, resident advantage, terrain, or partner strength.
9. **Individual-level payoff consequences** for participation, nonparticipation, survival, resource capture, or group augmentation.
10. **Demographic recovery** so rare conflict can recur rather than terminate the experiment.

### 9.3 Numerical advantage and risk sensitivity

The imbalance-of-power hypothesis proposes that coalitionary killing becomes attractive when attackers can achieve overwhelming numerical advantage and therefore low personal risk. Chimpanzee data are consistent with a major role for asymmetry in many lethal attacks, but this should not be generalized into a universal rule. Sifaka and other systems show residence and location effects; gorilla coalitionary attacks can arise under different social structures; human warfare includes retaliation, alliance obligations, exchange, coercion, and defense.

Genesis should let agents estimate advantage only through perception and learning:

- local count and proximity of familiar allies;
- observed opponent size/health;
- injury history;
- location value and escape routes;
- signal responses;
- recent arrival of partners.

Do not provide a global team-strength comparison.

### 9.4 Participation and free riding

Conflict participation can be a collective-action problem, but not always. Participation may be directly beneficial through:

- immediate resource capture;
- protection of offspring, mate, cache, or shelter;
- preservation of strong social partners;
- increased group size or future support;
- status or mating benefits;
- avoidance of punishment or exclusion;
- reciprocal assistance.

Chimpanzee boundary-patrol research suggests mutualistic group augmentation and strong social ties can explain participation without assuming abstract group loyalty. Genesis should measure each participant's expected and realized effects, not label all participation altruistic.

### 9.5 Communication and coalition recruitment

Possible low-cognition recruitment paths are:

- follow a familiar organism that leaves a congregation point;
- approach a target another familiar organism attacks;
- emit a local pulse when detecting an intruder;
- increase signal amplitude with nearby allies;
- wait until local allied presence exceeds a learned threshold;
- mark or repeatedly visit a frontier route.

A signal is not necessary for initial coalition tests. The comparison `action observation only` versus `neutral local signals added` is scientifically valuable. If coalition rates rise only with a signal whose semantics were authored, the result is weak. If arbitrary channels acquire recruitment meaning through evolution or learning, the result is stronger.

### 9.6 Leadership

Leadership may emerge from:

- superior spatial information;
- higher resource need or reproductive stake;
- lower injury sensitivity;
- greater strength;
- social centrality;
- consistent initiation followed by learned trust;
- signal production differences.

Measure leadership through influence: does one individual's movement or action predict changes in others after controlling for shared stimuli? Remove or replace high-influence individuals and test whether coordination reorganizes. Do not create a leader gene or command privilege.

### 9.7 Territorial and material value

Organized attacks are more plausible when successful action changes future payoffs. Candidate consequences include:

- displacement from a renewable patch;
- access to a cache or structure;
- capture of portable resources;
- destruction or appropriation of tools;
- reduced rival density near breeding sites;
- acquisition of a safe route or shelter;
- marker replacement and boundary shift.

A kill with no material, spatial, reproductive, or deterrent consequence may still occur, but repeated costly organization is less likely to be selected.

### 9.8 Population density and geometry

Very low density prevents repeated encounters. Very high density can erase stable spatial communities and make every location contested. Geometry matters:

- bottlenecks create predictable confrontation points;
- large open spaces permit avoidance;
- separated patches promote discrete clusters;
- overlapping resource gradients create soft boundaries;
- refuges and escape routes alter injury risk;
- visibility and occlusion affect surprise and coordination.

Density should be analyzed as encounter opportunities per organism-time, not only individuals per area.

### 9.9 Recurrence, revenge, and deterrence

Repeated conflict requires memory or persistent environmental effects. Recurrence can be driven by:

- revenge against remembered attackers;
- reoccupation of contested resources;
- marker replacement;
- unresolved boundary overlap;
- demographic recovery;
- inherited avoidance or aggression tendencies;
- socially transmitted patrol or attack conventions.

The same mechanisms can produce deterrence and fewer attacks. A successful defense may stabilize a boundary rather than intensify war. Genesis should measure both event frequency and changes in intrusion hazard.

### 9.10 Capturing resources versus destroying competitors

Distinguish payoffs:

- **capture:** goods, tools, structures, territory, or access change hands;
- **exclusion:** rivals leave, but assets remain;
- **denial:** resources or structures are destroyed so nobody benefits;
- **demographic reduction:** fewer competitors remain later;
- **deterrence:** future intrusion falls;
- **status/reproductive effects:** participation changes social access.

These produce different evolutionary dynamics. A system that rewards kills directly will overproduce destruction. A physical world in which benefits must be carried, occupied, repaired, or exploited better tests whether conflict is economically sustainable.

### 9.11 Operational standard for “organized inter-group conflict”

Do not apply the label unless all six conditions hold:

1. **Persistent social partitions:** at least two inferred interaction communities persist across multiple analysis windows and are not reducible to one temporary patch.
2. **Directed hostility:** aggression is disproportionately between those communities after opportunity controls.
3. **Coalitionary coordination:** a nontrivial fraction of episodes exceed temporal/trajectory/target-coupling nulls.
4. **Recurrence:** the same community pair has repeated episodes beyond encounter expectations.
5. **Cross-episode state:** prior episodes change later behavior, spatial use, signaling, defense, or target choice.
6. **Consequences:** conflict changes resources, structures, territory-use, injuries, mortality, reproduction, or deterrence.

A seventh, stronger criterion for culturally organized conflict is that a locally distinctive tactic, route, signal, weapon use, or participation norm persists across demographic turnover and depends on social learning.

## 10. Evidence from artificial systems

### 10.1 What artificial systems can and cannot establish

Artificial systems are valuable because mechanisms, costs, population structure, and ablations can be controlled precisely. They do not prove that the same mechanism dominates in nature, and they can easily manufacture an outcome through representation choices. Evidence should be sorted into four classes:

1. **Existence proof:** a mechanism can produce a pattern.
2. **Robustness result:** the pattern survives parameter, noise, mutation, and seed variation.
3. **Comparative causal result:** removing one mechanism selectively removes the pattern.
4. **Open-ended emergence result:** the relevant categories and strategies were not fixed in the reward, representation, or team structure.

Many social-agent papers are strong existence proofs but weak evidence for open-ended emergence because agents are assigned teams, games, tags, or rewards that already define the social ontology.

### 10.2 Collective-motion models

Reynolds' “boids” and later biologically grounded models show that local separation, alignment, and cohesion can produce flocking without centralized control. Couzin et al. (2002) demonstrated multiple collective states and spatial sorting from local interaction zones. Buhl et al. (2006) experimentally validated a density-dependent order transition in locusts. Couzin et al. (2005) showed leadership-like information flow from a minority of informed individuals without explicit leader identity.

**Genesis lesson:** spatial grouping and influence can be tested with very small controller repertoires. These systems are a baseline for aggregation, not evidence of cooperation or factions.

### 10.3 Spatial evolutionary games and networks

Spatial Prisoner's Dilemma models show that local interaction can let cooperators form clusters that protect one another from exploitation (Nowak & May, 1992). Graph-theoretic work derives conditions under which network degree and update rules affect cooperation (Ohtsuki et al., 2006; Taylor et al., 2007). The important caution is that outcomes can reverse when update rules, interaction neighborhoods, migration, or competition scales change.

**Genesis lesson:** “space promotes cooperation” is not a universal law. The exact birth-death schedule, dispersal, local competition, and interaction radius are causal mechanisms. They must be versioned and experimentally varied.

### 10.4 Tag-based cooperation

Riolo, Cohen, and Axelrod (2001) demonstrated cooperation based on arbitrary similarity tags without reciprocity or memory. Roberts and Sherratt (2002) showed that a key result could collapse when identical-tag individuals were allowed to defect, prompting a published response. Axelrod, Hammond, and Grafen (2004) analyzed coevolving tags and conditional altruism. Hammond and Axelrod (2006) produced high in-group cooperation and out-group defection in an ethnocentrism ABM.

These models are theoretically important and directly relevant as **positive controls**, but they also illustrate Genesis's central risk: if agents receive a clear tag and a contingent same/different response gene, faction-like behavior is nearly built into the representation.

**Genesis lesson:** use continuous, noisy, costly, independently evolving cues; let cue production, perception, tolerance, and action be separable. Compare against an explicit-tag treatment to quantify the shortcut.

### 10.5 Evolutionary robotics and kin assortment

Waibel, Floreano, and Keller (2011) used evolved simulated robots to manipulate helping costs, benefits, and relatedness, obtaining quantitative agreement with Hamilton's rule despite a complex genotype-to-behavior mapping. Waibel, Keller, and Floreano (2009) showed that genetic team composition and individual- versus team-level selection interact with the cooperation and credit-assignment demands of a task.

**Genesis lesson:** embodied controllers can evolve helping under controlled assortment, but group-level fitness assignment changes the problem. Genesis should retain individual reproduction and let shared ecological effects generate any between-group covariance.

### 10.6 Evolution of communication and concealment

Mitri, Floreano, and Keller (2009) evolved robots that emitted visible light while foraging. Initially, light near food created useful inadvertent information. Competition then selected for suppression, but information did not vanish completely. This demonstrates a coevolutionary information ecology rather than a one-way path toward honest communication.

**Genesis lesson:** every signal channel must support silence, deception, eavesdropping, and cue exploitation. Signal meaning and reliability should be outcomes.

### 10.7 Reciprocity bootstrapping in evolutionary robotics

André and colleagues' evolutionary-robotics work found that reciprocity may be rare because the necessary controller functions do not evolve gradually under many conditions; a fully contingent strategy can require simultaneous recognition, memory, helping, and response machinery. Even when reciprocity is stable once present, it may be inaccessible from the current mutational neighborhood.

**Genesis lesson:** distinguish evolutionary stability from evolutionary accessibility. Seeded reciprocity, evolved-from-scratch reciprocity, kin-scaffolded reciprocity, and partner-choice-scaffolded reciprocity are separate treatments.

### 10.8 Stigmergic construction and swarm systems

Termite-inspired construction robots can build complex structures using local rules and environmental cues without centralized plans (Werfel, Petersen, & Nagpal, 2014). Ant-inspired systems show route formation and task allocation through local markers and response thresholds. These systems are generally designed rather than open-endedly evolved, but they establish that persistent environmental state can coordinate agents with minimal cognition.

**Genesis lesson:** shared structures and decaying markers are high-leverage mechanisms for cooperation, territory, and traditions. Their presence should precede symbolic communication.

### 10.9 Emergent exchange in embodied multi-agent learning

Garbus and Pollack (2024) reported an emergent resource-exchange protocol based on dropping and picking up resources. A campfire created congregation and low-pressure interaction time; agents learned defenses against some cheating and showed tolerated-theft-like patterns without dedicated punishment or combat mechanics.

**Genesis lesson:** social opportunity structure—where agents pause, meet repeatedly, and can manipulate transferable goods—may be more important than an explicit “trade” action. Implement physical transfer and shared gathering sites, not a market API.

### 10.10 Multi-agent reinforcement learning social dilemmas

Sequential social-dilemma environments show that cooperation, exploitation, inequity responses, and conventions can emerge in temporally extended tasks. However, many studies fix team membership, reward functions, episode boundaries, and action semantics. Hide-and-seek autocurricula can produce tool use and strategic escalation, but predefined teams and competitive reward structures mean they are evidence for strategy complexity, not spontaneous group formation.

**Genesis lesson:** use these systems for controller and curriculum insights, not as evidence that factions emerged. In Genesis, survival and reproduction should arise from world physics and ecology rather than a team score.

### 10.11 Artificial societies and explicit cultural tags

Classic artificial-society models such as Sugarscape demonstrate how local resource landscapes, trade, migration, inheritance, disease, cultural features, and conflict can interact. They are useful for exploring macro-patterns, but “tribe” or culture tags and explicit combat rules can directly impose the categories under study.

**Genesis lesson:** preserve the rich ecological and demographic interactions while omitting authored tribe membership. If a Sugarscape-like tag treatment is implemented, label it a mechanistic contrast, not the main model.

### 10.12 Digital evolution and public goods

Digital-evolution systems have shown cooperation, cheating, complex traits, and ecological feedback under mutation and selection. Microbial experiments and models similarly show that public signals and goods are vulnerable to cheaters and that relatedness or spatial structure can protect cooperation (for example, Diggle et al., 2007). Digital organisms often have simplified interaction and reproduction semantics, so conclusions depend on the mapping between code, phenotype, and ecological payoff.

**Genesis lesson:** record actual donor costs and recipient benefits, and avoid assuming that a labeled cooperative instruction has the same evolutionary meaning as embodied help.

### 10.13 Dynamic social-network analysis

Palla et al. (2007) and Mucha et al. (2010) developed methods for tracking evolving communities in temporal networks. Network-based diffusion analysis has been used to test social transmission in wild chimpanzees (Hobaiter et al., 2014). These methods support observer-side group and tradition inference without online membership.

**Genesis lesson:** dynamic communities should be inferred from rolling, weighted, multiplex interaction networks. Results must be checked across window sizes and algorithms; the clustering output must never enter the simulation.

### 10.14 Evidence matrix

| Artificial-system family | What it demonstrates well | What is usually pre-authored | Genesis use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boids/collective motion | Aggregation and decentralized motion | Local response architecture | Stage 1 baseline |
| Spatial games | Assortment and local cluster protection | Payoff game and action labels | Theory/control, not world mechanics |
| Tag cooperation ABMs | Cue-conditioned assortment and ethnocentrism | Tag dimensions and in/out response | Positive control for faction shortcut |
| Evolutionary robotics | Embodied genotype-to-behavior evolution | Task arena and fitness schedule | Strong design precedent |
| Swarm construction | Stigmergy and decentralized building | Construction target/rules | Mechanism inspiration, not emergence proof |
| Sequential MARL dilemmas | Learned long-horizon cooperation/exploitation | Rewards, teams, episode tasks | Controller benchmark only |
| Artificial societies | Macro-demographic interactions | Culture/tribe fields and policy rules | Ecological inspiration with categories removed |
| Temporal-network methods | Offline group persistence and diffusion inference | Analysis windows/algorithms | Observer toolkit |

## 11. Recommended minimal Genesis mechanisms

### 11.1 Design principles

The recommended mechanisms obey six constraints:

1. **No privileged membership:** organisms never receive an inferred group, culture, territory, or species label.
2. **Perception before classification:** all social distinctions arise from sensory cues and learned history.
3. **Physical actions before social verbs:** carry, drop, push, block, signal, strike, repair, and follow replace authored actions such as cooperate, punish, recruit, raid, or declare war.
4. **Costs and consequences are explicit:** energy, time, risk, injury, missed opportunity, and resource flows are logged.
5. **Every high-level claim has a null and ablation:** patterns are not accepted from visual inspection.
6. **All learned and environmental state is replayable:** social memory, markers, signal fields, injury, and structures are versioned checkpoint state.

### 11.2 M1 — Local embodied social perception

**Minimal implementation.** A deterministic spatial query returns a bounded, sorted set of nearby visible bodies and actions. Inputs include relative bearing, distance, velocity, orientation, visible morphology, carried object features, current action class, and injury cues.

**Required perception.** Line-of-sight or local-radius sensing with occlusion and finite resolution.

**Required memory.** None for basic aggregation; a short sensor history helps estimate motion.

**Required actions.** Move, orient, stop, approach, withdraw, and optionally follow.

**Genetic component.** Sensor ranges/costs if evolvable; controller weights and attention biases.

**Learned component.** Optional cue salience and action-outcome associations.

**Cultural component.** None initially; later, local movement conventions may spread.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Continuous or fine-grained space with collision and visibility constraints.

**Determinism risk.** Neighbor enumeration and vector reductions can depend on hash or traversal order.

**Computational cost.** With a spatial hash, approximately O(N + E_local) per tick; cap perceived neighbors at `k`.

**Control condition.** Bodies are replaced by non-social moving objects with identical motion statistics.

**Ablation.** Remove conspecific motion/orientation inputs while preserving resource sensing.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Social aggregation and alignment should fall selectively, while resource-driven clustering remains.

### 11.3 M2 — Independently tunable resource supply, concentration, and renewal

**Minimal implementation.** Resources occupy physical locations, regenerate through versioned deterministic rules, can be consumed or carried, and vary along independent axes: total supply, patchiness, patch size, renewal, predictability, portability, and storability.

**Required perception.** Local resource amount/type and possibly gradients.

**Required memory.** None for opportunistic foraging; spatial memory for persistent exploitation and defense.

**Required actions.** Move, consume, collect, carry, drop, cache, and manipulate.

**Genetic component.** Foraging preferences, movement strategy, carrying capacity, metabolism.

**Learned component.** Patch values, renewal timing, depletion history.

**Cultural component.** Routes, processing techniques, cache conventions may later transmit.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Ecological replacement sufficient to sustain multiple generations.

**Determinism risk.** Resource regeneration draws must come from keyed streams; cell updates require stable ordering.

**Computational cost.** O(active resource cells) plus organism interactions; sparse patches reduce cost.

**Control condition.** Same total supply distributed diffusely versus concentrated; same concentration at different total supply.

**Ablation.** Flatten the resource field while preserving global abundance.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Concentration should increase repeated co-presence and defendability independently of scarcity; diffuse scarcity should not automatically produce stable territories or coalitions.

### 11.4 M3 — Evolvable social-motion response

**Minimal implementation.** Controllers can condition movement on local bodies and their motion; no dedicated flocking equation is required if the neural controller has adequate inputs and outputs. A hand-coded local-rule baseline may be retained for validation.

**Required perception.** Relative motion and proximity.

**Required memory.** None; short-term memory enables leader following and route persistence.

**Required actions.** Approach, align, avoid, follow, pause.

**Genetic component.** Neural topology/weights, social attraction thresholds, sensory investment.

**Learned component.** Familiar-partner attraction and context-specific following.

**Cultural component.** Route and departure conventions may spread later.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Locomotion costs and benefits of grouping or co-location.

**Determinism risk.** Simultaneous movement resolution and collision arbitration.

**Computational cost.** Dominated by local perception; O(E_local).

**Control condition.** Resource field replayed with social inputs masked.

**Ablation.** Randomly rotate observed neighbor velocity vectors while retaining positions.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Polarization and collective departures should fall more than static co-location.

### 11.5 M4 — Heritable perceptible phenotype cues

**Minimal implementation.** A small cue vector is generated from ordinary heritable traits and development, expressed through visible/olfactory/acoustic-like phenotype, and sensed with finite error. Cue production and response genes are independent.

**Required perception.** Cue vector with range, noise, and occlusion appropriate to modality.

**Required memory.** None for innate preference; template memory for phenotype matching.

**Required actions.** Any social action can condition on perceived cue values.

**Genetic component.** Cue production, receptor sensitivity, matching thresholds, response biases, linkage architecture.

**Learned component.** Self, caregiver, or early-companion templates; updated cue reliability.

**Cultural component.** Signals may become conventional, but phenotype cues alone are genetic/developmental.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Repeated social encounters and enough assortment for cue use to matter.

**Determinism risk.** Platform-dependent distance math and sensory-noise draws.

**Computational cost.** O(k × cue_dimensions) per perceiver; keep dimensions small.

**Control condition.** Permute cue vectors among bodies while preserving pedigree and behavior.

**Ablation.** Set cue-response weights to zero or decorrelate cues from ancestry.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Cue-dependent assortment should disappear under permutation; pedigree assortment caused by limited dispersal may remain.

### 11.6 M5 — Bounded familiarity and social memory

**Minimal implementation.** Each organism has `m` deterministic memory slots containing a learned perceptual fingerprint, familiarity, received benefit, received harm, confidence, and last-use tick. Updates are local and serializable.

**Required perception.** Re-identifiable perceptual features; identity can be uncertain.

**Required memory.** Fixed sparse table; no global history.

**Required actions.** Partner choice, approach/avoid, aid/refuse, retaliate, signal.

**Genetic component.** Capacity, learning rates, decay, generalization, value sensitivity.

**Learned component.** All partner values and fingerprints.

**Cultural component.** Social memory can support conventions and reputation but is not itself culture.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Repeated encounters and meaningful partner heterogeneity.

**Determinism risk.** Hash-map order, ties in recognition, and nondeterministic eviction.

**Computational cost.** O(m) naive matching or O(log m)/indexed matching; likely O(Nm) memory overall.

**Control condition.** Same capacity used for non-social environmental memories.

**Ablation.** Reset social records at fixed intervals or replace partner keys with cue-class averages.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Partner-specific reciprocity, dear-enemy effects, and delayed retaliation should collapse; immediate mutualism and kin assortment may persist.

### 11.7 M6 — Physical carrying, dropping, transfer, and tolerated access

**Minimal implementation.** Resources and objects can be held, moved, dropped, handed within reach, stolen, spilled, or left in shared space. Transfer changes physical possession and energy/resource ledgers; there is no `share` verb.

**Required perception.** Nearby bodies, held objects, ground objects, reachability, recipient state.

**Required memory.** None for provisioning; partner memory for exchange and reciprocity.

**Required actions.** Pick up, hold, drop, place, offer/release, take, consume.

**Genetic component.** Carrying morphology, release thresholds, social preferences, controller parameters.

**Learned component.** Partner value, need recognition, exchange expectations.

**Cultural component.** Exchange locations, tolerated-taking conventions, and object-gift traditions may spread.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Portable resources, storage limits, and situations creating asymmetric need or acquisition.

**Determinism risk.** Simultaneous pickup/transfer conflicts and object ownership arbitration.

**Computational cost.** O(local object interactions); event logging can be large.

**Control condition.** Identical resources made nonportable or instantly consumed.

**Ablation.** Disable intentional release near others while preserving accidental drops.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Measured directed sharing and exchange should fall, while spillover and tolerated co-feeding remain distinguishable.


### 11.8 M7 — Shared structures, caches, and persistent world modification

**Minimal implementation.** Organisms can place and combine ordinary materials into persistent configurations; structures alter shelter, movement, visibility, storage, or processing through general physical properties. Caches retain objects. Paths or terrain can be modified where the broader world model allows it.

**Required perception.** Local material, geometry, structure state, accessibility, and visible work by others.

**Required memory.** None for reactive stigmergy; spatial/object memory for reuse and maintenance.

**Required actions.** Carry, place, push, join, detach, repair, enter, block, retrieve.

**Genetic component.** Manipulation capabilities, material preferences, controller policy, learning biases.

**Learned component.** Place value, action affordances, maintenance outcomes.

**Cultural component.** Construction sequences, site traditions, maintenance norms, and artifact reuse can transmit.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Materials with persistent, general consequences; no recipe table is required.

**Determinism risk.** Contact resolution, combination order, structural updates, and floating-point geometry.

**Computational cost.** Potentially high: object count, collision broad phase, structure graph updates, and persistence.

**Control condition.** Same materials decay rapidly or structures provide no shared benefit.

**Ablation.** Remove persistence while preserving immediate manipulation possibilities.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Long-lived congregation sites, environmental inheritance, stigmergic task continuation, and structure-centered defense should fall selectively.

### 11.9 M8 — Costly semantically neutral local signals

**Minimal implementation.** Organisms emit bounded analog pulses on a small number of channels. Signals attenuate with distance and may be directional. Production costs energy/time and can be withheld or falsified.

**Required perception.** Channel amplitude, direction, timing, and optionally emitter perceptual features.

**Required memory.** None for innate response; associative memory for learned meaning and trust.

**Required actions.** Emit channel values, orient, approach, withdraw, join, ignore.

**Genetic component.** Emission policy, receptor sensitivity, signal cost tolerance, innate biases.

**Learned component.** Context-signal-response associations and emitter reliability.

**Cultural component.** Local conventions, recruitment calls, alarms, and dialects can emerge and spread.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Contexts in which information changes receiver outcomes.

**Determinism risk.** Propagation order, mixing, noise, and simultaneous emissions.

**Computational cost.** O(local emitter-receiver edges) or field-cell updates; cap range and channels.

**Control condition.** Sham channels with identical energetic cost but random receiver mapping.

**Ablation.** Mute emission, scramble channel identity, or block receiver learning.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Signal-conditioned recruitment or defense should collapse under appropriate ablation; action-observation-only coordination may remain.

### 11.10 M9 — Decaying environmental markers

**Minimal implementation.** Organisms deposit a bounded local vector field or physical trace that decays deterministically and can be mixed, masked, overwritten, or erased. Organisms perceive field values, not depositor IDs.

**Required perception.** Local field intensity, gradients, age proxy, and possibly heritable cue components.

**Required memory.** None for trail following; spatial association memory for territory and deception.

**Required actions.** Deposit, overmark, erase, follow, avoid, investigate.

**Genetic component.** Marker chemistry/vector, production cost, receptor tuning, response policy.

**Learned component.** Marker-context value and depositor-class associations.

**Cultural component.** Route, boundary, cache, and work-site conventions.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Persistent space where delayed information is useful.

**Determinism risk.** Field diffusion/reduction order, sparse-cell iteration, and decay rounding.

**Computational cost.** O(active marker cells × channels) plus sensing; sparse representation recommended.

**Control condition.** Equal energetic cost with zero persistence, or marker fields spatially shifted in observer-only replay.

**Ablation.** Set decay to one tick, disable deposition, or scramble channel vectors.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Marker-dependent route reinforcement, low-contact boundary maintenance, and delayed stigmergic work should weaken while direct encounter responses remain.

### 11.11 M10 — Bounded spatial memory and learned site value

**Minimal implementation.** Organisms maintain a coarse, sparse set of place memories or landmark-relative values: resource yield, hazard, structure/caches, social outcomes, marker patterns, and uncertainty. No global territory polygon is stored.

**Required perception.** Local landmarks, position estimate or path integration, resource/social events.

**Required memory.** Fixed-capacity place table or quantized map with deterministic decay and eviction.

**Required actions.** Navigate, revisit, patrol, avoid, cache, mark, defend.

**Genetic component.** Capacity, learning rates, exploration policy, site-fidelity bias.

**Learned component.** All place values and route expectations.

**Cultural component.** Routes and site preferences may be socially transmitted later.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Persistent spatial heterogeneity and revisit opportunities.

**Determinism risk.** Coordinate quantization, path integration, map merging, tie-breaking.

**Computational cost.** O(place slots) memory per organism; bounded lookup per decision.

**Control condition.** Preserve sensory inputs but reset place memory each life phase or periodically.

**Ablation.** Remove social-outcome layers while preserving food memory, and vice versa.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Site fidelity, learned resident advantage, patrol routes, and stable territory use should fall according to the removed layer; immediate patch attraction remains.

### 11.12 M11 — Target-directed damage, injury, recovery, and death

**Minimal implementation.** General attack/manipulation actions can apply force or damage within physical reach. Damage costs energy, has latency/cooldown, and causes graded impairment, recovery, and possible death. Resources remain physical and may be dropped or occupied after conflict.

**Required perception.** Target location/state, attack cues, nearby partners/opponents, escape geometry.

**Required memory.** None for immediate defense; social/spatial memory for retaliation, deterrence, and raids.

**Required actions.** Strike, shove, bite-like action, block, flee, pursue, release; exact repertoire depends on body model.

**Genetic component.** Morphology, aggression thresholds, risk sensitivity, attack policy, healing allocation.

**Learned component.** Opponent strength, injury risk, location-contingent confidence, retaliation history.

**Cultural component.** Attack timing, recruitment, route, object use, and restraint conventions can later transmit.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Contested value and enough demographic buffering to survive costly conflict.

**Determinism risk.** Simultaneous impacts, collision order, damage accumulation, and death-trigger ordering.

**Computational cost.** Local physics plus event logs; conflict spikes can increase interaction density.

**Control condition.** Threat/display action with identical energetic cost but no injury; or damage enabled with no capturable/defendable value.

**Ablation.** Disable damage while preserving collision, blocking, signals, and avoidance.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Casualties and physical coercion disappear, but group boundaries, signaling, sharing, and nonviolent defense may remain. Organized conflict should not be inferred merely because pre-damage crowd competition existed.

### 11.13 M12 — Observational and social learning

**Minimal implementation.** Observation of another's action and outcome modifies the learner's attention, action value, or sequence probability. Begin with local/stimulus enhancement and outcome copying; add higher-fidelity sequence imitation only as an explicit later capability.

**Required perception.** Actor, action, target/object, outcome, and context within sensory limits.

**Required memory.** Demonstration traces or incremental action-value updates; model identity optional.

**Required actions.** Existing physical actions; no separate cultural action.

**Genetic component.** Social-learning rate, model bias, conformity/novelty bias, attention, critical periods.

**Learned component.** Behavior values, object affordances, signal responses, routes, tactics.

**Cultural component.** This mechanism enables conventions and traditions by definition, but cultural status still requires observer evidence.

**Environmental prerequisite.** Behavioral variants with differential outcomes and opportunities to observe tolerant models.

**Determinism risk.** Observation ordering, simultaneous demonstrations, learning-update reductions, and keyed exploration draws.

**Computational cost.** O(observed salient events); event filtering is essential at scale.

**Control condition.** Yoked individual learning receives identical environmental outcomes but no actor-action information.

**Ablation.** Disable social updates while preserving perception; scramble model identity; remove conformity bias only.

**Falsifiable prediction.** Group-specific variants should converge, fail to spread along social-network paths, or disappear across turnover while genetically or ecologically driven behavior remains.

### 11.14 Explicit comparison of required design toggles

| Toggle | Without | With | Main scientific use | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explicit group IDs | Dynamic, uncertain groups inferred from interactions | Perfect membership and in/out tests | Positive-control upper bound | Factions by construction |
| Kin recognition | Kin effects only through spatial assortment and caregiving | Cue-conditioned kin bias possible | Separate assortment from recognition | Direct pedigree input trivializes result |
| Social memory | Mutualism, by-product benefit, kin assortment, stigmergy | Reciprocity, partner choice, retaliation, neighbor recognition | Test cognition dependency | Large state and identity shortcuts |
| Signals | Coordination through cues, movement, and artifacts | Recruitment, alarm, conventions, deception | Test communication's marginal contribution | Authored meanings or free broadcast |
| Shared structures | Sociality centered on bodies/resources | Persistent public goods, caches, environmental inheritance | Test artifact-mediated groups | Hidden ownership or recipes |
| Damage | Competition, blocking, avoidance, theft | Injury, coercion, defense, killing | Distinguish aggression from nonviolent competition | Aggression saturation/extinction |
| Resource scarcity | Lower demand pressure | Higher demand/supply ratio | Test competition pressure | Confounded with patchiness |
| Resource concentration | Diffuse, less monopolizable value | Repeated encounters and defendability | Test resource-defense theory | One hotspot mistaken for group |
| Territorial markers | Boundaries rely on encounters, structures, memory | Low-cost advertisement and stigmergy | Test marker-mediated defense | Marker becomes faction badge |

### 11.15 Recommended release order

**Phase A — Social opportunity without social labels**

- M1 local embodied perception;
- M2 resource structure;
- M3 social movement;
- M6 carrying/drop mechanics in non-cooperative use.

**Phase B — Recognition and affiliation**

- M4 phenotype cues;
- M5 familiarity/social memory;
- M10 spatial memory.

**Phase C — Cooperation and environmental inheritance**

- intentional transfer affordances within M6;
- M7 structures/caches;
- M9 markers.

**Phase D — Coordination**

- M8 neutral signals;
- joint manipulation and action observation refinements.

**Phase E — Conflict**

- M11 damage/injury only after stable nonviolent baselines.

**Phase F — Culture**

- M12 social learning after multiple independently learnable variants exist.

### 11.16 Mechanisms not recommended as mainline primitives

- faction or tribe IDs;
- inherited diplomatic state;
- explicit ally/enemy lists created by the engine;
- global reputation score;
- owner-tagged territory polygons;
- war declarations;
- coalition or squad objects visible to controllers;
- group fitness bonuses;
- attack rewards or kill points;
- scripted punishment roles;
- leader commands;
- fixed signal semantics;
- fixed cooperative tasks labeled for controllers;
- cultural trait labels directly copied as tokens.

Any of these may be useful as a **positive control** demonstrating the behavior an easier scripted system would produce. They should not be confused with the target architecture.

## 12. Event-log measurements

### 12.1 Measurement principle

The observer must log **opportunities, actions, and consequences**. Logging only actions creates severe bias: an organism that attacks 10 times after 10 encounters is more aggressive than one that attacks 10 times after 1,000 encounters, even though the raw count is equal. Likewise, zero sharing is uninterpretable if no organism ever possessed a transferable surplus near another organism.

Metrics should be computed over organism-time, dyad-opportunities, resource-opportunities, and episode exposure. High-level labels are derived after simulation and stored separately from replay-critical state.

### 12.2 Core event schema

Every event should have a deterministic sequence key:

```text
(run_id, tick, phase_id, event_type_order, actor_id, target_id_or_zero, local_seq)
```

Recommended common fields:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `run_id`, `seed_manifest_hash`, `build_hash`, `schema_version` | Reproducibility and provenance |
| `tick`, `substep`, `event_seq` | Stable temporal ordering |
| `actor_id`, `target_entity_id`, `object_id`, `location_id` | Actor-target linkage; IDs remain observer-only |
| actor and target positions/orientations | Spatial context and trajectory reconstruction |
| action type and parameters | Physical behavior, not inferred social function |
| perceptual opportunity summary | Which bodies, objects, signals, and resources were detectable |
| energy, health, injury, inventory before/after | Cost and consequence accounting |
| resource amount/type before/after | Sharing, theft, capture, depletion |
| signal channel/amplitude/direction | Communication analysis |
| marker deposit/erase and field delta | Stigmergy and territorial analysis |
| structure/material state delta | Construction, maintenance, destruction, reuse |
| reproduction, birth, death, parent IDs | Demography and lineage |
| controller/action-state hash where practical | Debugging and causal replay |
| local environment snapshot hash | Verify matched opportunities without logging the full world |

If complete perception logging is too expensive, retain deterministic replay checkpoints and enough indices to reconstruct perception windows exactly. For rare conflict and transfer events, store richer pre/post context than for routine movement.

### 12.3 Derived event types

The observer can derive episode tables without changing the engine:

- encounter opportunity;
- co-presence bout;
- affiliative contact;
- resource acquisition and depletion;
- intentional or ambiguous transfer;
- joint manipulation;
- signal emission and reception opportunity;
- marker encounter;
- intrusion into another entity's high-use area;
- target-directed attack;
- fight episode;
- coalition candidate;
- raid candidate;
- defense candidate;
- structure-building/maintenance episode;
- migration/fission/fusion event;
- learning adoption candidate.

Derived tables must record the exact algorithm version and parameters used.

### 12.4 Dynamic group inference

Construct a temporal multiplex network in rolling or event-adaptive windows. Nodes are organisms. Edge layers may include:

- proximity beyond ecological expectation;
- following/alignment;
- affiliative contact;
- resource transfer;
- tolerated co-feeding;
- joint manipulation;
- signaling/reception;
- aid/defense;
- aggression, kept as a signed or separate layer.

A normalized dyadic association can be defined as:

\[
A_{ij}(w)=\frac{I_{ij}(w)}{O_{ij}(w)+\epsilon},
\]

where \(I_{ij}\) is weighted interaction in window \(w\), and \(O_{ij}\) is estimated opportunity based on simultaneous activity, visibility, spatial overlap, and relevant resource state. This prevents co-resident but rarely interacting organisms from appearing strongly affiliated merely because they share a map.

Use at least two inference approaches, such as:

- dynamic modularity/multilayer community detection;
- link clustering or temporal connected components with persistence thresholds;
- latent block or relational-event models for confirmatory analysis.

All ties must be resolved deterministically, but scientific conclusions should not rely on the arbitrary tie-break. Report sensitivity to window length, edge weighting, resolution, and algorithm.

### 12.5 Group cohesion

Report several nonredundant measures:

- mean and median pairwise distance within inferred group, normalized by detection radius and environmental density;
- proximity-network density and weighted strength;
- velocity polarization during movement;
- angular dispersion;
- fraction of active time in the largest connected component;
- split/merge frequency;
- nearest-neighbor identity persistence;
- within-group interaction rate divided by opportunity.

A useful standardized cohesion effect is:

\[
C_g = \frac{\text{observed within-group association} - E_0}{SD_0},
\]

where \(E_0\) and \(SD_0\) come from a matched null preserving individual trajectories or habitat use while disrupting partner identity.

### 12.6 Group persistence and turnover

For inferred group \(g_t\) and its best-matching successor \(g_{t+1}\), report:

\[
J(g_t,g_{t+1})=\frac{|g_t\cap g_{t+1}|}{|g_t\cup g_{t+1}|}.
\]

Because births and deaths make raw Jaccard scores harsh, also report:

- retained-survivor fraction;
- member entry and exit hazards;
- group half-life;
- lineage continuity;
- split/fusion events;
- persistence of network core versus periphery;
- adjusted mutual information across whole partitions;
- survival curves for inferred communities.

A group is not persistent merely because a location remains occupied by unrelated replacements. That is **site continuity**; it may become culturally important but is analytically distinct from social continuity.

### 12.7 Assortative interaction

For any trait \(x\)—genetic relatedness, phenotype cue, behavior, age, lineage, signal convention—measure dyadic assortment on the opportunity-normalized network. Options include:

- weighted Newman assortativity;
- dyadic regression or relational-event hazard with `similarity(x)` predictor;
- permutation preserving degree, location, and activity;
- comparison of observed recipient choice against the set of currently available partners.

Report both **encounter assortment** and **action assortment**. Kin may encounter one another often due to limited dispersal but receive no preferential help once opportunity is controlled.

### 12.8 Relatedness within groups

Compute at least two relatedness concepts:

- pedigree coefficient or genealogical category;
- realized genomic similarity or identity-by-descent estimate.

For each inferred group, compare mean within-group relatedness with:

- the whole population;
- spatially matched random sets;
- same-age cohorts;
- organisms sharing the same natal region;
- cue-matched but unrelated organisms.

This identifies whether “kin groups” are actually family clusters, place cohorts, or cue-based assortments.

### 12.9 Cooperation rate and causal cost-benefit accounting

A behavior is cooperative only if there is evidence of recipient/joint benefit and actor cost or mutual gain. For event \(e\), estimate:

\[
\Delta_a(e)=Y_a^{\text{observed}}-Y_a^{\text{counterfactual no action}},
\]

\[
\Delta_r(e)=Y_r^{\text{observed}}-Y_r^{\text{counterfactual no action}},
\]

where \(Y\) may be energy, injury risk, resource acquisition, survival probability, offspring success, or task completion. Counterfactuals can be estimated by:

- exact branched replay from a checkpoint with the action suppressed;
- matched events with similar context;
- randomized action availability treatment;
- mechanistic accounting where costs and benefits are physically explicit.

Classify cautiously:

- `actor +, recipient +`: mutualism;
- `actor 0/+, recipient +`: by-product or mutual benefit;
- `actor -, recipient +`: candidate altruism;
- `actor -, recipient -`: conflict or error;
- delayed effects: retain an uncertainty label until a horizon is specified.

### 12.10 Resource sharing

Report:

- amount transferred per opportunity;
- transfer direction and latency;
- donor surplus and recipient need;
- intentional handoff probability;
- tolerated-taking duration before resistance;
- sharing with kin, familiar partners, mates, offspring, and strangers;
- future repayment or defense received;
- donor and recipient lifetime outcomes;
- resources lost to spillage or abandonment.

Use a transfer-intent confidence score based on placement, orientation, release timing, recipient reach, and repeatability. Do not force ambiguous cases into “sharing.”

### 12.11 Reciprocity

Direct reciprocity is a temporal dyadic effect. Estimate whether help from \(i\) to \(j\) increases the future hazard of help from \(j\) to \(i\), controlling for:

- future encounter opportunities;
- kinship and familiarity;
- shared resource patch;
- individual cooperativeness;
- recipient need and donor surplus;
- prior aggression;
- season and age.

Use lagged relational-event or survival models and compare against:

- identity-permuted histories;
- time-reversed histories;
- social-memory ablation;
- cue-class memory in place of individual memory.

Symmetric exchange can arise from similar needs or mutualism; contingency on prior partner action is the key.

### 12.12 Partner choice and cheater exclusion

Measure:

- probability of repeat interaction conditional on partner payoff;
- leaving or refusal hazard after cheating;
- search time and outside-option availability;
- network centrality loss after defection;
- cheater reproductive success and invasion trajectory;
- time for cooperation to recover after cheater introduction.

Partner-choice evidence strengthens if cheaters lose access without an increase in direct harm and if disabling partner switching restores cheater success.

### 12.13 Punishment and policing

A candidate punishment event is a costly action against a defector after the immediate opportunity for self-defense has passed. Report:

- trigger behavior;
- punisher relationship to victim and beneficiary;
- punisher cost and target cost;
- latency;
- whether target cooperation changes later;
- retaliation and escalation;
- third-party benefits;
- second-order free-rider patterns.

Policing evidence requires reduction in conflict or cheating that benefits a wider interaction system. Dominant coercion must be reported separately.

### 12.14 Territory stability

Infer utilization distributions or occupancy surfaces for individuals and dynamic communities. For each window, report:

- home-range area;
- core-area area;
- overlap with neighbors;
- center displacement;
- occupancy exclusivity;
- marker density and age;
- resource/structure value;
- intrusion opportunities and responses;
- boundary uncertainty.

Territory stability can be measured as spatial overlap between consecutive core-use or defended-use surfaces, corrected for seasonal resource movement. A region is “defended use” only when intrusion predicts costly resident response or reliable advertisement/avoidance effects.

### 12.15 Boundary defense

Define an intrusion relative to a resident organism's or inferred community's prior high-use surface, not a hard world border. Measure:

- detection-to-response latency;
- number and identity of responders;
- approach, block, chase, signal, and attack rates;
- response intensity as a function of depth into the core;
- resident and intruder numerical balance;
- marker exposure;
- resource/structure value;
- outcome and future intrusion hazard.

Compare with matched encounters in neutral overlap zones. Boundary-specific defense should be stronger or differently structured than generic stranger aggression.

### 12.16 Coalition size and composition

For every coalition candidate, record:

- participant count and arrival order;
- prior social ties among participants;
- relatedness;
- target count and identity;
- local numerical ratio over time;
- positions, trajectories, and roles;
- signals observed/emitted;
- injuries, withdrawals, and gains;
- participant reuse across episodes;
- nonparticipants who had an opportunity to join.

Report coalition-size distribution with exposure denominators and distinguish opportunistic pile-ons from recruited or coordinated actions.

### 12.17 Attack coordination

No single coordination statistic is sufficient. Use a composite evidence panel:

1. **Temporal synchrony:** attack or approach times are closer than in within-episode time-shuffled nulls.
2. **Conditional recruitment:** one attack increases a familiar observer's join hazard after controlling for target salience.
3. **Target concordance:** attackers select the same or complementary target more often than independent-choice models predict.
4. **Trajectory coupling:** approach vectors change conditional on partner positions.
5. **Role complementarity:** blocking, pursuit, signaling, and striking co-occur in combinations associated with better outcomes.
6. **Synergy:** coalition success exceeds the prediction from participant abilities acting independently.
7. **Counterfactual dependence:** removing or muting one participant in branched replay changes others' decisions or the outcome.

A simple standardized synchronization score is:

\[
Z_{sync}=\frac{S_{obs}-\mu(S_{null})}{\sigma(S_{null})},
\]

but the exact synchrony function and null must be specified before viewing results.

### 12.18 Inter-group versus intra-group aggression

Because group labels are inferred, report aggression under multiple partitions and resolution parameters. Compute:

\[
R_{between}=\frac{A_{between}/O_{between}}{A_{within}/O_{within}},
\]

where \(A\) is attack count or harm and \(O\) is encounter opportunity. Also report absolute rates; a high ratio can result from almost no within-group contact.

Sensitivity checks:

- exclude the interactions used to infer groups, then test aggression on held-out windows;
- infer groups only from affiliative layers, not aggression;
- control for spatial range overlap;
- compare individual-level latent-space models with hard partitions.

### 12.19 Conflict recurrence

For each inferred community pair, define conflict episodes using pre-registered spatial and temporal gaps. Report:

- episode rate per encounter opportunity;
- inter-event interval distribution;
- opponent-pair recurrence;
- participant reuse;
- retaliation hazard after losses;
- boundary or resource-state persistence;
- conflict duration and escalation;
- cessation following deterrent outcomes.

Compare against renewal-process, Poisson/overdispersed, and self-exciting models. A clustered event process is not necessarily revenge; repeated resource renewal can produce the same pattern.

### 12.20 Casualties and injury burden

Report:

- injuries and deaths per organism-time;
- injuries per conflict opportunity and per participant;
- attacker versus defender burden;
- severity, recovery time, and disability;
- age/sex/morphology/lineage distribution;
- direct and delayed mortality;
- reproductive loss;
- population extinction and bottleneck probability.

Avoid using only deaths. High injury cost can drive deterrence even when killings are rare.

### 12.21 Resource transfer following conflict

Track physical consequences for a fixed post-conflict horizon:

- objects or food changing possession;
- cache access;
- structure occupancy;
- patch use and yield;
- marker replacement;
- displacement distance and duration;
- destruction and repair;
- reproductive-site access.

Classify capture, exclusion, denial, and destruction separately. An attack that creates no persistent material or spatial benefit should not be assumed to be resource-motivated.

### 12.22 Signal conventions

For each channel or signal pattern, estimate:

- context-conditioned production probability;
- receiver response distribution;
- mutual information `signal ↔ context` and `signal ↔ response`;
- emitter/receiver identity effects;
- local-community divergence;
- temporal stability;
- transmission along social-network edges;
- deception and eavesdropping effects.

Playback or branched replay is essential: inject the signal without the original context and test whether receivers respond. Permute signal channels to test arbitrary convention dependence.

### 12.23 Group-specific traditions

A candidate tradition must satisfy:

1. at least two behavior variants are possible under comparable ecology;
2. one variant is locally predominant in an inferred community;
3. the pattern persists across a pre-specified duration and partial member turnover;
4. adoption follows observation or social-network exposure more strongly than individual exposure alone;
5. genetics, morphology, resource distribution, and site affordances do not fully explain the difference;
6. social-learning ablation reduces diffusion or persistence.

For conflict-related traditions, variants might include:

- recruitment signal pattern;
- patrol route;
- attack onset threshold;
- flanking versus direct approach;
- object/tool choice;
- restraint or treatment of retreating opponents;
- marker style;
- post-conflict resource handling.

Use neutral terms such as “variant A” until causal evidence supports a cultural label.

### 12.24 Distinguishing organization from common-stimulus response

Use at least five nulls:

1. **Common-stimulus model:** each organism responds independently to the same target/resource/hazard based on its state and distance.
2. **Time-shuffle null:** preserve each organism's action count and episode context but shuffle action times within valid opportunity windows.
3. **Identity-shuffle null:** preserve trajectories and environmental exposure but permute organism identity or social tie labels.
4. **Trajectory-rotation or phase-shift null:** preserve movement autocorrelation and habitat use while disrupting co-movement.
5. **Branched causal replay:** suppress one signal, participant, memory record, marker, or attack and replay from an identical checkpoint with keyed RNG streams held constant where causally valid.

Evidence for organization strengthens when observed coordination exceeds all relevant nulls and targeted ablations remove the effect in the predicted way.

### 12.25 Observer uncertainty

Every high-level metric should include:

- confidence interval or seed distribution;
- algorithm/version identifier;
- exposure denominator;
- sensitivity to time window, distance threshold, and community resolution;
- fraction of events left unclassified;
- alternative plausible interpretations;
- whether the metric is descriptive, predictive, or causal.

The observer should be allowed to say “ambiguous” rather than force a faction, territory, or war label.

## 13. Dependency-ordered experimental program

### 13.1 Program-wide rules

The stages below are **gates**, not a feature roadmap that assumes success. A later stage should not be interpreted if its prerequisite stage lacks a robust causal result. A negative result is scientifically useful and should trigger mechanism review rather than relabeling weak behavior.

#### Seed policy

Use a two-step design for every treatment cell:

1. **Pilot:** 20 paired seeds to estimate variance, event rarity, extinction rate, and runtime.
2. **Confirmatory ensemble:** choose the sample by simulation-based power analysis or a pre-specified information target. The counts below are starting minima, not universal laws.

Recommended starting confirmatory counts:

| Stages | Paired development seeds per cell | Untouched holdout seeds per cell | Reason |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1–3 | 40 | 20 | Common continuous outcomes |
| 4–8 | 60 | 30 | Evolutionary response and moderate event rarity |
| 9 | 100 | 50 | Aggression may be sparse and extinction-prone |
| 10 | 150 | 75 | Coalitionary events are rare and null-heavy |
| 11 | 250 | 100 | Recurrent conflict requires community-pair exposure |
| 12 | 300 | 150 | Cultural persistence requires long horizons and turnover |

If compute is constrained, reduce factorial breadth before reducing independent seeds below what rare-event uncertainty permits. Use sequential stopping only with pre-registered criteria based on exposure or precision, never because a desired behavior appeared.

#### Pairing and RNG

Treatments and ablations should share a base seed manifest and named stream keys. Common random numbers reduce variance, but a causal intervention will eventually alter downstream events; do not force streams to remain synchronized through traversal-dependent consumption. Counter-based or keyed draws are preferred.

#### Gate standard

A stage passes only when:

- the primary effect is specified before confirmatory runs;
- treatment-level uncertainty excludes the practical null, not merely zero at one tick;
- a substantial, pre-specified fraction of seeds exhibits the phenomenon or the event-rate difference is precise;
- at least one targeted ablation has the predicted selective effect;
- matched null models do not explain the result;
- the effect replicates in untouched holdout seeds;
- extinction, density, and opportunity differences do not account for it.

The exact practical thresholds should be calibrated in Stage 0 pilots and then frozen.

### 13.2 Stage 1 — Spatial aggregation

**Question.** Can local embodied rules and ecology produce cohesive or temporary groups without identity, kin cues, social memory, signals, structures, or damage?

**Enabled mechanisms.** M1 local perception, M2 resource topology, M3 social-motion response. Reproduction can be disabled for controller validation, then enabled for evolutionary tests.

**Primary treatment matrix.**

- social-body perception: absent / position only / position plus velocity;
- resource distribution: uniform / diffuse stochastic / concentrated patches;
- density: low / medium / high;
- environmental motion cue: stationary / moving resource or hazard gradient;
- controller source: hand-coded local-rule benchmark / evolved controller.

**Primary measurements.**

- largest connected-component fraction;
- within-component pairwise distance;
- velocity polarization and milling index;
- co-presence bout duration;
- split/fusion frequency;
- group movement accuracy toward a gradient;
- persistence after a resource patch is removed or moved.

**Success criterion.** Social-perception treatments must produce cohesion or collective motion above resource- and trajectory-matched nulls, replicated across holdout seeds. At least one effect must survive outside immediate resource radii. A suggested screening criterion is a median standardized cohesion effect greater than 2 null standard deviations and at least 70% of seeds above the null median; final thresholds should be pilot-calibrated.

**Controls.**

- non-social moving decoys with identical size and motion statistics;
- resource-only attraction with social inputs masked;
- periodic boundaries versus walled arena;
- fixed density with altered map area;
- randomized neighbor velocity while preserving neighbor position.

**Ablations.** Remove alignment input; remove attraction; remove repulsion; mask social bodies after groups form; rotate neighbor velocity vectors.

**Minimum seed requirement.** Start with 40 paired confirmatory seeds per cell plus 20 holdout; increase if group-state transitions are bimodal.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if all apparent groups vanish after controlling for patch attraction, wall effects, and density, or if the effect exists only under one arbitrary update order.

**Likely confounds.** Boundary trapping, collision resolution, resource bottlenecks, density-dependent sensor saturation, controller exploitation of entity ordering, and transient initialization clusters.

### 13.3 Stage 2 — Stable affiliative groups

**Question.** Can repeated, partner-specific association persist beyond shared resource attraction?

**Enabled mechanisms.** Stage 1 plus M5 familiarity memory. Keep kin cues, explicit signals, structures, and damage disabled. Initialize founders to be approximately unrelated and spatially mixed where feasible.

**Primary treatment matrix.**

- familiarity memory: absent / cue-class value / individual fingerprint;
- memory capacity: small / medium;
- decay: fast / slow;
- resource patches: static / periodically relocated;
- forced mixing pulse: absent / one brief relocation event.

**Primary measurements.**

- opportunity-normalized dyadic association;
- dynamic-community persistence;
- nearest-neighbor identity retention;
- co-movement away from resources;
- reunion after temporary separation;
- group fission/fusion and member turnover;
- site continuity versus social continuity.

**Success criterion.** Inferred communities must persist across multiple ecological cycles and show partner-specific association in neutral space. Patch relocation should not fully erase the social network, and individual-memory ablation should selectively reduce persistence.

**Controls.**

- site labels follow the organisms while individuals are permuted;
- memory table stores places but not bodies;
- cue-class memory without individual recognition;
- non-social repeated exposure to moving decoys;
- identical encounter schedules with partner IDs permuted.

**Ablations.** Periodically reset familiarity; cap memory to one slot; scramble fingerprints while preserving phenotype distribution; remove learned attraction but retain memory storage.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 60 paired seeds per cell plus 30 holdout if group persistence is heavy-tailed; 40+20 may suffice after pilot if effects are continuous and common.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if “groups” are only whoever currently occupies a patch, if communities cannot be distinguished from movement autocorrelation, or if individual-memory removal has no predicted effect.

**Likely confounds.** Site fidelity mislabeled as affiliation, shared circadian schedule, age cohorting, same-resource specialization, community-detection resolution, and memory fingerprint collisions.

### 13.4 Stage 3 — Kin-biased grouping

**Question.** Can kin-correlated groups arise through dispersal, early association, and perceptible phenotype rather than direct pedigree access?

**Enabled mechanisms.** Stage 2 plus M4 heritable cues, birth/natal association, and controlled dispersal. No controller input exposes relatedness.

**Core factorial design.** Separate three variables:

1. **Actual relatedness structure:** random mixing / limited natal dispersal.
2. **Cue-pedigree correlation:** intact / permuted among newborns.
3. **Early familiarity:** offspring remain near caregivers / cross-fostered or spatially reassigned.

This creates treatments where actual kinship, cue similarity, and familiarity agree or conflict.

**Primary measurements.**

- pedigree and genomic relatedness within inferred groups;
- partner choice by relatedness after encounter control;
- cue similarity assortment;
- parent-offspring and sibling association;
- adoption of unrelated familiar individuals;
- group persistence after maturation and migration.

**Success criterion.** At least one embodied pathway must create kin-biased association above natal-location and encounter nulls. The causal signature must match the manipulation: cue permutation should remove phenotype-matching effects; cross-fostering should redirect familiarity-based effects; limited dispersal effects may remain without recognition.

**Controls.**

- observer-only pedigree labels removed from all runtime structures;
- identical cue distributions with pedigree randomized;
- identical pedigree with cues randomized;
- self-template versus caregiver-template learning;
- no-social-memory treatment with limited dispersal.

**Ablations.** Remove cue perception; disable early imprinting; increase dispersal; recombine cue and response loci more strongly; add false-beard mutants.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 60 paired seeds per cell plus 30 holdout because interactions among three factors can be substantial.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if kin assortment disappears after controlling for natal location and encounter opportunity, or if it depends on a hidden direct-relatedness query.

**Likely confounds.** Maternal inheritance, shared microbiome-like state if modeled, same-age cohorts, spatial inheritance, phenotype effects on habitat preference, and observer kinship estimation depth.

### 13.5 Stage 4 — Cooperation

**Question.** Can organisms evolve or learn actions with measurable joint or recipient benefit under real costs?

**Enabled mechanisms.** Stages 1–3 as selected; M6 physical transfer primitives and one or more general joint-action affordances. Social signals, damage, and social learning remain off unless a specific comparison requires them.

**Task families.** Use at least two to avoid overfitting to one morphology:

- **Immediate mutualism:** two aligned forces move or open an object faster than either alone.
- **Delayed shared benefit:** maintaining a shelter/path/processing site costs an actor but later benefits nearby users.
- **Guarding/vigilance analogue:** one organism forgoes immediate acquisition while reducing a local hazard.

No task should expose a “cooperation state” input.

**Primary measurements.**

- actor costs and recipient/joint benefits from branched replay;
- joint-task completion and synergy;
- participant assortment;
- individual and lineage fitness effects;
- stability under mutation;
- within- versus between-community covariance in cooperative trait change.

**Success criterion.** A costly action must causally improve recipient or joint outcome, occur above mechanistic baseline, and be maintained by evolution or learning across holdout seeds. The analysis must distinguish mutualism, by-product benefit, and candidate altruism.

**Controls.**

- same action animation with recipient benefit removed;
- benefit delivered to a random distant organism;
- solo-solvable version with matched energy cost;
- forced co-location without partner choice;
- no-relatedness and high-relatedness treatments.

**Ablations.** Remove partner memory; increase dispersal; remove joint nonlinearity; eliminate future interaction; remove structure persistence for public-good tasks.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 60 paired seeds per cell plus 30 holdout; use more if cooperative variants appear only after long evolutionary waiting times.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if the action has no actor cost, no recipient benefit under counterfactual replay, or only appears because fitness is assigned at a team level.

**Likely confounds.** Shared stimulus, mechanical hitchhiking, hidden reward shaping, unequal opportunity, controller exploiting task reset, and indirect self-benefit mislabeled as altruism.

### 13.6 Stage 5 — Cheater resistance

**Question.** Can cooperation persist or recover when variants exploit benefits while withholding costs?

**Enabled mechanisms.** A validated Stage 4 cooperation ecology plus selected stabilizers tested separately: kin assortment, partner choice, social memory, structure access, or punishment-capable actions. Do not activate all stabilizers simultaneously at first.

**Cheater construction.** Introduce standardized variants at pre-registered frequencies, for example 5% and 20%, that retain benefit collection but have a reduced contribution probability. Use both:

- **engineered diagnostic cheaters** for causal testing;
- **naturally evolved low-contribution lineages** for ecological validity.

**Primary measurements.**

- cheater frequency and reproductive success;
- cooperation rate per opportunity;
- network exclusion and partner loss;
- punishment/retaliation costs;
- group productivity and extinction;
- recovery time after invasion;
- migration and fusion sensitivity.

**Success criterion.** At least one mechanism must prevent a cheater sweep or restore cooperation without an authored anti-cheater rule. Its predicted signature must appear—for example, partner loss under partner choice or reduced aid after memory-based defection.

**Controls.**

- neutral migrant with same cue novelty but cooperative behavior;
- low-contribution mutant whose lower output comes from injury or low energy rather than strategy;
- forced-interaction treatment preventing exit;
- full anonymity versus individual recognition;
- identical group productivity with benefits randomized.

**Ablations.** Disable the hypothesized stabilizer one at a time; reset memory; increase mixing; remove access exclusion; prevent harm after defection; decouple pleiotropic functions.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 80 paired seeds per cell plus 40 holdout is preferable for invasion dynamics; 60+30 is a lower screening bound.

**Falsification condition.** Resistance is not established if cheaters fail only because of an unrelated viability defect, if cooperation persists solely because benefits are inaccessible to cheaters by authored permissions, or if extinction removes both strategies.

**Likely confounds.** Mutation load, cue novelty, founder effects, demographic bottlenecks, cheater inability to reach benefits, hidden kin structure, and insufficient invasion duration.

### 13.7 Stage 6 — Resource sharing

**Question.** Can intentional transfer, tolerated access, or exchange emerge from physical possession and asymmetric need?

**Enabled mechanisms.** M6 full carrying/drop/transfer, bounded social memory as a treatment, and congregation opportunities. Shared structures may be enabled in a separate branch. Damage remains disabled to prevent coercion from obscuring early sharing.

**Primary treatment matrix.**

- resource divisibility: indivisible / divisible;
- spoilage: fast / slow;
- acquisition variance: low / high;
- carrying capacity: low / high;
- recipient need visibility: hidden / visible;
- congregation/downtime site: absent / present;
- social memory: absent / present.

**Primary measurements.**

- directed transfer per opportunity;
- donor surplus and recipient need;
- tolerated taking versus active handoff;
- reciprocal lag effects;
- kin/familiarity bias;
- donor and recipient fitness consequences;
- emergence of stable exchange locations or sequences.

**Success criterion.** Physical resource flow to others must exceed accidental drop and proximity-matched baselines. At least one of intentional placement, tolerated access, need-sensitive provisioning, or reciprocal exchange must survive holdout tests and have a coherent causal mechanism.

**Controls.**

- invisible recipient need;
- nonportable resources;
- drops teleported to a random nearby location;
- recipient replaced by a non-social collector;
- same congregation geometry without resource asymmetry.

**Ablations.** Remove release action; remove social memory; remove congregation time; equalize acquisition; scramble recipient identity; make resources immediately spoil after release.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 80 paired seeds per cell plus 40 holdout if transfers are rare; 60+30 if pilot event rates are high.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if apparent sharing is entirely spillage, theft without tolerance, offspring feeding hard-coded by role, or a side effect of inventory overflow.

**Likely confounds.** Drop mechanics, collision at consumption sites, recipient stealing before donor can recover an item, inventory caps, kin co-location, and observer intent classification.


### 13.8 Stage 7 — Territorial occupancy

**Question.** Can organisms or inferred communities develop stable, value-dependent space use without an explicit territory state?

**Enabled mechanisms.** Validated social baseline plus M10 spatial memory. Use concentrated renewable resources, shelters, caches, or structures as independent treatments. Begin without damage; allow occupation, blocking, avoidance, and markers only in separate cells.

**Core factorial design.**

- total supply: low / high;
- concentration: diffuse / patchy;
- renewal: ephemeral / persistent;
- spatial memory: absent / present;
- persistent structure/cache: absent / present;
- marker field: absent / present;
- map geometry: open / bottlenecked.

**Primary measurements.**

- individual and community utilization distributions;
- site fidelity and revisit intervals;
- core-area stability;
- occupancy exclusivity and overlap;
- movement response to neighbor high-use areas;
- resident advantage without damage;
- relation between site value and occupancy investment;
- site continuity after member turnover.

**Success criterion.** Stable core-use areas must exceed resource-only movement nulls and show learned place dependence. The observer should recover graded spatial partitioning or persistent site fidelity without any owner field. Spatial-memory ablation should selectively reduce stability or resident effects.

**Controls.**

- resources moved while place coordinates remain;
- mirrored landscapes with organisms swapped;
- spatial memory retained for food but not social outcomes;
- uniform resources with identical abundance;
- observer territory inference applied to nonsocial foragers.

**Ablations.** Reset place memory; flatten resource concentration; remove structures; increase patch relocation; disable markers; randomize natal site.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 60 paired seeds per cell plus 30 holdout; use longer runs rather than only more seeds if occupancy stability requires ecological cycles.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if inferred “territories” are simply Voronoi partitions around current resources, disappear whenever resources shift by one cell, or depend on a hidden owner/region label.

**Likely confounds.** Resource autocorrelation, map edges, reproduction near parents, locomotion cost gradients, home range mistaken for territory, and analysis bandwidth.

### 13.9 Stage 8 — Boundary defense

**Question.** Does intrusion into a repeatedly used area trigger location-specific, costly response or advertisement?

**Enabled mechanisms.** Stage 7 plus nonlethal blocking/chasing, M9 markers as a treatment, and M8 local signals as a separate treatment. Damage remains off in the primary branch; a low-damage comparison may follow.

**Intrusion protocol.** Use both natural incursions and controlled diagnostic perturbations. In a diagnostic perturbation, introduce or redirect a matched unfamiliar organism into a resident core, overlap zone, or neutral area while preserving the resident's local resource state as closely as possible.

**Primary measurements.**

- detection-to-response latency;
- approach, block, chase, marker, and signal rates;
- response versus intrusion depth;
- number of responders and coalition tendency;
- energetic/opportunity cost of defense;
- intruder retreat and future avoidance;
- boundary displacement after outcomes;
- marker-mediated response when residents are absent.

**Success criterion.** Response must be stronger or structurally different in resident cores/boundaries than in matched neutral encounters and must predict altered access or future intrusion. Removing spatial memory or markers should have mechanism-specific effects.

**Controls.**

- familiar neighbor versus unfamiliar intruder;
- resident core versus identical-value unoccupied patch;
- resource item moved to a neutral site;
- non-social moving object intrusion;
- sham marker with equal sensory salience;
- current occupant versus long-term resident swap.

**Ablations.** Remove markers; remove spatial social memory; prevent blocking/chasing; mask intruder cue novelty; relocate the resource; remove signals.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 80 paired seeds per cell plus 40 holdout if controlled incursions are sparse; 60+30 can screen continuous response effects.

**Falsification condition.** Boundary defense is falsified if response depends only on immediate resource proximity, is identical in neutral space, or persists when resident place memory and occupancy history are removed.

**Likely confounds.** Stranger aggression, novelty response, resource defense at any location, intruder condition, unequal local numbers, and markers that directly encode depositor identity.

### 13.10 Stage 9 — Individual aggression

**Question.** Under what ecological and social conditions does target-directed conspecific aggression emerge after damage becomes possible?

**Enabled mechanisms.** Validated Stage 1–8 substrate plus M11 damage. Coalition recruitment is deliberately limited: signals may be off, and analysis initially focuses on one-on-one attacks.

**Core factorial design.** Independently vary:

- scarcity: low / high;
- concentration: diffuse / concentrated;
- renewability: low / high;
- capturability: no portable gain / portable gain;
- injury severity: low / moderate / high;
- recovery: fast / slow;
- kin/familiarity recognition: absent / present;
- population density: low / medium / high.

**Primary measurements.**

- target-directed attack rate per opportunity;
- attack contexts and targets;
- injury and energetic cost;
- resource/space consequences;
- retaliation and avoidance;
- dominance asymmetry;
- aggression toward kin, familiar partners, strangers, and inferred community members;
- population persistence and extinction.

**Success criterion.** At least one treatment produces reproducible target-directed aggression that is adaptive or maintained, with context-specific consequences. The analysis must separate predation, immediate defense, dyadic resource fights, and indiscriminate attack.

**Controls.**

- threat/display action with no damage;
- attack cost but zero target harm;
- damage with no resource or spatial gain;
- concentrated abundant resources versus diffuse scarce resources;
- collision-matched non-targeted impacts;
- heterospecific prey if predation exists, analyzed separately.

**Ablations.** Remove damage; remove capturable value; raise injury cost; hide target state; remove social memory; eliminate pursuit; equalize strength.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 100 paired seeds per cell plus 50 holdout, with an event-exposure target such as a pre-registered number of valid conspecific encounter opportunities.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if harm is accidental, if aggression only appears under near-zero cost with no benefit, if it causes universal extinction before selection can act, or if all attacks are predatory feeding behavior.

**Likely confounds.** Physics impulses counted as attacks, resource crowding, starvation-induced motor noise, attack action dominating because of controller scaling, and survivors appearing “successful” only after demographic collapse.

### 13.11 Stage 10 — Coalitionary aggression

**Question.** Can two or more organisms coordinate harmful action against a social target beyond independent response to the same stimulus?

**Prerequisites.** Stable affiliative communities from Stage 2, target-directed aggression from Stage 9, and enough perception to observe partners' trajectories and attacks. Use at least three sustainable inferred communities to avoid a forced binary world.

**Treatment branches.**

1. action observation only;
2. action observation plus familiarity;
3. neutral local signals added;
4. spatial memory and contested high-value sites;
5. shared structures/caches;
6. kin-biased versus nonkin stable groups;
7. low versus high injury cost;
8. numerical asymmetry visible versus partially occluded.

**Primary measurements.**

- coalition candidate rate per multi-organism opportunity;
- temporal synchrony and conditional join hazard;
- target concordance;
- trajectory coupling and role complementarity;
- local numerical ratio over time;
- participant social ties and relatedness;
- success synergy over independent-ability model;
- nonparticipant opportunity and later treatment;
- signals, initiation, and influence structure.

**Success criterion.** A treatment must produce repeated multi-attacker episodes that exceed common-stimulus, time-shuffle, identity-shuffle, and independent-hazard nulls. At least one branched replay or targeted ablation must show that a partner's action or signal causally changes another participant's decision or outcome.

A strong screening gate is:

- coordination score above the 99th percentile of matched nulls at the treatment level;
- positive conditional recruitment effect in held-out episodes;
- synergy or causal dependence in at least one outcome dimension;
- replication in holdout seeds.

Do not require every seed to evolve coalitions; report emergence probability and waiting-time distribution.

**Controls.**

- multiple organisms independently exposed to one weak target;
- target salience replay with partner actions hidden;
- familiar partners replaced by perceptually matched strangers;
- signal playback without target;
- equal local numbers but different global community sizes;
- damage disabled while attack gestures remain visible.

**Ablations.** Mute signals; hide partner attack cues; reset social memory; remove contested value; randomize partner positions; suppress the first initiator in branched replay; prevent approach following while retaining target perception.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 150 paired seeds per cell plus 75 untouched holdout, or an exposure-based design yielding a pre-specified number of valid multi-party encounter episodes per treatment.

**Falsification condition.** Coalitionary aggression is falsified if simultaneous attacks are reproduced by independent target-response models, if partner removal does not change decisions or outcomes, or if coalition membership is simply an engine-assigned group.

**Likely confounds.** Target weakness, crowding, simultaneous resource arrival, attack cooldown synchronization, shared sensory gradients, global clock cues, group inference using aggression itself, and repeated clones with identical deterministic policies.

### 13.12 Stage 11 — Repeated inter-group conflict

**Question.** Can persistent inferred communities repeatedly engage in coordinated hostile episodes with opponent-specific, spatial, material, or demographic consequences?

**Prerequisites.** Multiple persistent groups, coalitionary coordination, sustainable demography, spatial memory, and contested value. Social signals and markers are treatments, not mandatory assumptions.

**World requirements.** Provide several nonidentical but substitutable high-value sites so communities can avoid, compete, shift, and capture rather than being forced into a single arena. Include refuges and recovery opportunities. Run long enough for births, deaths, migration, fission, and boundary movement.

**Primary treatment matrix.**

- scarcity × concentration × renewability;
- portable loot versus immobile resource access;
- structures/caches absent versus present;
- memory short versus long;
- markers absent versus present;
- signals absent versus present;
- migration low versus moderate;
- injury/death cost moderate versus high;
- post-conflict resource regeneration fast versus slow.

**Primary measurements.**

- persistent social partitions inferred without aggression edges;
- between- versus within-community aggression per opportunity;
- coalition coordination and participant reuse;
- community-pair recurrence and inter-event intervals;
- raid and defense candidates;
- boundary and utilization shifts;
- resource capture, denial, destruction, and repair;
- injury, mortality, reproduction, migration, fission, and extinction;
- retaliation and deterrence hazards;
- nonparticipation and free-riding outcomes.

**Success criterion.** All six operational conditions in Section 9.11 must hold: persistent partitions, directed hostility, coalitionary coordination, recurrence, cross-episode state, and consequences. The result must survive alternative community algorithms and held-out windows.

**Controls.**

- same resource schedule with community identities permuted offline;
- one shared hotspot versus multiple substitutable sites;
- repeated patch renewal with damage disabled;
- damage enabled but spatial memory reset between encounters;
- stable groups with no capturable/defendable value;
- explicit group-ID treatment as a positive-control upper bound, clearly segregated from mainline results.

**Ablations.** Remove cross-episode social memory; relocate contested resources; mute recruitment signals; disable markers; prevent resource capture while retaining harm; remove structures; increase migration; lower or raise injury cost; break coalition partner continuity.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 250 paired seeds per cell plus 100 holdout is a reasonable starting point, but rare-event power should be based on community-pair encounter exposure. Use multiple community pairs per run where independence assumptions are modeled correctly.

**Falsification condition.** The stage fails if conflicts are single-patch crowding events, communities are defined only from aggression, recurrence matches resource renewal with no opponent memory, or coordinated episodes have no persistent consequence.

**Likely confounds.** Pseudoreplication of many episodes from few seeds, extinction censoring, one dominant lineage, map geometry forcing encounters, global seasonal clocks, analysis labels bleeding into visualization-driven interpretation, and post hoc episode thresholds.

### 13.13 Stage 12 — Conflict-related cultural traditions

**Question.** Can a conflict-related behavioral variant spread socially, become community-specific, and persist across demographic turnover without being reducible to genotype or ecology?

**Prerequisites.** At least two viable conflict or defense variants, validated social learning M12, persistent communities, and sufficient observation. The project should first demonstrate non-conflict traditions in a lower-risk domain to validate the cultural measurement pipeline.

**Candidate variants.**

- alternative recruitment signal sequences;
- direct versus flanking approach;
- different patrol routes to the same boundary;
- different thresholds for joining an attack;
- alternative object/tool use with comparable affordances;
- retreat-pursuit restraint conventions;
- marker placement patterns;
- post-conflict cache or loot handling.

The engine must not name or reward a variant. Variants arise from controller/action differences and are clustered offline.

**Treatment design.**

- social learning absent / present;
- local enhancement only / outcome copying / action copying;
- model bias none / success-biased / familiarity-biased / conformist;
- migration low / moderate;
- environmental affordances equalized across communities;
- cross-fostering or juvenile relocation;
- genetically mixed founders versus lineage-clustered founders;
- seeded demonstrator as diagnostic treatment versus unseeded emergence.

**Primary measurements.**

- variant frequency by community and time;
- adoption hazard after social exposure;
- network-based diffusion fit;
- persistence across births/deaths and model loss;
- genetic and ecological predictors;
- migrant convergence or retention;
- cross-foster resemblance to natal versus rearing community;
- effect of social-learning ablation;
- performance and conflict outcome consequences.

**Success criterion.** A behavior variant must be locally differentiated, persist through meaningful turnover, diffuse along social pathways, and resist explanation by genotype, morphology, local materials, resource geometry, and independent learning. Social-learning removal must reduce spread or persistence. A seeded-demonstrator treatment establishes capacity; an unseeded replicate establishes stronger emergence.

**Controls.**

- yoked individual learners with identical action outcomes but no demonstrations;
- ecologies swapped between communities;
- offspring or migrants raised in a different community;
- genetic lineages redistributed while preserving social network;
- model identity permuted;
- behavior-label classifier trained on one seed and tested blind on holdout seeds;
- nonfunctional arbitrary variant to test conformity separately from utility.

**Ablations.** Disable social updates; hide action details but show outcomes; remove conformity bias; remove successful models; increase migration; equalize signals; reset local marker fields; prevent juveniles from observing conflict.

**Minimum seed requirement.** 300 paired seeds per cell plus 150 holdout, or enough independent community lineages to avoid treating communities within one run as fully independent. Long-duration replication matters more than dense sampling of one world.

**Falsification condition.** Cultural interpretation is rejected if variants track genotype after cross-fostering, disappear when ecology is swapped, arise at the same rate without social observation, or persist only because one inherited controller lineage dominates.

**Likely confounds.** Genetic hitchhiking, material availability, spatial affordances, age structure, one influential founder, classifier drift, observer selection of visually striking variants, and social learning that merely increases generic arousal rather than transmitting a tactic.

## 14. Controls and ablations

A social result is useful only when the mechanism responsible for it can be isolated. Genesis should therefore treat controls and ablations as part of the feature design, not as later validation work. Every mechanism should be switchable through versioned configuration, and every switch should leave the rest of the world as unchanged as possible. For interventions that alter the number or timing of random draws, counter-based or keyed random generation is preferable so that unaffected entities retain comparable stochastic inputs.

### 14.1 General experimental-control hierarchy

Use four progressively stronger kinds of comparison:

1. **Descriptive baseline:** compare the observed metric with a preregistered threshold. This is the weakest evidence and should never establish organization by itself.
2. **Opportunity-matched null:** preserve who could have interacted, where, and when, while randomizing the hypothesized social dependency. Examples include temporal jitter, identity permutation within local encounter sets, or randomized action onsets conditional on exposure.
3. **Mechanism ablation:** disable the relevant cue, memory, action, cost, or world affordance while preserving other capacities.
4. **Branched counterfactual:** restore an identical checkpoint, change exactly one mechanism or local event, and compare divergence across an ensemble of paired branches.

The unit of replication is normally the independently seeded world, not the interaction, organism, episode, inferred group, or time window. Nested observations can improve precision, but they do not create independent evolutionary histories.

### 14.2 Required design comparisons

| Design variable | Minimal “without” condition | Minimal “with” condition | What the comparison can establish | Main confound or leakage risk | Preferred interpretation if no difference appears |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Explicit group IDs** | No identity or controller input derived from group classification | A persistent inherited or assigned ID visible to controllers | How much IDs simplify assortment, discrimination, and coordination | IDs create factions by construction, collapse recognition into one bit, and can silently encode observer labels | Prefer the no-ID design; failure implies missing ecology, recognition, memory, or coordination—not a need to add factions automatically |
| **Kin recognition** | Pedigree logged but unavailable to organisms | Relatedness estimated through evolvable cues, familiarity, or phenotype matching; never exact coefficient unless testing an artificial upper bound | Whether kin-biased assortment or aid requires recognizable correlates | A direct kin scalar is an oracle and can overstate the accessibility of inclusive-fitness strategies | Distinguish failure of recognition from absence of selection for kin bias using a temporary oracle benchmark |
| **Social memory** | Only current percepts and internal non-social state | Bounded memory of encountered individuals/cues, actions, outcomes, or familiarity | Requirements for partner choice, reciprocity, revenge, dominance, and differentiated relationships | Unique immutable identity tokens can make recognition unrealistically easy | Determine whether simple familiarity or location-conditioned memory is sufficient before adding identity-rich memory |
| **Signals** | Agents can observe ordinary movement and action consequences only | Costly, local, mutable signals with evolvable production and response | Whether recruitment, alarm, convention, or deceptive signaling adds causal coordination | Fixed semantic channels become commands or faction badges | A null result may mean signals lack payoff-relevant ambiguity, reach, reliability, or learnability |
| **Shared structures** | Resources and cover are transient; no persistent constructed object | Persistent, physically accessible structures that can be modified, occupied, or defended | Whether environmental inheritance stabilizes groups, cooperation, and site defense | Structure ownership flags or creator-only privileges script social claims | Infer association from use, investment, defense, and succession; do not store social ownership |
| **Damage** | Contact, blocking, displacement, stealing, and signaling but no injury | Directional costly attack with injury, recovery, death risk, and opportunity costs | Whether aggression and conflict require harmful capability | A cheap attack button can turn every competition into violence and obscure nonviolent strategies | If damage fails to produce organized conflict, the missing variables are probably coordination, asymmetry, value, memory, or recurrence |
| **Resource scarcity** | Carrying capacity comfortably exceeds demand over the relevant horizon | Lower total availability or higher demand, with all else held constant | Effect of total insufficiency | Scarcity often changes density, movement, mortality, and encounter rates simultaneously | Interpret through those mediators rather than asserting a direct scarcity-to-war law |
| **Resource concentration** | Same total resources dispersed broadly and weakly defendable | Same total resources concentrated into patches, caches, sites, or seasonal pulses | Effect of defendability, exclusion, and contest value independent of total scarcity | Concentration can create bottlenecks and collision artifacts | A stronger effect than scarcity supports resource-defense or contest-economics mechanisms rather than generic deprivation |
| **Territorial markers** | Memory and repeated occupancy only | Persistent local deposits, modifications, odors, marks, or signals with decay and production cost | Whether external memory improves boundary stability, deterrence, and convention | A marker interpreted as “owner team” scripts property and faction membership | Marker meaning must be learned or evolved from local consequences, and competitors must be able to imitate, overwrite, ignore, or exploit it |

### 14.3 Explicit group-ID upper-bound experiment

An explicit group identifier is not recommended for the production architecture, but a tightly isolated **upper-bound treatment** can be scientifically useful. Give organisms a one-dimensional arbitrary tag that is persistent for a limited experimental interval, visible locally, mutable, and not tied to observer-generated communities. Compare it with:

- no tag;
- a freely mutable tag;
- a costly tag;
- a noisy tag;
- a tag genetically linked to helping or aggression;
- a tag inherited independently of behavior;
- a perceptually rich phenotype from which similarity must be learned.

The upper-bound condition estimates how much of the difficulty comes from recognition. It must not be interpreted as evidence that natural groups would have emerged, and it must never be allowed to become a hidden permanent faction field.

### 14.4 Recognition factorial

A useful factorial separates **pedigree**, **familiarity**, **phenotypic similarity**, and **local association**:

| Treatment | Pedigree correlation | Familiarity correlation | Phenotype correlation | Location correlation | Diagnostic purpose |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Natural rearing | Yes | Yes | Usually yes | Usually yes | Ecological baseline, but causally ambiguous |
| Cross-fostering | No or reduced | Yes | No or reduced | Yes | Familiarity versus pedigree/phenotype |
| Familiar stranger introduction | No | No | Matched or mismatched | Controlled | Phenotype matching and novelty |
| Kin cue scrambling | Yes | Yes | Cue removed/noised | Yes | Reliance on the candidate cue |
| Pedigree reassignment control | Logged label permuted only | Unchanged | Unchanged | Unchanged | Observer-analysis sanity check; must not alter behavior |
| Spatial redistribution | Preserved | Preserved or controlled | Preserved | No | Site association versus relationship |

Because exact cross-fostering may not map cleanly onto asexual or continuously reproducing artificial organisms, the equivalent intervention can exchange newborn locations, caregivers, social neighborhoods, or cue expression while preserving genome and pedigree records.

### 14.5 Social-memory ladder

Rather than moving directly from no memory to a dictionary of named individuals, test a ladder of increasing representational power:

1. no social memory;
2. recent local encounter trace without identity;
3. familiarity strength keyed to perceptual signature;
4. last action and outcome for a bounded number of signatures;
5. decaying valence or trust estimate;
6. action-specific history;
7. third-party observation and reputation;
8. episodic sequence memory.

At each level, cap capacity, specify deterministic eviction, and test whether the next level produces a qualitatively new behavior. This identifies the minimum cognition needed and avoids smuggling a social database into the organism.

### 14.6 Signal controls

Signals require more than an on/off ablation. At minimum compare:

- **silent control:** production disabled;
- **unheard control:** signals emitted and paid for but not perceived;
- **playback control:** recorded local signals replayed without the original sender action;
- **identity permutation:** sender identity or phenotype reassigned within an opportunity-matched set;
- **semantic permutation:** signal channel remapped between sender and receiver at birth or checkpoint;
- **cheap versus costly production:** tests stability under deception;
- **private versus public reception:** separates dyadic coordination from common knowledge;
- **short versus persistent lifetime:** separates communication from environmental marking;
- **innate versus learned response:** tests convention formation;
- **novel migrant test:** asks whether an outsider learns or misinterprets a local convention.

A signal convention should be claimed only when arbitrary variation in signal form is associated with a stable, community-specific response mapping and when perturbing that mapping disrupts behavior.

### 14.7 Shared-structure controls

Persistent objects can cause social association without social attraction. Controls should include:

- duplicate, equally useful structures in unoccupied locations;
- structures that decay before social memory matters;
- structures that remain but cannot be modified;
- structures that can be modified but not defended;
- identical structures generated exogenously rather than constructed;
- creator identity hidden or permuted;
- access made non-excludable;
- structure relocation after groups form;
- occupants relocated while structure remains;
- material distribution swapped between sites.

A structure-mediated tradition requires more than repeated use. Demonstrate lineage-independent continuity of a modification or practice, social exposure effects, and persistence beyond the original builder.

### 14.8 Damage and aggression controls

Damage should be decomposed into distinct physical consequences:

- body blocking without injury;
- displacement or knockback;
- theft or possession transfer;
- temporary impairment;
- recoverable injury;
- permanent impairment;
- reproductive loss;
- lethal harm.

Keep action range, energy cost, execution delay, and collision mechanics matched where possible. This separates attraction to displacement or theft from selection for injury. Predation should have distinct event semantics from conspecific aggression even if both use the same physical strike primitive, because target consumption and competitive exclusion imply different payoffs.

A **sham attack** that has the same animation, delay, and energetic cost but causes no target harm can detect whether observers respond to visible action form or experienced consequence. An **unobserved damage** treatment can separate deterrent/reputational effects from direct incapacitation.

### 14.9 Scarcity-by-concentration factorial

Total resource abundance and spatial concentration should be crossed, not bundled:

| | Dispersed resources | Concentrated resources |
|---|---|---|
| **Abundant** | Low defendability, low deprivation | Valuable sites but enough alternatives; tests exclusion incentives without severe need |
| **Scarce** | High search pressure but weak monopolizability | High need and high defendability; strongest contest treatment but most confounded |

Additional orthogonal factors are renewability, predictability, transportability, cacheability, substitutability, and temporal pulsing. Equalize total expected energetic value when testing concentration. Measure the realized—not merely configured—distribution of access, travel time, and encounter rate.

### 14.10 Territorial-marker factorial

Cross marker **persistence**, **specificity**, **cost**, and **contestability**:

- persistence: transient / one episode / multiple lifetimes;
- specificity: generic occupancy trace / producer phenotype / individually distinctive;
- cost: free / energetic / exposure risk / material consumption;
- contestability: immutable / erasable / overwritable / forgeable;
- perceptibility: local only / line-of-sight / diffusible;
- semantics: no innate meaning / evolved response / learned association.

The least scripted viable condition is a generic, costly, decaying physical trace with no owner field and no innate “respect” response. Organisms may learn that traces predict occupants, retaliation, depleted resources, or safe passage.

### 14.11 Coordination null models

Coalition claims require multiple nulls because apparently synchronized action can emerge from shared exposure:

1. **Independent-hazard null:** each organism’s attack probability is conditioned on its own distance, visibility, energetic state, injury, target identity, local ally count, and resource context.
2. **Common-stimulus null:** preserve the exact intruder trajectory or resource event but sever observation among potential allies.
3. **Time-shift null:** shift one actor’s action train within periods of unchanged opportunity.
4. **Identity-permutation null:** permute actors among organisms with matching position and state.
5. **Recruitment-ablation null:** preserve alarm exposure but remove directional or target-specific information.
6. **Counterfactual ally removal:** replay from a checkpoint with one potential ally absent or unable to attack.

Evidence for coordination includes conditional temporal dependence, target convergence, role differentiation, recruitment response, and a joint outcome greater than predicted by these opportunity-matched models. Mere attack overlap is insufficient.

### 14.12 Cultural-transmission controls

To distinguish culture from genes, ecology, and independent learning, combine:

- cross-fostering or newborn relocation;
- migrant adoption tests;
- social-network diffusion analysis;
- demonstrator seeding;
- ghost or playback controls;
- ecology swaps;
- material and affordance equalization;
- social-learning ablation;
- genotype and morphology covariates;
- turnover sufficient to replace original performers;
- arbitrary alternative variants with equivalent utility;
- blind behavioral coding or preregistered classifiers.

A group-specific behavior should not be called a tradition merely because it clusters geographically. The strongest operational case is a stable variant that is acquired preferentially after social exposure, crosses genetic lineages, persists through turnover, and remains differentiated when environments are exchanged.

| Kin-correlated cues |  | ✓ |  |  |  |  |  |  |

The matrix deliberately rejects a single linear “social complexity ladder.” For example, mutualistic cooperation can occur without kin recognition, territorial occupancy can occur without damage, and coalitionary defense may arise before resource sharing.

### 14.14 Interaction ablations and non-additivity

Mechanisms can substitute for or amplify one another. Important two-way interactions include:

- kin cues × dispersal distance;
- familiarity memory × population turnover;
- social memory × repeated encounter probability;
- signaling × line-of-sight occlusion;
- signal cost × deception payoff;
- structures × environmental predictability;
- resource concentration × renewability;
- concentration × injury cost;
- damage × numerical advantage;
- marker persistence × spatial memory;
- social learning × migration;
- partner choice × punishment;
- coalition recruitment × attack commitment cost.

Ablating one mechanism in only one background can therefore give a misleading conclusion. The experiment plan should use sparse factorial designs, response-surface methods, or adaptive design to identify interactions while retaining independent world seeds as the replication level.

### 14.15 Negative and positive controls

**Negative controls** should include perceptually invisible observer labels, randomized pedigree labels, nonfunctional signals, noninjurious sham attacks, and impossible-to-access resource patches. These should not influence behavior.

**Positive controls** are artificial capability checks, not preferred production designs. Examples are an exact relatedness oracle, a stable arbitrary tag, a direct recruitment cue, or a simplified jointly operable resource. They answer whether the evolutionary and learning system can exploit a mechanism at all. If a positive control fails, interpreting a naturalistic treatment as evidence against the underlying theory is premature.

### 14.16 Preregistration and blinded analysis

Before viewing focal outcomes, record:

- the world versions and configuration hashes;
- primary metrics and thresholds;
- seed-generation rule and exclusion policy;
- minimum independent runs;
- event windows and community-detection parameters;
- null model and covariates;
- treatment contrasts;
- stopping rule;
- handling of extinctions and failed runs;
- distinction between confirmatory and exploratory analyses.

Where practical, analyze treatment-blinded event tables. Qualitative video review should follow, not determine, the primary classification.

## 15. Determinism implications

Social behavior magnifies small state differences. A one-tick change in visibility can alter a fight, which changes injury, migration, reproduction, relatedness, and later community structure. The correct goal is therefore not to make trajectories insensitive to perturbation; it is to make every trajectory exactly reproducible from the complete initial state, versioned rules, and explicit inputs.

### 15.1 Determinism contract for social mechanics

For a fixed engine build, schema version, configuration, initial checkpoint, external command stream, and seed namespace, Genesis should guarantee:

1. identical authoritative state at every committed tick;
2. identical event ordering and event payloads;
3. identical random values for every named stochastic decision;
4. identical save bytes or a documented canonical equivalent;
5. identical observer-independent simulation hashes;
6. exact restoration of inherited, learned, social-memory, marker, structure, injury, and controller-plasticity state;
7. no behavior dependence on observer classifications, visualization frame rate, log consumer timing, thread scheduling, hash-map iteration, memory address, or platform library behavior.

Determinism does not imply identical outcomes across different seeds, nor does it justify generalization from a single replay.

### 15.2 Named, keyed random streams

Use a counter-based or otherwise key-addressable pseudorandom function where practical. A draw key should identify the causal event rather than rely on a mutable global draw position. A conceptual key is:

```text
RNG(
  world_seed,
  subsystem_id,
  tick,
  event_kind,
  primary_entity_id,
  secondary_entity_id,
  local_draw_index,
  schema_version
)
```

Recommended independent namespaces include:

- world generation and resource renewal;
- weather or seasonal forcing;
- reproduction and mate-choice tie breaks;
- mutation by operator class;
- developmental noise;
- controller action sampling;
- lifetime-learning exploration;
- perception noise;
- signal production and reception noise;
- attack resolution and injury;
- collision or possession tie breaks;
- memory replacement when alternatives are exactly tied;
- exogenous experimental interventions.

The draw key must use canonically ordered entity identifiers for symmetric events. For directional events, explicitly distinguish actor and target. A branch intervention should not shift unrelated entities onto different random sequences merely because one event disappeared.

### 15.3 Stable entity identity

Every organism, object, structure component, resource packet, signal deposit, and persistent marker needs a stable simulation identifier. IDs should be:

- assigned by a deterministic allocator;
- never derived from memory addresses;
- never recycled within the period covered by logs or checkpoints unless a generation field prevents aliasing;
- serialized exactly;
- sortable using a total order;
- distinct from lineage IDs, genome IDs, observer community labels, and perceptual signatures.

Organisms need not perceive stable IDs. The engine and observer require them for causal logging; the controller should receive only modeled sensory information.

### 15.4 Tick-phase ordering

Social mechanics are especially vulnerable to traversal-order bias. Use explicit phases with immutable read snapshots or deterministic command buffers. One viable sequence is:

1. apply versioned external interventions;
2. renew or transform environment state;
3. construct spatial indices from committed positions;
4. generate perception snapshots;
5. update internal physiology and memory decay;
6. evaluate controllers using the same pre-action world snapshot;
7. emit intent records;
8. resolve movement and physical conflicts in canonical order;
9. resolve signals, transfers, construction, attacks, and mating using documented arbitration rules;
10. apply injury, possession, energy, and structure changes;
11. apply lifetime-learning updates from a canonical experience record;
12. process death, birth, mutation, and lineage updates;
13. commit markers and other delayed fields;
14. emit canonical events and state hash;
15. checkpoint if scheduled.

Alternative orders are valid, but the order itself is part of the model and version contract. An organism must not benefit merely because its entity appears earlier in an array.

### 15.5 Simultaneous action and contest resolution

When several agents target one resource, location, or opponent, do not resolve simply by iteration order. Options include:

- physically modeled simultaneous impulses;
- deterministic auction or priority based on modeled traits;
- all-participant outcome computation from the same pre-resolution state;
- keyed random tie break among exactly eligible competitors;
- explicit action latency and arrival time;
- deterministic proportional sharing.

The arbitration rule has evolutionary consequences. A hidden lower-ID advantage can become a selectable pseudo-trait if birth order correlates with lineage or age.

### 15.6 Neighborhood queries

Spatial hashes, grids, trees, and broad-phase collision structures may return neighbors in implementation-dependent order. Canonicalize candidate sets before any order-sensitive computation. Common strategies are:

- sort by stable entity ID;
- sort by quantized distance, then entity ID;
- accumulate commutative fixed-point summaries where identity is not relevant;
- use deterministic top-k selection with explicit ties;
- validate that parallel construction yields the same canonical buckets.

A controller that attends sequentially to neighbors must receive a deterministic order. Better still, make the order a modeled sensory fact—such as angular order or distance order—rather than an incidental data-structure order.

### 15.7 Numerical representation

Fixed-point arithmetic remains the safest authoritative representation for positions, energy, damage, signal intensity, relationship weights, marker decay, and learning state. Specify:

- scale and range;
- rounding mode;
- saturation or overflow behavior;
- division-by-zero behavior;
- transcendental approximations;
- normalization rules;
- update associativity and order.

If floating point is used, enforce a supported platform profile, prohibit unsafe reassociation and fused-operation variation unless standardized, avoid nondeterministic reductions, and regression-test bitwise state across all supported targets. Social-network statistics can be computed offline in higher precision because they do not feed behavior.

### 15.8 Deterministic neural and learning updates

Lifetime learning introduces additional state and ordering hazards. For each organism, serialize:

- all synaptic weights and plastic traces;
- neuromodulatory state;
- recurrent activations and delays;
- eligibility traces;
- optimizer or adaptation state, if any;
- short- and long-term memories;
- learned cue embeddings;
- individual/familiarity records;
- learned signal-response mappings;
- pending experiences not yet committed.

Define whether multiple experiences in one tick are aggregated or applied sequentially. If sequential, define canonical order. Plasticity must not iterate over hash maps, sparse edges, or active neurons in unstable order. Structural lifetime plasticity, if later allowed, needs deterministic innovation IDs and canonical mutation/application order just as inherited topology does.

### 15.9 Deterministic bounded social memory

A bounded memory table should specify:

- perceptual key construction;
- collision handling when two organisms look similar;
- update equation;
- decay timing;
- capacity;
- replacement policy;
- exact tie break;
- treatment of identity drift;
- serialization order.

Least-recently-used eviction is deterministic only when “recent” has a total-order timestamp and simultaneous accesses have a tie rule. Reservoir sampling needs named keyed draws. Avoid standard-library hash behavior as part of the model.

### 15.10 Signals and environmental fields

Diffusing or decaying signals can become platform-dependent if updates accumulate in parallel. Use one of:

- integer cellular fields with fixed kernels;
- double-buffered grids updated from canonical source lists;
- deterministic sparse tiles with sorted keys;
- exact rational or fixed-point decay schedules;
- event-based deposits with analytically defined intensity at query time.

Specify boundary conditions and kernel normalization. Markers from multiple producers should combine with an associative integer rule or a canonical ordered reduction. If producer-specific components are retained, define capacity and deterministic pruning.

### 15.11 Structures, contacts, and physics

General-purpose continuous rigid-body engines often make strict cross-platform determinism difficult. For Genesis, favor a restricted physics model with:

- fixed-step integration;
- quantized positions and orientations;
- simple primitive shapes;
- canonical contact ordering;
- bounded solver iterations;
- deterministic friction and restitution;
- explicit stacking limits;
- no sleeping/wake decisions based on nondeterministic thresholds;
- canonical joint and breakage resolution.

Construction and damage events should reference exact component IDs. Simultaneous modification of one component needs deterministic conflict arbitration.

### 15.12 Damage, death, and coalition events

Log the primitive causal chain rather than a high-level interpretation:

```text
attack_intent -> attack_executed -> contact -> damage_applied
-> impairment_change -> death_cause_candidate -> death_committed
```

A “coalition attack” should be computed offline from primitive actions. The authoritative engine should not write a coalition ID into organism state. If an online summary event is convenient, it must be derived after action resolution and must never feed back into controllers.

Death attribution can be ambiguous when injuries accumulate. Use a documented ledger of damage contributions and causal windows rather than assigning the kill to the final touch by default. Preserve all contributors for analysis.

### 15.13 Reproduction during social events

Reproduction, death, and membership changes can occur while a group or conflict episode is being inferred. Keep engine semantics simple:

- births and deaths occur at defined commit phases;
- newborn IDs and parentage are fixed before event emission;
- mate selection sees a specified world snapshot;
- posthumous reproduction, gestation, or stored gametes are explicit states;
- lineage and pedigree are observer-accessible records but not automatic social inputs.

Offline group membership should use temporal intervals rather than assume a static population.

### 15.14 Observer separation and no-label leakage

Maintain a one-way interface:

```text
Authoritative simulation state/events -> observer and offline analysis
```

Never allow:

```text
observer community / culture / species / territory / era label
    -> perception, reward, controller input, mutation, resource rule, or action resolution
```

A mandatory leakage test should run the same checkpoint with:

- no observer attached;
- one observer configuration;
- a different clustering algorithm and thresholds;
- visualization disabled or at a different frame rate;
- logs sampled at different rates where sampling is nonauthoritative.

The authoritative state-hash sequence must be identical in all cases.

### 15.15 Offline analysis determinism

Observer results should also be reproducible, although they are not part of organism behavior. Version:

- event schema;
- extraction query;
- time-window rule;
- community-detection algorithm and implementation;
- random seeds or deterministic initialization;
- tie-breaking and label alignment across windows;
- null-model generator;
- statistical model;
- classifier weights;
- plotting code.

Community labels are arbitrary permutations. Align labels across time by a deterministic maximum-overlap rule, and store the mapping. Report sensitivity to algorithm, resolution parameter, and time window rather than treating one partition as ground truth.

### 15.16 Save and restore

A complete checkpoint for this research program must include at least:

- authoritative clock and phase;
- world configuration and schema versions;
- all entity states and stable IDs;
- resource fields and renewal schedules;
- structures and component damage;
- possession and pending transfers;
- signal and marker deposits;
- organism physiology and injury;
- genomes and developmental state;
- neural controller and all learned/plastic state;
- bounded memories and learned recognition state;
- pending action intents or delayed actions;
- gestation/reproduction state and pedigree;
- RNG namespace/counter data where keys alone are insufficient;
- scheduled exogenous interventions;
- canonical hash of the checkpoint.

Loading should fail closed on unknown required fields, incompatible numeric formats, or schema mismatches. Migrations must be deterministic and explicitly versioned.

### 15.17 Replay verification levels

Use several replay tests:

1. **Immediate round trip:** save, load, and compare canonical state.
2. **Short continuation:** replay hundreds of ticks and compare every tick hash.
3. **Long continuation:** compare periodic hashes across many generations.
4. **Social stress test:** checkpoint immediately before multi-agent contests, births, deaths, signal bursts, and construction conflicts.
5. **Cross-machine test:** compare supported CPU architectures and operating systems.
6. **Thread-count test:** run with different worker counts and scheduling stress.
7. **Observer-isolation test:** vary attached observers and logging consumers.
8. **Branch reproducibility:** repeat the same intervention branch twice and compare exactly.

When a mismatch occurs, hierarchical hashes—world region, subsystem, entity, and component—make the first divergence traceable.

### 15.18 Counterfactual branches and random coupling

Paired branches are powerful but require care. Two useful coupling modes are:

- **structural-key coupling:** unchanged causal events receive identical keyed draws; deleted or added events do not shift others;
- **distributional independence:** treatment-specific stochastic events use separate namespaces, while common exogenous forcing remains coupled.

Document which mode is used. For a direct causal intervention, common random numbers can reduce variance. For long evolutionary trajectories, paired branches eventually become different causal worlds; report both paired early effects and independent-seed long-run effects.

### 15.19 Parallelism

Deterministic parallel execution is possible when work is divided into read-only evaluation followed by canonical merge. Recommended pattern:

- partition entities by stable spatial tile or ID range;
- evaluate perceptions/controllers independently against immutable snapshots;
- write commands to per-partition buffers;
- concatenate buffers in canonical partition order;
- sort commands by declared resolution key;
- resolve cross-partition conflicts centrally or with deterministic ownership;
- use fixed reduction trees for aggregates.

Work stealing may change runtime but must not change merge order. Avoid atomic “first wins” mechanics for authoritative decisions.

### 15.20 Determinism risk register

| Risk | Failure mode | Mitigation | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hash-map iteration | Different neighbor/action order | Sorted keys or deterministic container | Container-implementation differential test |
| Thread scheduling | Different contest winner | Intent buffering and canonical resolution | Thread-count and scheduler stress test |
| Floating-point reduction | Divergent fields or learning | Fixed point or fixed reduction tree | Cross-platform bitwise test |
| Global sequential RNG | Intervention shifts all later draws | Keyed named streams | Paired-branch RNG audit |
| Physics contact order | Different positions/injuries | Restricted deterministic solver | Dense-contact replay corpus |
| Memory eviction ties | Different social histories | Total-order timestamps and ID tie break | Forced-capacity unit tests |
| Neural sparse traversal | Different plastic updates | Canonical node/edge order | Topology permutation test |
| Observer feedback | Labels alter behavior | One-way process/interface boundary | Observer configuration hash test |
| Save omission | Restored social behavior diverges | Explicit state manifest and fail-closed load | Mid-conflict checkpoint test |
| Schema migration | Silent semantic change | Versioned deterministic migrations | Golden-checkpoint suite |
| Log backpressure | Simulation timing changes | Asynchronous nonauthoritative copy or deterministic bounded policy | Slow/absent consumer test |
| ID allocation race | Different newborn/object IDs | Central deterministic allocation plan | Concurrent birth/construction test |

### 15.21 Determinism checklist for every new social mechanism

Before merging a mechanism, answer:

- What authoritative state does it add?
- What is the canonical update phase?
- What is its total ordering rule?
- What named RNG namespace and key does it use?
- Does it depend on traversal or neighbor order?
- How are simultaneous claims resolved?
- How is all learned and pending state serialized?
- Does any observer label enter its inputs?
- Can it be ablated without shifting unrelated random draws?
- What golden replay covers its worst multi-agent case?
- What computational precision and overflow policy apply?
- What schema version change is required?

No social feature should be considered complete until these questions have executable tests.

## 16. Compute and population-scale implications

The limiting cost of social evolution is rarely the neural controller alone. Perception edges, remembered partners, persistent environmental state, long evolutionary horizons, and causal logging can dominate. Scale should therefore be planned around the number of **interaction opportunities and independent group histories**, not only the number of organisms.

### 16.1 Principal cost drivers

Let:

- \(N\) = living organisms;
- \(O\) = persistent world objects and structure components;
- \(C\) = active spatial cells or tiles;
- \(E_t\) = organism–organism perceptual or interaction edges at tick \(t\);
- \(M\) = average retained social-memory records per organism;
- \(S\) = signal or marker channels;
- \(A\) = average active neural connections per organism;
- \(L\) = emitted primitive events per tick.

Approximate authoritative costs are:

| Subsystem | Typical time cost per tick | Typical memory cost | Scaling hazard |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Spatial indexing | \(O(N + O)\) expected for a bounded-density grid/hash | \(O(N + O + C)\) | Dense cells and pathological clustering |
| Local perception | \(O(E_t)\) plus sorting/top-k | transient \(O(E_t)\) or streamed | \(E_t\) approaches \(N^2\) if radius is too large |
| Neural control | \(O(NA)\) | \(O(NA)\) | Evolved topology bloat and recurrent state |
| Social memory | \(O(NM)\) worst-case update/decay, usually sparse | \(O(NM)\) | Unbounded identity tables |
| Signals/markers | \(O(CS)\) for dense grids or active-tile cost for sparse fields | \(O(CS)\) | Long-lived producer-specific traces |
| Structures/physics | Broad phase near \(O(N+O)\); contact resolution depends on local density | \(O(O + contacts)\) | Stacking, dense construction, fragmentation |
| Logging | \(O(L)\) serialization and I/O | buffer plus retained corpus | Full percept/controller logging can exceed simulation cost |
| Offline network analysis | Algorithm-dependent; often at least proportional to temporal edge count | event and graph storage | Dense temporal networks, repeated nulls, many thresholds |

The practical objective is bounded local density and bounded per-agent state. A world with 5,000 agents each perceiving 20 neighbors may be cheaper and scientifically better than 1,000 agents with global visibility.

### 16.2 Population size by experimental claim

These are starting ranges, not universal biological requirements. The correct scale depends on carrying capacity, dispersal, generation time, encounter radius, and the number of independently persistent social units.

| Experimental stage | Suggested initial population range | Desired independent units per world | Why |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Spatial aggregation | 50–500 | 5–30 transient clusters | Enough density for phase transitions and finite-size checks |
| Stable affiliative groups | 100–1,000 | 5–20 groups | Allows turnover, fission, fusion, and between-group variance |
| Kin-biased grouping | 200–1,500 | 20+ family lineages across several groups | Separates pedigree from spatial founder effects |
| Cooperation and cheaters | 200–2,000 | Multiple local interaction neighborhoods | Supports invasion tests and avoids one group determining the result |
| Sharing and division of labor | 300–2,000 | 10+ persistent groups or sites | Provides role and flow data without requiring global society |
| Territorial occupancy/defense | 300–3,000 | 10–50 defendable patches/home ranges | Enables resident–intruder contrasts and boundary geometry |
| Individual aggression | 200–2,000 | Many dyads across contexts | Estimates risk and escalation without requiring coalitions |
| Coalitionary aggression | 500–5,000 | At least 10 groups with repeated encounters | Coalition events are rare and require alternative partners/opponents |
| Repeated inter-group conflict | 1,000–10,000 where feasible | Preferably 10–30 persistent communities | Separates opponent-specific recurrence from two-group idiosyncrasy |
| Conflict traditions | 1,000+ over many turnovers | Multiple independent community lineages | Cultural inference requires transmission, migration, and replacement |

A smaller world can still produce a valid proof of mechanism, but estimates of persistence, migration, coalition choice, and cultural differentiation will be fragile if only two groups ever exist. Several independent worlds with moderate populations are generally more informative than one enormous world.

### 16.3 Effective sample size

A run containing 100,000 attacks does not supply 100,000 independent observations if they come from the same ten organisms under one ecology. Report the hierarchy:

- independent engine seeds;
- independently initialized worlds within a seed family, if applicable;
- populations or islands;
- inferred communities;
- lineages;
- episodes;
- dyads;
- primitive events.

Use multilevel models, cluster-robust uncertainty, or seed-level summaries. Avoid selecting “successful” runs and then treating their internal episodes as evidence for prevalence.

### 16.4 Seed budgets

Rare evolutionary outcomes require many seeds, but seed counts should be tied to uncertainty rather than ritual numbers. A practical sequence is:

1. **Engineering smoke tests:** 5–20 fixed seeds designed to trigger edge cases; not scientific evidence.
2. **Pilot distribution:** 30–50 independent seeds per treatment to estimate extinction, variance, and event prevalence.
3. **Confirmatory mechanism test:** often 100–300 seeds per treatment, with preregistered primary metric and power/simulation-based justification.
4. **Rare coalition/conflict search:** adaptive screening may use thousands of short seeds, followed by an unbiased confirmatory sample and full-length reruns.
5. **Cultural persistence:** fewer but much longer runs may be needed; retain enough independent community lineages to estimate between-world variation.

When computation is constrained, reduce factorial breadth before reducing independent seeds below a level where treatment effects are distinguishable from evolutionary contingency.

### 16.5 World area, density, and geometry

Population size without world geometry is uninterpretable. Track:

- realized organisms per traversable area;
- local neighbor-count distribution;
- mean free path and travel time between resource patches;
- encounter opportunity by dyad and group;
- fraction of world occupied by high-value resources;
- boundary length and permeability;
- refuge availability;
- line-of-sight and occlusion;
- edge-to-area ratio;
- seasonal connectivity.

Periodic boundaries reduce edge accumulation but can make territories wrap unnaturally and eliminate frontier effects. Reflecting or hard boundaries can create artificial congregation and defensible corners. Large buffer regions, irregular natural barriers, or multiple topology treatments are preferable when territorial geometry is central.

### 16.6 Interaction radius and edge explosion

For approximately uniform two-dimensional density \(\rho\) and perception radius \(r\), expected neighbor count scales roughly as \(\rho \pi r^2\). Doubling radius therefore quadruples candidate neighbors before occlusion or top-k filtering. Recommended safeguards:

- local sensory range tied to modeled physics;
- angular or distance top-k attention;
- line-of-sight pruning;
- separate low-bandwidth aggregate senses from identity-bearing focal perception;
- hard diagnostic warnings when candidate neighborhoods exceed design bounds;
- density-dependent profiling.

Do not solve scaling by giving organisms a global list of allies or enemies; that would also script social knowledge.

### 16.7 Social-memory scale

An all-pairs relationship matrix costs \(O(N^2)\) and grants implausible memory. Prefer bounded sparse records. Illustrative capacities:

- 4–16 signatures: familiarity, recent reciprocation, simple partner choice;
- 16–64: differentiated local relationships in moderate groups;
- 64–256: richer reputation or fission–fusion societies, with substantial cost;
- global population table: use only as an artificial upper bound.

Memory capacity itself can be evolvable if it has explicit energetic, developmental, latency, or genome-size costs. Otherwise evolution will often maximize it simply because the simulator makes cognition free.

### 16.8 Recognition computation

Phenotypic matching can be implemented as a small learned or inherited embedding rather than pairwise genome comparison. Cost options include:

- direct distance in a low-dimensional perceived cue vector: \(O(d)\) per encounter;
- bounded prototype memory: \(O(Kd)\);
- recurrent familiarity update: \(O(d)\);
- exact pedigree coefficient: expensive offline and too direct as a behavioral input;
- shared signal association: constant to low-dimensional cost but deception-prone.

Pedigree relatedness for analysis can be calculated from lineage records offline or incrementally cached. Do not put the resulting coefficient into the controller unless running a labeled oracle control.

### 16.9 Signal and marker fields

Dense grids are predictable but can be expensive at large map sizes. Sparse active-tile fields are preferable when deposits are localized, provided key ordering and pruning are deterministic. Techniques include:

- chunked tiles activated on first deposit;
- integer decay that reaches zero in bounded time;
- scheduled expiry buckets;
- separable fixed kernels;
- analytical point sources queried locally;
- channel caps and source aggregation;
- distance-limited propagation;
- multiresolution observer copies that do not affect authoritative perception.

Producer-specific scent components can become \(O(NSC)\) in the worst case. Aggregate physical properties where possible, or retain only bounded top contributors with canonical pruning. A generic trace is both cheaper and less faction-like.

### 16.10 Structures and mutable-world growth

Open-ended construction can increase object count indefinitely. Control growth through physical—not arbitrary social—constraints:

- conservation of material;
- minimum component size;
- energetic and time cost;
- structural load and decay;
- weathering;
- breakage and reusable debris;
- bounded spatial resolution;
- garbage collection only for physically irrelevant zero-mass or fully decayed records;
- deterministic merging of identical adjacent material cells where scientifically acceptable.

Track object and component creation per generation. Structure proliferation is an evolutionary outcome, but uncontrolled representational fragmentation is an engine artifact.

### 16.11 Neural-controller cost

Variable neural topology and lifetime plasticity can dominate at scale. Use:

- explicit connection and neuron budgets or costs;
- sparse canonical adjacency;
- integer/fixed-point activations where possible;
- bounded recurrent horizon/state;
- phenotype compilation cached by genome hash;
- batched evaluation only if it preserves exact ordering and arithmetic;
- separate controller-complexity metrics in evolutionary analysis.

A social behavior that appears only when controllers become extremely large may be scientifically interesting but operationally incompatible with large populations. Record the minimum controller complexity at which the behavior survives ablation.

### 16.12 Logging architecture

Primitive social events should be append-only, schema-versioned, and columnar after capture. A high-value event schema contains identifiers and state references, not full state duplication. Suggested tiers:

**Tier 0: authoritative audit**

- births, deaths, mutation and lineage;
- checkpoints and configuration changes;
- possession/resource/structure changes;
- signal/marker deposits;
- attacks, damage, transfer, mating;
- state hashes.

**Tier 1: social analysis**

- encounter opportunity;
- proximity and co-movement summaries;
- observation edges;
- directed aid/share/attack events;
- recruitment exposure and response;
- entry/exit from spatial cores;
- selected memory updates.

**Tier 2: diagnostic sampling**

- full percept vectors;
- controller activations;
- plastic updates;
- detailed contact solver records;
- high-frequency trajectories.

Tier 2 should be activated for selected windows or entities because continuous full logging can dwarf simulation state. Sampling policy must not alter behavior and must be recorded.

### 16.13 Event volume estimates

Illustrative order-of-magnitude calculation:

- 2,000 organisms;
- 10 ticks per simulated second;
- average 12 visible conspecifics;
- one opportunity edge logged every second rather than every tick;
- 10 million simulated seconds.

Raw opportunity edges would still total roughly 240 billion records. This is not necessary. Compress exposure into intervals or sufficient statistics:

```text
(observer, target, start_tick, end_tick,
 min_distance, mean_distance, visibility_fraction,
 actions_observed_bitset, context_summary)
```

Similarly, co-membership and territory occupancy can be reconstructed from sampled trajectories plus exact discrete events. Preserve sufficient temporal resolution for recruitment and coordination analyses, where sub-episode timing matters.

### 16.14 Compression and storage

Recommended practices:

- binary authoritative event stream with fixed schema and checksums;
- asynchronous conversion to Arrow/Parquet or an equivalent columnar format;
- dictionary encoding for event types and immutable genome hashes;
- delta encoding for ticks and positions;
- run/seed/treatment partitioning;
- periodic trajectory keyframes plus deterministic deltas;
- separate immutable metadata manifest;
- content-addressed genome and controller phenotype blobs;
- retention tiers: full audit, analysis-ready summaries, and disposable diagnostics;
- explicit provenance from every derived table back to event ranges and code version.

Do not discard primitive harm, transfer, recruitment, or observation events after producing aggregate “war” labels. Those labels may change as definitions improve.

### 16.15 Checkpoint cadence

Checkpointing enables replay, branch interventions, and recovery, but learned state and mutable world objects make checkpoints large. Balance:

- periodic full canonical checkpoints;
- deterministic incremental snapshots or event-log replay between full checkpoints;
- event-triggered checkpoints before rare high-value episodes only when trigger logic is observer-independent or recorded as an external experimental action;
- content-addressed deduplication of unchanged genome/controller data;
- compression that is deterministic or treated as nonauthoritative packaging.

For coalition studies, retain checkpoints before first contact, first recruitment signal, first attack, and major casualty where feasible. Since those moments are often identified after the fact, a rolling checkpoint ring or deterministic event replay is more reliable than trying to predict them online.

### 16.16 Offline network-analysis cost

A temporal interaction graph can be much larger than the simulation. Reduce it through scientifically declared aggregation:

- time windows based on organismal timescale, not convenient wall-clock units;
- edge weights normalized by opportunity;
- separate affiliation, aid, observation, signal, transfer, and aggression layers;
- sparse thresholding only after preserving raw weighted summaries;
- incremental community analysis;
- deterministic label alignment;
- sensitivity analysis across windows and resolution parameters.

Dynamic community detection and null-model permutations can dominate postprocessing. Run coarse screens first, then full multilayer or temporal methods on preregistered treatments and holdout seeds.

### 16.17 Coordination-analysis cost

Testing whether attacks are coordinated may require conditional hazard models, permutation nulls, and branched replays. Use a funnel:

1. cheap candidate detector based on temporal overlap, common target, and proximity;
2. opportunity-matched statistical null;
3. recruitment and observation analysis;
4. detailed trajectory reconstruction;
5. branched ally-removal or signal-ablation replay for a stratified sample;
6. full confirmatory ensemble on the resulting mechanistic hypothesis.

Never let the cheap detector become the final coalition definition merely because the causal analysis is expensive.

### 16.18 Evolutionary horizon

Social strategies often need longer than individual optimization because they depend on:

- enough descendants for kin structure;
- repeated group fission and fusion;
- cheater invasion and response;
- environmental turnover;
- convention establishment;
- migration between communities;
- replacement of founders;
- mutation of recognition and signaling systems;
- extinction and recolonization.

Report time in several units: ticks, organism lifetimes, median generation intervals, population turnovers, resource-renewal cycles, and inferred group lifetimes. “One million ticks” is not a meaningful evolutionary horizon without those conversions.

### 16.19 Distributed execution

Independent seeds are embarrassingly parallel and should be the main scaling axis. Within-world distribution is harder because cross-boundary perception, movement, signals, and structures require deterministic synchronization. Prefer:

- many independent worlds per worker;
- deterministic checkpoint/restart;
- immutable build/container manifest;
- per-run output directories and checksums;
- no worker-specific seed derivation based on arrival order;
- centralized or precomputed treatment/seed manifest;
- exact duplicate-run detection.

Only distribute one world across nodes when population scale demands it. Spatial partitions then need fixed ownership, halo exchange at explicit phases, canonical cross-partition command ordering, and failure semantics that never silently skip a tick.

### 16.20 GPU considerations

GPU acceleration can help batched neural evaluation, perception transforms, and dense fields, but strict reproducibility is difficult when kernels use unordered atomics, nonassociative reductions, architecture-dependent transcendental functions, or mixed precision. A safe path is:

1. retain a CPU deterministic reference implementation;
2. restrict GPU kernels to fixed-shape integer or precisely specified operations;
3. avoid atomic conflict resolution;
4. use fixed reduction trees;
5. compare authoritative hashes across supported GPU models;
6. treat approximate GPU modes as separate noncanonical experiment classes, never as replay-equivalent.

For early social experiments, CPU parallelism across seeds is likely more valuable than accelerating one world at the cost of determinism.

### 16.21 Adaptive experiment allocation

Computation can be allocated adaptively without bias if rules are declared in advance. Examples:

- terminate worlds that become irrecoverably extinct, while counting extinction as an outcome;
- extend worlds that cross a preregistered candidate threshold, but validate prevalence in an unbiased fixed-horizon sample;
- use sequential confidence bounds for obviously null engineering variants;
- allocate more seeds near transition regions discovered in a pilot;
- reserve a fixed untouched holdout seed set for final confirmation.

Do not report only the extended “interesting” worlds. The screening denominator and selection rule are part of the result.

### 16.22 Compute-priority order

When resources are limited, prioritize in this order:

1. deterministic correctness and exact replay;
2. independent seeds and appropriate controls;
3. primitive event logs sufficient for causal classification;
4. long enough horizons for the claim;
5. multiple communities and demographic turnover;
6. ablations and opportunity-matched nulls;
7. richer controller architectures;
8. visual fidelity;
9. larger single-world population beyond what the claim needs.

A visually impressive 100,000-agent world with one seed, opaque metrics, and no ablations provides weaker evidence than a modest, well-replicated system.

### 16.23 Recommended initial scale envelope

For a first deterministic implementation, a reasonable target envelope is:

- 500–2,000 organisms per world;
- local perception capped near 16–32 identity-bearing neighbors;
- 16–64 social-memory entries per organism;
- 2–8 generic signal/marker channels;
- 10–50 persistent resource or structure sites;
- 50–300 independent seeds for a confirmatory treatment;
- event logging focused on intervals and primitive actions;
- one to several hundred organism lifetimes per evolutionary phase;
- exact checkpoints frequent enough to branch rare episodes.

This envelope should support stages 1–9 and some stage-10 coalition tests. Repeated inter-group conflict and culturally transmitted tactics may demand larger populations, longer runs, or both, but increasing scale should follow evidence that the prerequisite stages work.

## 17. Major theoretical disputes

The mechanisms reviewed here come from fields with genuine disagreements about causal language, levels of explanation, empirical prevalence, and historical interpretation. Genesis should not encode one side of these disputes as a privileged law. It should implement separable mechanisms and let experiments ask which descriptions are useful under specified conditions.

### 17.1 Inclusive fitness versus multilevel selection

**Broad common ground.** Heritable social behavior changes in frequency according to its effects on transmission, including effects mediated through social partners. Population structure, relatedness, assortment, and between-group differences can matter.

**Dispute.** Inclusive-fitness theorists often describe selection using effects on recipients weighted by relatedness; multilevel-selection analyses partition change into within- and between-group components. Much disagreement concerns mathematical framing, domain of convenient application, and claims about explanatory primacy. The controversy intensified around models of eusociality and whether inclusive-fitness methods are necessary or generally sufficient (Nowak, Tarnita, and Wilson 2010; Abbot et al. 2011).

**Genesis implication.** Log pedigrees, interaction networks, fitness components, migration, and group partitions, then analyze the same run through both lenses. Do not implement a “group selection force,” a group fitness variable, or an inclusive-fitness reward. Selection must arise from differential survival and reproduction under individual mechanics.

**Falsifiable comparison.** Test whether a helping allele’s change is predicted by direct-fitness decomposition, relatedness-weighted recipient effects, and multilevel Price-equation partitions. Divergence between approximations can reveal nonadditivity, demographic feedback, changing group definitions, or strong selection rather than establish that one theory is universally invalid.

### 17.2 What relatedness means

Relatedness can mean genealogical kinship, genetic similarity at causal loci, excess identity relative to a population reference, phenotypic resemblance, or statistical assortment. These are not interchangeable. Local competition among kin can cancel benefits that appear favorable under a naive \(rb>c\) calculation, and recognition cues need not track genome-wide pedigree.

**Genesis implication.** Report several quantities separately: pedigree coefficient, genome-wide similarity, similarity at social loci, cue similarity, familiarity, and realized interaction assortment. Never label phenotypic similarity as kinship without validation.

### 17.3 Group selection and the reality of groups

Some models define groups as discrete reproducing collectives; others use temporary neighborhoods, demes, interaction sets, or communities inferred from networks. A between-group covariance can be calculated for many partitions, but its biological significance depends on whether the partition captures causal population structure.

**Genesis implication.** Use multiple observer partitions and demonstrate robustness. Group-level reproduction, propagule formation, or extinction should be inferred from organism movement and lineage flow unless collective reproduction becomes a physically implemented phenomenon. Do not let a clustering algorithm create causal groups retroactively.

### 17.4 Does spatial structure promote cooperation?

Spatial clustering can let cooperators interact repeatedly and protect one another, as in classic spatial games. But local competition, update rule, mobility, graph degree, replacement neighborhood, benefit form, and stochasticity can reverse or erase the effect. There is no universal theorem that “space causes cooperation.”

**Genesis implication.** Cross interaction radius with offspring dispersal, resource scale, replacement scale, and movement. Measure both social assortment and competition among relatives or cooperators. An effect attributed to “space” should survive controls for encounter repetition and resource monopolization.

### 17.5 Reciprocity: central mechanism or special case?

Direct reciprocity is theoretically powerful under repeated interaction, recognition, memory, and sufficient future value. Its empirical importance varies across taxa and contexts, and apparent turn-taking can instead reflect mutualism, symmetry, tolerated theft, partner control, or correlated state. Bootstrapping reciprocity is difficult when initial help is costly and no relationship exists.

**Genesis implication.** Begin with byproduct mutualism and partner choice before assuming scorekeeping. Require lagged, partner-specific contingency after controlling for opportunity and current state. Compare no memory, familiarity only, last-outcome memory, and richer history.

### 17.6 Indirect reciprocity and reputation

Indirect reciprocity depends on observers, reliable attribution, information propagation, and norms for evaluating actions. Human models often assume cognitively rich public reputations and shared moral rules. Evidence in nonhuman systems is more limited and sometimes better explained by direct observation of service quality or partner choice.

**Genesis implication.** Treat reputation as an advanced emergent possibility. Do not supply a global score. Implement local observation and bounded memory; then test whether third-party information changes partner behavior. A public scalar reputation would be a positive-control oracle, not the minimal design.

### 17.7 Punishment versus partner choice

Costly punishment can stabilize cooperation in some models and experiments, but it can also invite second-order free riding, antisocial punishment, escalation, and high social cost. Partner choice, ostracism, switching, and market-like competition can sustain cooperation without paying to harm defectors and may be more widespread under fluid associations.

**Genesis implication.** Provide the physical options to refuse, leave, withhold, displace, or attack. Do not encode “punish defector” as a special action. Compare whether cheater resistance is carried mainly by avoidance, differential access, direct retaliation, third-party intervention, or injury.

### 17.8 Policing and coercion

Policing can increase group efficiency, but observed aggression toward defectors may serve dominance, reproductive competition, resource defense, or self-interest rather than group welfare. In eusocial systems, worker policing is linked to reproductive relatedness and colony organization; extrapolation to flexible groups is not automatic.

**Genesis implication.** Classify policing functionally only after demonstrating that intervention suppresses a behavior that harms others or collective output, that interveners bear a cost or gain a direct benefit, and that alternative motives are controlled.

### 17.9 Green beards, tags, and arbitrary similarity

Green-beard systems show that a cue genetically associated with social behavior can direct aid toward cue-bearers. They also face recombination, mutation, mimicry, and exploitation unless linkage or pleiotropy is maintained. Artificial tag models can produce striking ethnocentrism, but their results are highly sensitive to mutation, spatial structure, update rules, and the fact that a low-cost perfect tag is supplied.

**Genesis implication.** Never infer that arbitrary tags are a neutral shortcut to realistic groups. Make cues noisy, multidimensional, mutable, and potentially deceptive; separate cue production from response genetically; and test recombination/linkage. Use tag models to study recognition dynamics, not to install factions.

### 17.10 Kin recognition versus familiarity

Animals may discriminate kin through phenotype matching, learned family templates, spatial association, or familiarity. Many apparent kin biases do not require computation of relatedness; growing up together can be enough. Conversely, familiarity may be a poor kin proxy under dispersal, adoption, or group fusion.

**Genesis implication.** Cross-fostering and cue-scrambling experiments are essential. A controller should not receive pedigree directly. Report whether behavior follows ancestry, rearing partner, phenotype, or place.

### 17.11 Leadership versus emergent influence

Collective movement can be led by informed minorities, persistent initiators, dominant individuals, central network positions, or simple differences in speed and motivation. A directional correlation does not establish command, intentional leadership, or recognized authority.

**Genesis implication.** Measure information flow, initiation, following probability, and counterfactual removal. Use “leader” only operationally, such as an individual whose behavior reliably predicts and causally changes group direction. Do not implement a leader role.

### 17.12 Division of labor: specialization or state dependence?

Repeated task differences can result from genetic specialization, learned skill, age, morphology, energetic state, spatial position, dominance, or random persistence. Colony-level efficiency does not prove evolved caste organization.

**Genesis implication.** Track within-individual task transitions, performance, exposure, morphology, age, and heritability. Transfer individuals between contexts and remove specialists. Claim division of labor only when differentiated activity is persistent and functionally interdependent; claim caste-like organization only with stronger developmental evidence.

### 17.13 Animal culture definitions

Definitions range from any socially transmitted group-typical behavior to narrower standards requiring durable traditions and exclusion of genetic and ecological explanations. The “method of exclusion” is often difficult in the wild because communities differ in both ecology and ancestry. Experimental diffusion studies and migrant convergence provide stronger evidence but remain limited to tractable behaviors.

**Genesis implication.** Use graded language:

- socially influenced acquisition;
- socially transmitted variant;
- persistent tradition;
- cumulative culture.

Each grade should have explicit evidence requirements. Geographic clustering alone is insufficient, and cumulative culture requires retained modifications that improve or elaborate prior variants across learners or generations.

### 17.14 Conformity and frequency dependence

A population-level preference for common behavior can stabilize traditions, but apparent conformity can arise from repeated exposure, demonstrator prestige, payoff-biased learning, or simple copying error. Statistical “conformity” requires a disproportionately strong probability of adopting the majority variant relative to exposure.

**Genesis implication.** Log exposure counts and model identity. Compare linear copying, majority-biased updating, payoff bias, and unbiased observation. Do not infer a conformity bias merely because variants become homogeneous.

### 17.15 Territoriality as a category or continuum

Definitions vary. Some require exclusive defense of a fixed area; others include defended resources, dynamic ranges, scent-mediated spacing, or occasional resident advantage. Home-range overlap does not exclude territorial behavior, and spatial segregation does not prove defense.

**Genesis implication.** Report component measures—site fidelity, exclusivity, active defense, marking, patrol, resident advantage, boundary stability, and cost—before assigning a territory label. Let offline analysis define stricter or broader thresholds.

### 17.16 Ownership conventions versus force

Prior-residence effects and “bourgeois” strategies can reduce contest costs when both parties follow an ownership convention. However, residents may win because of information, motivation, resource valuation, physical preparation, or selected habitat quality rather than convention. A convention requires role-contingent behavior not reducible to strength.

**Genesis implication.** Swap residents and intruders, equalize condition, relocate resources, and stage interactions in neutral sites. Test whether the same organism changes strategy with role and whether marker or occupancy history mediates the effect.

### 17.17 Scarcity and conflict

A common claim is that scarce resources cause violence. Evidence is more conditional. Scarcity can increase competition, but dispersed, unpredictable, nondefendable resources may favor mobility or tolerance. Concentrated, valuable, predictable, excludable resources can motivate defense even when total abundance is high. Extreme scarcity can make fighting unaffordable or disperse populations.

**Genesis implication.** Separate total abundance from concentration, renewability, predictability, transportability, substitutability, and contest cost. Treat scarcity as one variable in contest economics, not a conflict switch.

### 17.18 Population pressure and density

Higher density raises encounter rates but can also increase partner availability, trade, information, and the cost of indiscriminate aggression. Low density can reduce coalition opportunities; very high density can produce collision-driven false aggression. Historical and ecological claims that population pressure alone explains warfare remain contested.

**Genesis implication.** Model realized encounter opportunity and local carrying capacity. Compare aggression per encounter, not only per unit time, and distinguish demographic pressure from spatial bottlenecks.

### 17.19 Predation, competition, and warfare analogies

Conspecific killing, predation, infanticide, cannibalism, lethal dominance, and inter-group attack have different payoff structures. Using the same damage primitive does not make them one behavioral phenomenon. Human “warfare” additionally invokes social identities, institutions, norms, planning, and often symbolic meaning that may be absent in artificial populations.

**Genesis implication.** Preserve narrow operational categories. Reserve “organized inter-group conflict” for recurrent, group-correlated, coordinated harmful episodes with identifiable collective consequences. Avoid “war” unless the report explicitly defines it as an observer shorthand and demonstrates the relevant criteria.

### 17.20 Adaptive coalitionary aggression in chimpanzees

Long-term chimpanzee studies document boundary patrols and lethal intercommunity attacks, and comparative analyses have argued that lethal aggression is better explained by adaptive variables such as party composition and numerical advantage than by human disturbance (Wilson et al. 2014). Critics and broader comparative work caution against treating one lineage as a transparent model of ancestral human warfare or assuming all intergroup hostility shares the same mechanism.

**Genesis implication.** The imbalance-of-power hypothesis is a testable design hypothesis: attacks should be more likely when attackers can reduce personal risk through local numerical superiority and isolated targets. It is not a license to assume that kin clusters plus damage will produce patrols or raids.

### 17.21 Origins and prevalence of human warfare

The frequency and antiquity of warfare among mobile foragers are debated. Bowles (2009) argued that intergroup conflict could have been sufficiently lethal to influence the evolution of human social behavior. Fry and Söderberg (2013) found that lethal events in a sample of mobile forager societies were often interpersonal rather than warlike. Archaeological cases such as Nataruk have been interpreted as inter-group violence and then challenged on depositional and contextual grounds; Jebel Sahaba shows repeated interpersonal violence but does not by itself settle the prevalence or organization of warfare across prehistory.

**Genesis implication.** Do not use a single ethnographic or archaeological narrative as a target trajectory. Run competing ecological models and use narrowly defined metrics for coordinated episodes, recurrence, group differentiation, and consequences.

### 17.22 War as adaptation, byproduct, or culturally organized institution

Theories variously emphasize evolved coalitional psychology, male reproductive competition, defense, resource capture, revenge, prestige, coercive institutions, cultural group selection, or byproducts of flexible social cognition. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and their importance likely varies across societies and periods.

De Dreu (2026) sharpens one side of this dispute by arguing that coalitionary conflict may be a by-product of capacities for cooperation and parochial solidarity rather than the ultimate cause that created cooperation. That claim is not a settled consensus, but it exposes a serious origin problem for models that invoke intergroup conflict as an explanation while presupposing stable groups, out-groups, and coalition participation.

**Genesis implication.** A minimal artificial-life engine can test conditions for coalitionary aggression and recurrent conflict, but it should not claim to reproduce human war without symbolic norms, institutions, demographic structure, and cultural history. Experiments should compare both causal directions—external threat increasing within-group cooperation, and prior cooperation/interdependence enabling coalitionary conflict—rather than embedding either direction as an assumption. “Organized conflict” is the scientifically safer target.

### 17.23 Sex differences and reproductive skew

Some theories connect coalitionary aggression to male reproductive skew and mating competition. Yet coalition participation, defense, and aggression vary widely across species and social systems; ecological and demographic variables matter. Artificial organisms may not have human-like sexes or parental investment.

**Genesis implication.** Implement reproductive roles only as biologically meaningful mechanics, then manipulate skew, parental investment, dispersal, and offspring dependence independently. Do not hard-code male coalitions or assign sex-specific aggression.

### 17.24 Interdependence and peace

Interdependence can reduce aggression by raising the opportunity cost of harming partners, but it can also create exploitation, coercion, or strategic attacks on competitors outside the exchange network. Trade and mutual dependence are not universal peace mechanisms.

**Genesis implication.** Make benefits physically contingent on complementary capabilities, exchange, shared structures, or joint tasks. Measure whether conflict shifts from within-network to between-network targets and whether dependence creates vulnerability.

### 17.25 Revenge and deterrence

Retaliation can deter exploitation, but it can also generate cycles of escalation, mistaken attribution, and lineage-level feuds. Observed repeated aggression may reflect simple conditioned aversion or target persistence rather than a revenge motive.

**Genesis implication.** Use operational language: opponent-specific response to remembered harm after the immediate contest. Test memory removal, identity ambiguity, time delay, third-party generalization, and whether retaliation reduces future attacks. Do not infer emotion or moral intent.

### 17.26 Communication: information, manipulation, or convention

Signals can provide information, manipulate receivers, coordinate mutual benefit, or persist as arbitrary conventions. Honest signaling may be stabilized by common interest, cost, constraint, repeated interaction, or receiver verification; conflict of interest can favor suppression and deception.

**Genesis implication.** Evolve signal production and receiver response separately. Measure information-theoretic association, causal effect, sender/receiver payoff, reliability, and deception. Never assign fixed meanings such as “enemy here” to channels in the core emergence experiments.

### 17.27 Swarm intelligence versus social cognition

Complex collective patterns can arise from extremely simple local rules, as flocking, trail formation, and self-organized construction demonstrate. Conversely, stable reciprocity, individual reputation, strategic coalition choice, and socially transmitted conventions may require richer memory and learning. Visual complexity is not a reliable measure of cognitive complexity.

**Genesis implication.** Maintain a ladder of controller and memory capacities and test the minimum sufficient system. Compare reactive agents with identical embodiment to learning agents. Attribute complexity to demonstrated causal requirements, not appearance.

### 17.28 Open-endedness versus engineered affordances

Artificial-life researchers disagree over how much structure can be supplied before emergence becomes design. Too little structure yields sterile worlds; too much yields scripted solutions. Actions such as transfer, attach, strike, carry, and signal are authored affordances, but labels such as ally, owner, raid, or faction would author social meaning.

**Genesis implication.** Author conserved materials, costs, local perception, mutable objects, and consequences. Keep categories in the observer. Positive controls may temporarily simplify tasks, but production claims must return to neutral affordances.

### 17.29 How disputes should shape reporting

For every result, separate:

- **observation:** what events and patterns occurred;
- **operational classification:** which preregistered criteria were met;
- **mechanistic inference:** which ablations or counterfactuals support a cause;
- **evolutionary explanation:** why the behavior increased under the modeled selection regime;
- **analogy:** how, if at all, it resembles a biological or human phenomenon.

Disagreement at the analogy or explanatory level should not erase a well-supported mechanistic result, and a compelling analogy should not substitute for causal evidence.

## 18. Risks of overinterpreting behavior

Artificial-life worlds are unusually vulnerable to compelling but false narratives. The observer sees trajectories after the fact, knows the experimental goal, and can assign meaning to any repeated pattern. The system therefore needs explicit epistemic safeguards.

### 18.1 Aggregation is not a social group

Agents can cluster because they independently seek the same food, temperature, shelter, mate, path bottleneck, or low-cost terrain. A group claim requires social dependence: removing or hiding conspecific cues should change cohesion, or partner identities should persist beyond the shared environmental cause.

**Minimum safeguard:** compare with resource- and path-conditioned nulls; relocate the attractor; test whether associations follow partners or place.

### 18.2 Proximity is not affiliation

Persistent closeness can reflect crowding, obstruction, predation, inability to disperse, or dominance exclusion. Affiliation implies a differentiated tendency to approach, remain near, follow, tolerate, aid, or reunite with particular partners relative to opportunity.

**Minimum safeguard:** normalize by co-presence and movement constraints; include approach/avoidance direction; test reunion after controlled separation.

### 18.3 Network communities are not ontological groups

Most community-detection algorithms produce a partition even for weakly structured data. Results vary with edge definition, window length, resolution, and random initialization. A partition can also be circular if aggression is used to define groups and then “between-group aggression” is measured.

**Minimum safeguard:** infer communities primarily from nonaggressive affiliation or association layers; report modularity/evidence against nulls; test multiple algorithms and windows; keep aggression as an external validation layer.

### 18.4 Related individuals together are not kin recognition

Limited dispersal automatically places relatives near one another. Kin-biased interaction requires more contact or aid than expected from spatial opportunity, or a response to a cue correlated with kinship.

**Minimum safeguard:** opportunity matching, cross-fostering/newborn relocation, cue scrambling, and comparison of pedigree, familiarity, and phenotype.

### 18.5 Similarity-based behavior is not necessarily kin selection

A tag or phenotype may correlate with strategy, location, resource niche, or ancestry. Helping similar agents can be green-beard selection, assortative mutualism, or learned convention rather than genome-wide kin discrimination.

**Minimum safeguard:** measure linkage to causal social loci, recombination, pedigree, and cue manipulability separately.

### 18.6 Symmetric exchange is not reciprocity

Two agents may alternately obtain resources because production fluctuates, because each takes when able, or because both prefer a mutualistic joint task. Reciprocity requires partner-specific contingency: past receipt or action changes future behavior after controlling for state and opportunity.

**Minimum safeguard:** lagged models, partner permutation, memory ablation, and checkpoint branches that alter one prior act.

### 18.7 Resource transfer is not altruistic sharing

A transfer can be accidental spill, failed defense, theft, payment, mating investment, parental provisioning, manipulation, or mutual exchange. Actor cost and recipient benefit must be measured counterfactually, and voluntary control must be established.

**Minimum safeguard:** distinguish give, tolerate, drop, steal, lose, and jointly consume at the primitive event level; use possession and counterfactual energy trajectories.

### 18.8 Joint benefit is not cooperation if no alternative exists

Two agents may be mechanically required to activate an object, but if the engine explicitly exposes a “cooperate” affordance or assigns paired roles, the result may be task compliance rather than evolved cooperation.

**Minimum safeguard:** neutral physical actions, alternative solo and exploitative strategies, variable partner availability, and evolution from initially uncoordinated controllers.

### 18.9 Reduced cheating is not punishment

Cheaters may disappear because they are less efficient, spatially excluded by resource use, unable to reproduce, or avoided. Aggression after cheating may be generalized arousal rather than contingent enforcement.

**Minimum safeguard:** classify the actual stabilizer—partner choice, assortment, direct retaliation, third-party intervention, policing, or ecological disadvantage—through targeted ablations.

### 18.10 Repeated task differences are not division of labor

One organism may remain near a resource and another near a structure because of location, age, body condition, or path dependence. Division of labor implies persistent specialization plus functional complementarity or increased collective output.

**Minimum safeguard:** role swaps, relocation, state covariates, specialist removal, task-transition entropy, and performance comparisons.

### 18.11 Home-range separation is not territoriality

Agents may use different regions because resources, birth sites, or barriers differ. Territory requires some combination of site fidelity, exclusion, active defense, marking, patrol, resident advantage, or learned occupancy expectations.

**Minimum safeguard:** resource swaps, resident removal, intrusion experiments, neutral-site contests, and measurement of costly defense.

### 18.12 Aggression near a site is not boundary defense

Attack rates can be high wherever density or valuable resources are high. Boundary defense requires a spatially organized response to intrusion into a repeatedly occupied core or border, ideally with resident-specific behavior and costs.

**Minimum safeguard:** compare matched encounters inside, outside, and near inferred boundaries while controlling for resource value and local numerical balance.

### 18.13 Markers are not property claims

A deposited trace may attract, repel, communicate depletion, or simply be waste. Calling it a territorial marker requires a relationship among deposit, occupancy, receiver behavior, contest outcome, and site persistence.

**Minimum safeguard:** erase, transplant, forge, and playback markers; separate producer presence from trace; test whether response is learned or inherited.

### 18.14 Attack overlap is not coordination

Several agents can attack the same target because all perceive it at once or because the target enters a crowded area. Coordination requires conditional dependence, recruitment, role differentiation, target convergence, or joint efficacy beyond independent responses.

**Minimum safeguard:** common-stimulus and independent-hazard nulls, observation ablation, signal playback, ally removal, and temporal-dependence analysis.

### 18.15 Group-correlated aggression is not coalitionary aggression

Members of one inferred community can independently be aggressive toward outsiders without acting together. Coalitionary aggression requires at least two attackers whose participation is socially coupled in one harmful episode.

**Minimum safeguard:** demonstrate that one actor’s presence, action, or signal changes another’s attack probability or target selection.

### 18.16 Coalitionary aggression is not organized inter-group conflict

A single joint attack can be opportunistic predation-like behavior, mobbing, or transient defense. Organized inter-group conflict requires persistent social units, repeated opponent-correlated episodes, coordination, and consequences such as exclusion, resource transfer, territorial change, deterrence, casualty patterns, or stable tactics.

**Minimum safeguard:** preregister recurrence horizon and group-persistence criteria; trace the same community pair through time; measure material consequences.

### 18.17 Lethal events are not “war” by default

The term “war” carries assumptions about identity, institutions, intent, planning, norms, and political organization. Artificial agents can meet an operational definition of repeated organized inter-group violence without any of those features.

**Minimum safeguard:** use narrower terms in primary analysis. Any “war-like” analogy must list both matched and absent properties.

### 18.18 Numerical advantage does not prove tactical assessment

Attacks may occur more often at favorable ratios because larger parties happen to encounter more targets, not because agents estimate risk or recruit allies.

**Minimum safeguard:** control encounter opportunity, manipulate visible ally number, hide selected allies, and test whether the same organism changes commitment before contact.

### 18.19 Retreat is not deterrence

Agents may leave because of injury, low energy, pathfinding, resource depletion, or controller noise. Deterrence implies that anticipated future cost changes behavior before or without direct harm.

**Minimum safeguard:** marker/signal playback, sham defenders, remembered opponent tests, and comparison before versus after observed attacks.

### 18.20 Revenge is not repeated target contact

Opponent-specific attacks after prior harm may arise because both parties occupy the same place or because injury changes movement. Revenge is an intentional human concept that should not be attributed from sequence alone.

**Minimum safeguard:** report “delayed opponent-specific retaliation”; manipulate memory and spatial association; test target substitution and time delay.

### 18.21 Dominance is not leadership

A strong individual may displace others but have no causal influence on collective movement or action selection. Conversely, an informed low-ranking individual may lead movement without dominance.

**Minimum safeguard:** measure separate dominance, initiation, following, information, and removal effects.

### 18.22 Centrality is not influence

Highly central organisms may be central because others choose them or because they occupy a busy location. Their removal may disrupt the network through geometry rather than information or leadership.

**Minimum safeguard:** matched-location removals, trajectory counterfactuals, temporal precedence, and controlled playback of their signals/actions.

### 18.23 Signal correlation is not communication

A signal can correlate with danger because both are caused by the same event. Communication requires that receivers perceive the signal and alter behavior, and ideally that production or response affects fitness.

**Minimum safeguard:** unheard and playback controls, signal-channel remapping, causal response estimates, and sender/receiver payoff analysis.

### 18.24 Stable signal variants are not symbolic language

Local conventions can be arbitrary and learned without compositional syntax, displacement, reference, or semantics comparable to language. A repeated alarm-like mapping is communication, not necessarily a symbol system.

**Minimum safeguard:** describe channel, context, response, arbitrariness, productivity, and generalization explicitly; avoid language claims without compositional tests.

### 18.25 Behavioral diffusion is not culture without alternatives

A behavior can spread because all agents independently discover the same high-payoff solution after the environment changes. Social exposure must predict adoption beyond common opportunity.

**Minimum safeguard:** demonstrator seeding, ghost controls, exposure-dose models, social-learning ablation, and equivalent arbitrary variants.

### 18.26 Geographic differences are not traditions

Communities may use different tactics because terrain, resources, body types, or inherited controllers differ. Long persistence makes ecological and genetic confounding worse, not better.

**Minimum safeguard:** ecology swap, cross-fostering/migration, genetic controls, and turnover. Culture should be the supported residual explanation, not the default label.

### 18.27 Persistence through time is not persistence through turnover

The same individuals can repeat a behavior for their entire lives. A tradition that outlives individuals requires transmission or externalized environmental inheritance.

**Minimum safeguard:** quantify performer replacement, learner chains, and behavior continuity across births, deaths, and migration.

### 18.28 Artifact continuity is not cultural transmission

A structure can persist physically and constrain later behavior even if no organism learns a practice from another. This is ecological inheritance or scaffolded behavior; it may be important, but it is not identical to social learning.

**Minimum safeguard:** separate behavior copied from observed agents from behavior elicited by inherited objects; remove demonstrators while preserving artifacts and vice versa.

### 18.29 A classifier label is not ground truth

Machine-learning classifiers can reproduce the biases of hand-labeled examples, drift across engine versions, and exploit incidental visual cues. High held-out accuracy within one seed family may not generalize.

**Minimum safeguard:** transparent primitive metrics, cross-seed and cross-version holdouts, adversarial examples, inter-rater checks, and provenance. Use classifiers to triage episodes, not define causal categories alone.

### 18.30 Visual review creates narrative bias

Observers are likely to notice dramatic coordinated-looking events and ignore thousands of null episodes. Camera angle, playback speed, and highlighted group colors can create apparent organization.

**Minimum safeguard:** analyze blinded event data first; sample videos by declared rules; include negative examples; render without inferred colors during primary coding.

### 18.31 Color-coding can create imaginary factions

A viewer that assigns stable colors to dynamic clusters encourages the human observer to perceive enduring teams even during fission, fusion, or weak association. If colors are exposed to organisms, the error becomes causal.

**Minimum safeguard:** label colors as observer overlays, show uncertainty and membership probabilities, allow overlays to be disabled, and never route them into perception.

### 18.32 Post hoc thresholds inflate discovery

Trying many group windows, cohesion thresholds, conflict definitions, and lag intervals until a desired pattern appears creates undisclosed multiple testing.

**Minimum safeguard:** preregister primary definitions, report sensitivity grids, correct exploratory searches, and confirm on untouched seeds.

### 18.33 Seed cherry-picking

Open-ended simulations naturally produce rare spectacular runs. Showing one is a demonstration of possibility, not evidence of frequency, robustness, or mechanism.

**Minimum safeguard:** publish the seed manifest, screening denominator, outcome distribution, failures, and exact selection rule. Distinguish existence proofs from population-level claims.

### 18.34 Survivor bias

Stable cooperative groups are easier to observe because failed or exploited groups disappear. Conditioning only on surviving groups can make cooperation look more effective and conflict less costly than it is.

**Minimum safeguard:** include extinction, failed formation, abandoned territories, and dissolved coalitions as outcomes; use competing-risk and lineage-survival analyses.

### 18.35 Extinction handling can reverse conclusions

Dropping extinct worlds, replacing them silently, or truncating before collapse biases treatment effects. A condition that generates spectacular cooperation in 10% of runs and extinction in 90% differs fundamentally from one producing moderate cooperation reliably.

**Minimum safeguard:** predefine extinction handling and report probability, timing, and cause alongside social metrics.

### 18.36 Pseudoreplication

Episodes within one evolutionary history share genomes, ecology, and institutions of interaction. Treating them as independent can produce arbitrarily small p-values.

**Minimum safeguard:** world-seed-level inference or hierarchical models with appropriate cluster levels; report independent seed count prominently.

### 18.37 Reward and fitness leakage

A hand-designed controller reward for “help allies,” “defend group territory,” or “attack outsiders” proves only that the behavior can be optimized under those labels. Even indirect fitness bonuses assigned to observer-defined cooperation or group success script the target.

**Minimum safeguard:** fitness must arise from energy, survival, reproduction, offspring success, and physical consequences. Observer classifications are diagnostic only.

### 18.38 Perception leakage

Providing exact relatedness, group membership, territory ownership, reputation, or enemy status turns difficult inference problems into direct inputs. Rich input names do not make them naturalistic.

**Minimum safeguard:** audit every controller feature and trace it to a physically or socially perceptible cue. Use oracle inputs only in explicitly labeled upper-bound controls.

### 18.39 Action leakage

Actions named `share`, `punish`, `recruit_ally`, `claim_territory`, or `declare_war` carry social semantics. Even when controllers choose them freely, the engine has encoded the institution.

**Minimum safeguard:** expose neutral primitives such as release, transfer force, emit channel, strike, block, attach, move, and modify. Infer function from context and consequences.

### 18.40 Environment leakage

A world with two colored resource zones, impermeable borders, and separate spawning pools may create two factions even without IDs. Scripted ecological partition can be as strong as a faction field.

**Minimum safeguard:** vary landscape continuity, migration corridors, resource identities, and birth locations; test whether social boundaries persist after ecological boundaries move or disappear.

### 18.41 Metric gaming by evolution

Once a metric affects selection, automated curriculum, or experiment continuation, organisms may exploit its implementation. Even observer-only adaptive screening can preferentially retain artifacts.

**Minimum safeguard:** keep primary social metrics outside fitness; use multiple independent measures; inspect counterexamples; validate on holdout definitions; record all adaptive decisions.

### 18.42 Deterministic replay does not establish causal truth

Exact replay proves implementation reproducibility, not that a label or theory is correct. A deterministic bug, biased metric, or scripted affordance will reproduce perfectly.

**Minimum safeguard:** combine replay with ablation, counterfactual branching, independent implementations where possible, and conceptual validity checks.

### 18.43 Sensitivity to arbitrary engine constants

A result may depend on one attack cooldown, field decay constant, perception radius, or memory size. That can be a real phase transition, but it limits generality.

**Minimum safeguard:** map response surfaces, nondimensionalize key ratios, and identify broad regimes rather than one tuned point.

### 18.44 Controller exploit versus intended physics

Agents may exploit collision tunneling, overflow, duplicate transfers, marker saturation, save/load discrepancies, or action timing to create apparent tactics.

**Minimum safeguard:** invariants, adversarial testing, resource conservation checks, contact diagnostics, exploit-focused replay review, and version-specific result provenance.

### 18.45 Hidden human intervention

Manual seed selection, terrain edits, organism relocation, or removal of “bad” agents can become an unreported designer influence.

**Minimum safeguard:** encode all interventions as timestamped external commands in the replay stream; report them; distinguish discovery runs from untouched experiments.

### 18.46 Anthropomorphic mental-state attribution

Terms such as trust, loyalty, fear, hatred, prestige, intention, and strategy can be useful shorthand but imply internal states not measured by action sequences.

**Minimum safeguard:** pair shorthand with an operational definition. Prefer “learned positive partner valence” to trust, “delayed opponent-specific retaliation” to revenge, and “initiation influence” to command unless internal representations are directly analyzed.

### 18.47 Human analogy inflation

A sequence from grouping to territory to coalition attack can tempt claims about tribes, politics, civilization, or war. Those categories include institutions, norms, symbolic identity, demographic organization, and historical meaning absent from the engine.

**Minimum safeguard:** state which components are present, absent, and merely analogous. The research target is emergence of social organization under specified mechanics, not a compressed reenactment of human history.

### 18.48 Negative results are informative

Failure to produce cooperation or organized conflict is not necessarily an engine failure. It may show that commonly asserted sufficient conditions are not sufficient, that cognition is inadequate, that costs dominate, or that the phenomenon is rare.

**Minimum safeguard:** report null regimes, extinction regimes, and failed transitions; use positive controls to separate lack of capacity from lack of selection.

### 18.49 Recommended claim ladder

Use the strongest claim justified by evidence, and no stronger:

| Evidence | Permissible claim |
|---|---|
| Spatial clustering above environmental null | Socially influenced aggregation |
| Persistent preferential associations | Stable affiliative grouping |
| Relatedness effect beyond opportunity | Kin-biased association or behavior |
| Actor cost and recipient benefit | Cooperative act under stated fitness accounting |
| Partner-specific lagged contingency | Reciprocity-like contingency |
| Repeated site use plus active exclusion | Territorial behavior |
| Targeted harmful action | Individual aggression |
| Outsider bias without joint dependence | Group-correlated aggression |
| Socially dependent multi-attacker episode | Coordinated coalitionary aggression |
| Persistent communities, recurrence, coordination, and collective consequences | Repeated organized inter-group conflict |
| Socially transmitted, community-specific variant across turnover | Conflict-related tradition |

This ladder should appear in reports and observer tooling so that visually dramatic evidence cannot silently upgrade the claim.

## 19. Open questions

The following questions are unresolved enough that Genesis should treat them as research targets rather than design assumptions. Each can be converted into a parameter sweep, staged capability test, or competing-model experiment.

### 19.1 What is the minimum social memory for differentiated relationships?

Can simple familiarity plus one decaying valence support stable affiliation, partner choice, and retaliation, or are action-specific histories required? How does the threshold depend on group size, encounter rate, cue ambiguity, and turnover?

**Proposed test:** evolve identical populations across a memory-capacity ladder and compare out-of-sample relationship persistence, cheater invasion, and controller complexity.

### 19.2 Is stable individual recognition necessary?

Fission–fusion groups and reciprocity seem to favor individual recognition, but cue classes, location, or recent co-presence may suffice. Stable IDs are computationally convenient but biologically strong.

**Proposed test:** compare immutable perceptual signatures, drifting phenotypes, familiarity without identity, and anonymous local traces while holding memory budget constant.

### 19.3 How much cue dimensionality prevents faction-like shortcuts?

A one-bit tag creates a direct partition. A high-dimensional phenotype may be harder to learn and easier to exploit through mimicry. The minimum useful dimensionality and mutation structure are unknown.

**Proposed test:** sweep cue dimension, heritability, noise, linkage, and production cost; measure assortment, deception, cue polymorphism, and collapse into binary camps.

### 19.4 Can kin-biased cooperation emerge without explicit recognition?

Limited dispersal and local interaction can create pedigree assortment even when organisms cannot identify kin. Is that sufficient under Genesis demography, or does local kin competition cancel the benefit?

**Proposed test:** cross offspring dispersal, replacement radius, resource competition scale, and cue availability; compare inclusive-fitness and multilevel decompositions.

### 19.5 When does familiarity outperform kinship as a social rule?

In dynamic groups, familiar nonkin may be better partners than unfamiliar kin. Selection may favor relationship history over ancestry.

**Proposed test:** vary migration, adoption/cross-fostering, interaction reliability, and environmental turnover; track evolved weighting of pedigree-correlated cues versus learned outcomes.

### 19.6 What is the weakest cooperation-supporting ecology?

Does cooperation require a deliberately joint task, or can it arise from generic movement, object possession, predation risk, or construction affordances? A too-obvious two-agent switch would script the answer.

**Proposed test:** compare byproduct mutualism, divisible resource handling, transport constraints, shared shelter, and mechanically complementary object manipulation without special social rewards.

### 19.7 Which cheater-control mechanism evolves first?

Assortment, partner choice, withdrawal, direct retaliation, policing, punishment, and reputation have different cognitive and ecological requirements.

**Proposed test:** begin with all neutral physical options available and classify which stabilizer appears; then ablate each in turn and run invasion assays.

### 19.8 Can resource sharing emerge without a dedicated transfer action?

Dropping, releasing, partial consumption, tolerated access, and co-carrying may be enough. A direct transfer primitive is cleaner but may overauthor sharing.

**Proposed test:** compare physical release/possession mechanics with an abstract recipient-directed transfer upper bound. Measure accidental transfer, tolerated theft, actor control, and learning difficulty.

### 19.9 How should possession be represented?

A binary owner field is efficient but can imply property. Fully physical contact may be expensive and unstable. A neutral holder relation may be acceptable if it represents mechanical control rather than legal ownership.

**Proposed test:** compare grasp constraints, inventory-like slots with physical access, and free objects; verify that others can contest, steal, receive, or jointly manipulate them.

### 19.10 Can territory emerge without markers or damage?

Repeated occupancy, spatial memory, blocking, and resource depletion might create stable exclusive use. If not, which addition—markers, displacement, injury, or structures—is minimal?

**Proposed test:** staged factorial beginning with occupancy and memory, then adding generic traces, blocking, noninjurious displacement, and finally damage.

### 19.11 What creates a true ownership convention?

Resident advantage can come from information and motivation rather than convention. Can role-contingent “resident persists, intruder retreats” behavior evolve when combat strength is equal and residence changes experimentally?

**Proposed test:** frequent role swaps, neutral-site contests, movable resources, and marker transplant, with explicit estimates of role-dependent policy.

### 19.12 How many marker channels are useful?

One generic trace may support occupancy prediction. Multiple channels could enable identity, status, route, or resource information but can become a symbolic or faction system.

**Proposed test:** sweep 0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 initially meaningless channels with costs; quantify evolved information, convention diversity, deception, and compute burden.

### 19.13 Should markers carry producer-specific information?

Producer identity supports recognition and deterrence, but exact identity fields are direct and expensive. Aggregate chemical mixtures or phenotypic signatures may be more naturalistic.

**Proposed test:** compare generic occupancy intensity, low-dimensional heritable signature, noisy individual signature, and exact-ID oracle.

### 19.14 How persistent should environmental memory be?

Long persistence supports territories and traditions but can freeze historical accidents, saturate the world, and create computational debt. Short persistence may make culture impossible.

**Proposed test:** express decay relative to movement time, organism lifespan, generation time, and site-renewal cycle; identify phase changes in boundary stability and convention inheritance.

### 19.15 What damage model produces meaningful contest economics?

Instant death strongly selects avoidance and numerical advantage; trivial recoverable damage can produce spam attacks. Injury should create graded costs, recovery, and opportunity tradeoffs.

**Proposed test:** vary wound accumulation, recovery, impairment, action latency, miss risk, and permanent loss while holding expected direct damage constant.

### 19.16 Can coalitionary defense emerge before offensive aggression?

Alarm and local convergence may produce mobbing or defense more readily than raids. This would undermine any assumed linear path from individual attack to offensive war.

**Proposed test:** vary intruder behavior, site value, target vulnerability, pursuit distance, and ability to capture resources; classify defense and pursuit separately.

### 19.17 What is the weakest recruitment mechanism?

Visual convergence on an attacker may coordinate allies without signals. Local arousal signals may recruit but not direct. Directional or target-referential signals are stronger and more language-like.

**Proposed test:** sequentially enable observation of attacks, generic alarm, sender bearing, target bearing, persistent marker, and learned multi-channel conventions.

### 19.18 Do organisms need to estimate numerical advantage?

Coalitional attacks could emerge from simple quorum thresholds, local social facilitation, or individual risk reduction without explicit counting.

**Proposed test:** manipulate ally visibility and spatial arrangement while preserving actual numbers; inspect controller sensitivity and use adversarial configurations where density cues conflict with count.

### 19.19 How does coalition free riding evolve?

Potential allies may benefit from a rival’s defeat without joining or taking injury. What keeps participation stable—kinship, reciprocal support, reputation, immediate spoils, coercion, or risk dilution?

**Proposed test:** log participation opportunities and payoffs, introduce low-participation mutants, and ablate benefit sharing, recognition, and future partner choice.

### 19.20 Can coalitions remain stable across changing tasks?

A defensive coalition may not persist in foraging, migration, or reproduction. Human-like alliances are multifunctional, but many animal coalitions are context-specific.

**Proposed test:** compare network overlap across affiliation, aid, movement, defense, attack, and sharing layers; perturb one context and observe transfer.

### 19.21 What generates fission and fusion rather than static groups?

Resource patch size, travel cost, information, predation, internal competition, mating, and social memory may create dynamic grouping. Static resource islands can manufacture static factions.

**Proposed test:** vary resource pulse, patch capacity, corridor geometry, and communication; measure event-triggered fission/fusion and identity continuity.

### 19.22 How should group persistence be defined through fission and fusion?

No single label rule is obviously correct. Maximum-overlap tracking may treat one daughter as the original group; lineage-aware tracking may preserve both; network-flow models may represent ancestry.

**Proposed test:** report multiple identity conventions and an explicit group genealogy. Keep all observer labels noncausal.

### 19.23 What counts as a group-level consequence of conflict?

Territorial expansion, resource capture, demographic loss, migration, dominance, and deterrence differ. Destruction of competitors can increase access without an explicit transfer.

**Proposed test:** build a causal consequence vector rather than a binary win/loss label and compare pre/post counterfactual trajectories.

### 19.24 When is resource capture favored over competitor destruction?

Transportable resources, reusable structures, and defensible sites reward capture; renewable dispersed resources may make exclusion less useful. Destruction can also damage the prize.

**Proposed test:** vary resource transportability, structure fragility, renewal, and post-conflict occupancy while keeping attack capability constant.

### 19.25 What creates repeated opponent-specific conflict?

Spatial adjacency alone can cause recurrence. Memory, retaliation, contested sites, signal conventions, and demographic replacement may strengthen or weaken it.

**Proposed test:** relocate group cores, scramble individual memory, preserve or erase markers, and compare recurrence conditional on continuing encounter opportunity.

### 19.26 Can deterrence emerge without reputation?

Direct experience, visible injury, markers, and observation of attacks may be enough. A global reputation system would be overpowered.

**Proposed test:** separate direct victim memory, third-party observation, persistent traces, and communicated signals; use novel migrants and naive juveniles as probes.

### 19.27 How do migration and group fusion affect conflict?

Migrants can transfer cues and tactics, reduce between-group relatedness, create bridges, or become targets. Fusion can terminate conflict or import feuds.

**Proposed test:** manipulate migration rate and migrant age, preserve exact social histories, and measure acceptance, cue convergence, tactic diffusion, and opponent generalization.

### 19.28 Can interdependence create cross-cutting ties that suppress conflict?

Shared resources or complementary skills may link individuals across communities and weaken binary boundaries. Alternatively, networks may polarize into competing blocs.

**Proposed test:** create physical complementarity that does not respect community structure; measure multiplex ties, attack inhibition, and whether bridges survive scarcity shocks.

### 19.29 How do resource shocks affect existing organization?

Acute scarcity may dissolve groups, strengthen sharing, intensify exclusion, trigger migration, or create coalitions. Outcomes likely depend on prior institutions and memory.

**Proposed test:** apply matched temporary shocks after social systems stabilize; branch from identical checkpoints; compare recovery, fission, mortality, and conflict.

### 19.30 Can conflict-related traditions arise without imitation?

Tactics may be inherited genetically, induced by artifacts, or learned individually from repeated ecological feedback. Social learning is only one transmission channel.

**Proposed test:** separately disable neural social updates, remove artifacts, shuffle genotypes, and expose naive agents to demonstrations without outcomes.

### 19.31 What fidelity is needed for cumulative improvement?

Low-fidelity imitation can sustain broad conventions but may not preserve multi-step tactics. High fidelity could make cumulative change possible yet computationally and cognitively costly.

**Proposed test:** vary observation detail, action segmentation, memory, and learner reconstruction; measure retention of functional substeps and improvement over model performance.

### 19.32 Can conflict traditions remain group-specific under migration?

Too little migration prevents transmission tests; too much erases differentiation. Conformity, markers, and partner choice may maintain boundaries.

**Proposed test:** map cultural differentiation against migration and learning bias while controlling genetic flow and ecology.

### 19.33 How can causal social learning be inferred from event logs alone?

Observation and adoption are endogenous: agents observe those near them and approach behaviors they already prefer. Network diffusion can therefore overstate transmission.

**Proposed test:** randomized demonstrator placement, exogenous playback, instrumental environmental visibility, and checkpoint branches that remove specific demonstrations.

### 19.34 How much neuromodulation is necessary?

Learning when to associate cues with reward, harm, or social outcomes may require modulatory signals. Too rich a learning system can memorize individuals and shortcut evolution.

**Proposed test:** compare fixed plasticity, reward-modulated plasticity, event-specific modulators, and no lifetime learning under equal state budgets.

### 19.35 Should social learning itself evolve?

Fixed imitation rules can create culture by design. Evolvable attention and plasticity are more open-ended but harder to optimize and validate.

**Proposed test:** begin with a minimal generic observation-learning substrate whose rates and biases are genetic; compare with fixed unbiased copying as a capability control.

### 19.36 What are the energetic costs of cognition and communication?

Without costs, memory, perception, signaling, and neural size may expand monotonically. Real organisms face metabolic, developmental, latency, and error tradeoffs.

**Proposed test:** impose separate, measurable costs for sensor range, memory capacity, signal amplitude, neural connections, and plastic updates; map which social mechanisms remain viable.

### 19.37 How should species and reproductive isolation be handled?

Long evolutionary runs may generate reproductive clusters. Species labels must remain observer-derived, but mate compatibility is a physical/genetic mechanism. Social boundaries may align with or cut across reproductive isolation.

**Proposed test:** infer reproductive networks offline; compare social assortment before and after compatibility evolves; never expose species classification as a controller input.

### 19.38 Can social organization bridge species boundaries?

Mutualism, commensalism, predation, and shared structures can produce mixed-species associations. Restricting social memory or signals to conspecifics would preclude this.

**Proposed test:** make perception capability trait-based rather than species-gated; measure cross-lineage exchange, signal learning, and coalition behavior.

### 19.39 How robust are results to controller architecture?

A mechanism may appear under one neural encoding because of inductive bias rather than ecology. Reactive networks, recurrent networks, plastic networks, and modular controllers may differ sharply.

**Proposed test:** replicate core experiments across controller classes with comparable parameter and compute budgets; report architecture × mechanism interactions.

### 19.40 How robust are results to evolutionary algorithm details?

Selection intensity, mutation rate, recombination, population regulation, and reproduction timing can determine social assortment and innovation.

**Proposed test:** sensitivity analysis across demography and mutation architecture; distinguish ecological conclusions from optimizer-specific effects.

### 19.41 How should rare innovations be preserved without scripting them?

Open-ended systems may lose promising coordination behaviors before they become beneficial. Novelty search, quality diversity, or lineage archives can preserve variation, but external selection objectives can distort natural evolution.

**Proposed test:** compare purely ecological selection with nonintervening archives, island models, and explicitly labeled research modes using diversity maintenance. Never feed observer social labels into organism fitness.

### 19.42 Can population structure alone create enduring cultural boundaries?

Assortative social learning and limited migration may be sufficient, or active conformity and identity signaling may be needed.

**Proposed test:** cross network modularity, migration, copying bias, and signal plasticity using neutral behavior variants.

### 19.43 What phase transitions should be expected?

Grouping, cooperation, territoriality, and conflict may arise abruptly when dimensionless ratios cross thresholds: benefit-to-cost, resource value-to-defense cost, future interaction probability, ally advantage-to-injury risk, or marker lifetime-to-return time.

**Proposed test:** identify and report nondimensional ratios, hysteresis, finite-size effects, and critical slowing rather than fitting only monotonic trends.

### 19.44 How can organized conflict be prevented from becoming an optimization attractor?

If killing cheaply releases resources, removes competitors, and has little risk, aggression may dominate for trivial reasons. The goal is possibility, not inevitability.

**Proposed test:** enforce conservation and realistic opportunity costs, make injury consequential, include escape and coexistence, and map peaceful as well as violent equilibria. Do not reward conflict directly.

### 19.45 What peaceful social complexity can emerge under the same substrate?

A conflict-focused program can overlook exchange, adoption, joint construction, migration networks, and cross-group traditions.

**Proposed test:** maintain outcome-neutral metrics and treatments where attack is absent or costly; compare whether the same recognition, memory, signaling, and structure mechanisms support nonviolent organization.

### 19.46 How should observer uncertainty be represented?

Group boundaries, coalition membership, territory, and culture may be probabilistic. Hard labels conceal ambiguity and encourage causal leakage.

**Proposed test:** store posterior or ensemble membership probabilities, confidence intervals, and alternative partitions; visualize uncertainty explicitly.

### 19.47 What constitutes replication across engine versions?

A result may disappear after physics, controller, or persistence changes. Exact old replay and conceptual replication are different.

**Proposed test:** retain versioned binaries/checkpoints for exact replay; rerun hypotheses under new versions with matched dimensionless parameters; report both continuity and changed assumptions.

### 19.48 Can formal causal models remain tractable at scale?

Full counterfactual inference over evolving populations is difficult because treatment changes future composition. Short-term causal effects and long-term evolutionary selection effects require different estimands.

**Proposed test:** define intervention horizons; use checkpoint randomization for proximal mechanisms and independent-seed treatment assignment for evolutionary outcomes; avoid combining them into one causal number.

### 19.49 What is the minimum evidence for an existence claim?

One exact replay can prove that the engine permits a trajectory, but only if the classification is valid and the behavior was not hand-seeded. Robust possibility may require multiple independent origins.

**Proposed test:** report whether a result is a single existence proof, repeated de novo emergence, stable evolutionary regime, or prevalent outcome. Require at least one untouched holdout origin for strong emergence claims.

### 19.50 What would falsify the broader Genesis social program?

A scientific program needs failure conditions. Examples include:

- no social cue or memory treatment outperforms environmental aggregation nulls;
- cooperation appears only under direct social rewards or explicit paired tasks;
- kin bias requires an exact relatedness oracle;
- territory requires stored ownership flags;
- coalition detection collapses under common-stimulus nulls;
- conflict recurs only because maps permanently partition populations;
- traditions track genotype or ecology after all feasible controls;
- results require one tuned seed or unstable engine artifact;
- strict deterministic execution cannot be maintained at the necessary scale.

Such results would not prove social emergence impossible. They would falsify particular proposed minimal mechanisms and force redesign rather than semantic relabeling.

## 20. Annotated bibliography

This bibliography prioritizes works that materially inform the engineering choices, measurement rules, experimental controls, or theoretical disputes in this report. It is selective rather than exhaustive. Primary studies are included alongside reviews and books that clarify mechanisms. An annotation describes why each source matters for Genesis; inclusion does not imply that every conclusion is uncontested.

### 20.1 Social evolution, selection, and cooperation foundations

1. **Hamilton, W. D. (1964). “The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour. I.” *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 7: 1–16. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(64)90038-4.**  Establishes the formal logic later summarized by Hamilton’s rule and remains foundational for analyzing how effects on relatives can favor costly social behavior. For Genesis, it motivates logging pedigree, actor costs, recipient benefits, and competitive scale without giving agents an exact relatedness oracle.

2. **Hamilton, W. D. (1964). “The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour. II.” *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 7: 17–52. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(64)90039-6.**  Extends the framework to population structure and social interactions. It is particularly relevant to distinguishing genealogical relatedness from realized assortment and to recognizing that local competition can alter expected outcomes.

3. **Trivers, R. L. (1971). “The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism.” *Quarterly Review of Biology* 46: 35–57. https://doi.org/10.1086/406755.**  Classic account of contingent cooperation among nonkin. It identifies repeated interaction, partner discrimination, and control of cheating as central conditions, making it a direct source for the social-memory and reciprocity experiments.

4. **Maynard Smith, J., and Price, G. R. (1973). “The Logic of Animal Conflict.” *Nature* 246: 15–18. https://doi.org/10.1038/246015a0.**  Introduced evolutionary game-theoretic analysis of contests. It supports modeling aggression as a cost-sensitive strategy rather than an automatic response to competition.

5. **Maynard Smith, J., and Parker, G. A. (1976). “The Logic of Asymmetric Contests.” *Animal Behaviour* 24: 159–175. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0003-3472(76)80110-8.**  Shows how ownership, resource value, and fighting ability can create asymmetric contest outcomes. This is central to resident–intruder controls and to separating conventions from physical or motivational asymmetry.

6. **Axelrod, R., and Hamilton, W. D. (1981). “The Evolution of Cooperation.” *Science* 211: 1390–1396. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7466396.**  Demonstrates the importance of repeated games and conditional strategies. Its simplified tournament setting is useful as a capability benchmark but should not be mistaken for an embodied ecology.

7. **Frank, S. A. (1998). *Foundations of Social Evolution*. Princeton University Press.**  Develops a broad selection framework connecting kin selection, group structure, and quantitative genetics. Useful for post hoc decomposition of Genesis runs and for avoiding overly narrow interpretations of “group selection.”

8. **Okasha, S. (2006). *Evolution and the Levels of Selection*. Oxford University Press.**  A rigorous treatment of individual- and group-level selection language. It supports the recommendation to analyze the same organism-level mechanics through multiple partitions rather than implement a group-level force.

9. **Nowak, M. A. (2006). “Five Rules for the Evolution of Cooperation.” *Science* 314: 1560–1563. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1133755.**  Synthesizes kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity, and group selection in compact theoretical conditions. It is a useful map of candidate mechanisms, but Genesis should test their assumptions rather than treat the five-rule list as exhaustive or universally sufficient.

10. **West, S. A., Griffin, A. S., and Gardner, A. (2007). “Social Semantics: Altruism, Cooperation, Mutualism, Strong Reciprocity and Group Selection.” *Journal of Evolutionary Biology* 20: 415–432. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1420-9101.2006.01258.x.**  Clarifies frequently conflated terms using actor and recipient fitness effects. Its semantic discipline underlies this report’s insistence on distinguishing cooperation, mutual benefit, altruism, and enforcement.

11. **Traulsen, A., and Nowak, M. A. (2006). “Evolution of Cooperation by Multilevel Selection.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 103: 10952–10955. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0602530103.**  Provides a concrete model in which competition within and between groups can be partitioned. It is useful for designing fission, migration, and extinction analyses while emphasizing that group structure must arise from demography.

12. **Nowak, M. A., Tarnita, C. E., and Wilson, E. O. (2010). “The Evolution of Eusociality.” *Nature* 466: 1057–1062. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09205.**  Presents a controversial alternative framing of eusocial evolution and argues against the necessity of inclusive-fitness theory. It should be read with the subsequent rebuttal rather than as settled consensus.

13. **Abbot, P., et al. (2011). “Inclusive Fitness Theory and Eusociality.” *Nature* 471: E1–E4. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09831.**  A multi-author response defending inclusive-fitness theory and criticizing the assumptions and interpretation of Nowak, Tarnita, and Wilson. Together the two papers exemplify a theoretical dispute that Genesis can inform through explicit demographic and causal logs but cannot resolve by terminology alone.

14. **Clutton-Brock, T. (2009). “Cooperation between Non-Kin in Animal Societies.” *Nature* 462: 51–57. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08366.**  Reviews mutualism, reciprocity, coercion, and partner choice beyond close kin. It supports implementing multiple routes to cooperation rather than expecting kin selection to explain every stable social benefit.

15. **Melis, A. P., and Raihani, N. J. (2023). “The Cognitive Challenges of Cooperation in Human and Nonhuman Animals.” *Nature Reviews Psychology* 2: 523–536. https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-023-00207-7.**  Distinguishes cognitively simple cooperation among kin or interdependent partners from mechanisms requiring identity, memory, and contingent response. It directly supports the dependency-ordered cognition ladder proposed for Genesis.

16. **Efferson, C., Bernhard, H., Fischbacher, U., et al. (2024). “Super-Additive Cooperation.” *Nature* 626: 1034–1041. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07077-w.**  Examines interactions between repeated interaction and intergroup competition in human cooperation. Its relevance is methodological: cooperation mechanisms can combine nonadditively, so single-factor ablations may miss interaction effects.

### 20.2 Collective behavior, aggregation, leadership, and dynamic groups

17. **Reynolds, C. W. (1987). “Flocks, Herds and Schools: A Distributed Behavioral Model.” *Computer Graphics* 21(4): 25–34. https://doi.org/10.1145/37402.37406.**  The classic “boids” demonstration shows that coherent collective motion can emerge from local separation, alignment, and cohesion rules without leaders or group IDs. It is an important capability baseline and an equally important warning that visual organization can arise from simple reactive rules.

18. **Couzin, I. D., Krause, J., James, R., Ruxton, G. D., and Franks, N. R. (2002). “Collective Memory and Spatial Sorting in Animal Groups.” *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 218: 1–11. https://doi.org/10.1006/jtbi.2002.3065.**  Demonstrates how local behavioral zones can produce group-level motion, sorting, and memory-like persistence. It motivates reactive aggregation experiments before adding individual social memory.

19. **Couzin, I. D., Krause, J., Franks, N. R., and Levin, S. A. (2005). “Effective Leadership and Decision-Making in Animal Groups on the Move.” *Nature* 433: 513–516. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03236.**  Shows that a small informed minority can guide a group without explicit signaling of leadership. It supports observer-inferred influence and counterfactual removal rather than a leader role.

20. **Buhl, J., Sumpter, D. J. T., Couzin, I. D., et al. (2006). “From Disorder to Order in Marching Locusts.” *Science* 312: 1402–1406. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1125142.**  Empirically demonstrates density-dependent transitions from disordered to aligned collective motion. It motivates finite-size and density sweeps and warns that aggregation regimes can change abruptly.

21. **Sumpter, D. J. T. (2006). “The Principles of Collective Animal Behaviour.” *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 361: 5–22. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2005.1733.**  Reviews local interaction, positive feedback, response thresholds, inhibition, and information pooling. It offers a mechanism vocabulary for separating swarm-level coordination from cognition-rich social organization.

22. **Aureli, F., Schaffner, C. M., Boesch, C., et al. (2008). “Fission-Fusion Dynamics: New Research Frameworks.” *Current Anthropology* 49: 627–654. https://doi.org/10.1086/586708.**  Frames grouping as a dynamic process rather than a fixed categorical state. It supports temporal community inference, group genealogies, and explicit fission/fusion metrics.

23. **Lukeman, R., Li, Y.-X., and Edelstein-Keshet, L. (2010). “Inferring Individual Rules from Collective Behavior.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 107: 12576–12580. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1001763107.**  Addresses the inverse problem of inferring local interaction rules from group geometry. This is relevant to Genesis observer analysis and to the risk that multiple micro-mechanisms can produce similar macroscopic patterns.

24. **Nagy, M., Ákos, Z., Biro, D., and Vicsek, T. (2010). “Hierarchical Group Dynamics in Pigeon Flocks.” *Nature* 464: 890–893. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08891.**  Uses time-lagged directional correlations to infer hierarchical influence in flight. It informs leadership metrics while illustrating that directional influence need not mean authority or command.

25. **Cavagna, A., Cimarelli, A., Giardina, I., et al. (2010). “Scale-Free Correlations in Starling Flocks.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 107: 11865–11870. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1005766107.**  Reports long-range behavioral correlations in natural flocks. It is relevant to measuring collective responsiveness, but such correlation still requires causal controls before being called coordination.

26. **Palla, G., Barabási, A.-L., and Vicsek, T. (2007). “Quantifying Social Group Evolution.” *Nature* 446: 664–667. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05670.**  Develops methods to track communities through time and relates persistence to size and turnover. It supports dynamic observer labels and group ancestry rather than permanent membership fields.

27. **Mucha, P. J., Richardson, T., Macon, K., Porter, M. A., and Onnela, J.-P. (2010). “Community Structure in Time-Dependent, Multiscale, and Multiplex Networks.” *Science* 328: 876–878. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1184819.**  Introduces a multislice framework for temporal and multilayer community detection. Useful for combining association, aid, signaling, transfer, and aggression networks while retaining the warning that resolution parameters affect partitions.

28. **Whitehead, H. (2008). *Analyzing Animal Societies: Quantitative Methods for Vertebrate Social Analysis*. University of Chicago Press.**  A broad methodological guide to association indices, social differentiation, permutation tests, and network inference. It is valuable for building observer metrics that account for sampling and opportunity.

29. **Croft, D. P., James, R., and Krause, J. (2008). *Exploring Animal Social Networks*. Princeton University Press.**  Connects network methods to behavioral ecology. It supports multiplex, directed, and weighted social representations rather than a single group label.

30. **Farine, D. R., and Whitehead, H. (2015). “Constructing, Conducting and Interpreting Animal Social Network Analysis.” *Journal of Animal Ecology* 84: 1144–1163. https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2656.12418.**  Reviews design, data collection, null models, and inferential pitfalls in animal social networks. It directly supports opportunity-conditioned permutations and caution about nonindependence.

31. **Couzin, I. D. (2009). “Collective Cognition in Animal Groups.” *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 13: 36–43. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2008.10.002.**  Explains how information processing can occur at group level through local interactions. It informs shared-attention and informed-minority experiments without requiring a centralized group mind.

### 20.3 Recognition, kin discrimination, cues, and green-beard systems

32. **Getz, W. M., and Smith, K. B. (1983). “Genetic Kin Recognition: Honey Bees Discriminate between Full and Half Sisters.” *Nature* 302: 147–148. https://doi.org/10.1038/302147a0.**  Early experimental evidence for kin discrimination using biologically available cues. It illustrates that recognition is a perceptual mechanism, not direct access to pedigree.

33. **Holmes, W. G., and Sherman, P. W. (1982). “The Ontogeny of Kin Recognition in Two Species of Ground Squirrels.” *American Zoologist* 22: 491–517.**  Emphasizes development, association, and phenotype matching in kin recognition. Relevant to cross-fostering and the separation of familiarity from ancestry.

34. **Parr, L. A., and de Waal, F. B. M. (1999). “Visual Kin Recognition in Chimpanzees.” *Nature* 399: 647–648. https://doi.org/10.1038/21345.**  Provides evidence that facial phenotype can support kin discrimination. It motivates noisy phenotypic cue vectors rather than exact lineage inputs.

35. **Charpentier, M. J. E., Boulet, M., Drea, C. M., and others (2015). “Social Shaping of Voices Does Not Impair Phenotype Matching of Kinship in Mandrills.” *Nature Communications* 6: 7609. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms8609.**  Shows that socially influenced signals can still contain kin-correlated information. It is relevant to Genesis because learned signal convergence and inherited recognition cues may coexist.

36. **Keller, L., and Ross, K. G. (1998). “Selfish Genes: A Green Beard in the Red Fire Ant.” *Nature* 394: 573–575. https://doi.org/10.1038/29064.**  A prominent natural green-beard case linking a genetic cue to social discrimination. It demonstrates possibility while highlighting how unusually tight cue–behavior linkage may be required.

37. **Jansen, V. A. A., and van Baalen, M. (2006). “Altruism through Beard Chromodynamics.” *Nature* 440: 663–666. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04387.**  Models dynamic green-beard polymorphisms and the instability created by cheating and cue evolution. It supports separately encoded signal production and response with recombination and deception tests.

38. **Gruenheit, N., Parkinson, K., Stewart, B., et al. (2017). “A Polychromatic ‘Green-Beard’ Locus Determines Patterns of Cooperation in a Social Amoeba.” *Nature Communications* 8: 14171. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms14171.**  Empirically demonstrates a multiallelic recognition system affecting cooperation. It argues against reducing all tag systems to a single binary marker and motivates polymorphic cues.

39. **Axelrod, R., Hammond, R. A., and Grafen, A. (2004). “Altruism via Kin-Selection Strategies That Rely on Arbitrary Tags with Which They Coevolve.” *Evolution* 58: 1833–1838. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0014-3820.2004.tb00465.x.**  Shows how arbitrary tags and conditional behavior can coevolve. Its relevance is strongest as a recognition benchmark; direct perfect tags are more faction-like than the recommended Genesis implementation.

40. **Riolo, R. L., Cohen, M. D., and Axelrod, R. (2001). “Evolution of Cooperation without Reciprocity.” *Nature* 414: 441–443. https://doi.org/10.1038/35106555.**  A tag-similarity model in which cooperation evolves without repeated reciprocity. The result is historically influential but sensitive to assumptions, making it a model to reproduce and ablate rather than a general design law.

41. **Roberts, G., and Sherratt, T. N. (2002). “Does Similarity Breed Cooperation?” *Nature* 418: 499–500. https://doi.org/10.1038/418499b.**  Critiques the robustness and interpretation of similarity-based cooperation. It reinforces the need to test mutation, tolerance thresholds, and defectors rather than report a single tag model.

42. **Hammond, R. A., and Axelrod, R. (2006). “The Evolution of Ethnocentrism.” *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 50: 926–936. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022002706293470.**  Demonstrates in-group cooperation and out-group defection in a spatial tag model. It is directly relevant as a warning: low-dimensional inherited tags plus local reproduction can generate faction-like outcomes largely because the model supplies the group-recognition substrate.

43. **Scott, T. W., Grafen, A., and West, S. A. (2022). “Multiple Social Encounters Can Eliminate Crozier’s Paradox and Stabilise Genetic Kin Recognition.” *Nature Communications* 13: 3902. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-31545-4.**  Shows theoretically that partner search across multiple encounters can maintain tag diversity and stabilize genetic kin recognition. It highlights an interaction between recognition and partner choice that a one-shot Genesis encounter model would miss.

### 20.4 Reciprocity, partner choice, punishment, policing, and cheating

44. **Nowak, M. A., and May, R. M. (1992). “Evolutionary Games and Spatial Chaos.” *Nature* 359: 826–829. https://doi.org/10.1038/359826a0.**  Shows that spatial locality can sustain complex coexistence patterns in evolutionary games. It is foundational for spatial cooperation models but also demonstrates that outcomes depend strongly on update and neighborhood rules.

45. **Ohtsuki, H., Hauert, C., Lieberman, E., and Nowak, M. A. (2006). “A Simple Rule for the Evolution of Cooperation on Graphs and Social Networks.” *Nature* 441: 502–505. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04605.**  Derives a compact benefit-to-cost condition under specific graph and update assumptions. Genesis can use it as a sanity check, but mobility, resource ecology, and endogenous networks violate its simplifying assumptions.

46. **Taylor, P. D., Day, T., and Wild, G. (2007). “Evolution of Cooperation in a Finite Homogeneous Graph.” *Nature* 447: 469–472. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05784.**  Demonstrates how local competition can offset the benefits of graph-based assortment. It is an important counterweight to claims that clustering automatically promotes cooperation.

47. **Nowak, M. A., and Sigmund, K. (1998). “Evolution of Indirect Reciprocity by Image Scoring.” *Nature* 393: 573–577. https://doi.org/10.1038/31225.**  A canonical model of reputation-based cooperation. Its strong informational assumptions motivate treating global image scores only as an oracle control while building Genesis from local observation.

48. **Noë, R., and Hammerstein, P. (1994). “Biological Markets: Supply and Demand Determine the Effect of Partner Choice in Cooperation, Mutualism and Mating.” *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* 35: 1–11. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00167053.**  Formalizes partner choice and competition among potential partners. It supports giving agents the ability to leave, compare, and switch rather than requiring punishment as the primary response to cheating.

49. **Bshary, R., and Grutter, A. S. (2006). “Image Scoring and Cooperation in a Cleaner Fish Mutualism.” *Nature* 441: 975–978. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04755.**  Provides experimental evidence that observers use service information in a cleaner-client mutualism. It offers a biologically grounded model of local reputation-like partner choice without a universal public score.

50. **Bshary, R., Grutter, A. S., Willener, A. S. T., and Leimar, O. (2008). “Pairs of Cooperating Cleaner Fish Provide Better Service Quality than Singletons.” *Nature* 455: 964–966. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature07184.**  Shows that social context and partner monitoring can improve cooperative service. It is relevant to joint performance, policing, and the possibility that a second cooperator changes cheating incentives.

51. **Rockenbach, B., and Milinski, M. (2006). “The Efficient Interaction of Indirect Reciprocity and Costly Punishment.” *Nature* 444: 718–723. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05229.**  Demonstrates that reputation and punishment can interact in human experiments. For Genesis, it supports factorial tests rather than assuming additive independent mechanisms.

52. **Grinsted, L., and Field, J. (2017). “Market Forces Influence Helping Behaviour in Cooperatively Breeding Paper Wasps.” *Nature Communications* 8: 13750. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13750.**  Provides evidence that availability of alternative social groups affects helping effort. It supports modeling outside options and migration as causal variables in cooperation.

53. **Ratnieks, F. L. W., and Visscher, P. K. (1989). “Worker Policing in the Honeybee.” *Nature* 342: 796–797. https://doi.org/10.1038/342796a0.**  A classic demonstration of worker policing in a eusocial system. It is useful for functional definitions of policing but should not be generalized to flexible groups without the reproductive structure that makes it adaptive.

54. **Diggle, S. P., Griffin, A. S., Campbell, G. S., and West, S. A. (2007). “Cooperation and Conflict in Quorum-Sensing Bacterial Populations.” *Nature* 450: 411–414. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature06279.**  Demonstrates social exploitation of signal-regulated public goods. It links signaling, density, cooperation, and cheating in a simple cognition-free system relevant to local public-goods experiments.

55. **Foster, K. R., Shaulsky, G., Strassmann, J. E., Queller, D. C., and Thompson, C. R. L. (2004). “Pleiotropy as a Mechanism to Stabilize Cooperation.” *Nature* 431: 693–696. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02894.**  Shows how genetic coupling between cooperative and private-benefit traits can limit cheating. It motivates pleiotropic genome experiments while warning that linkage architecture can determine social stability.

56. **Santorelli, L. A., Thompson, C. R. L., Villegas, E., et al. (2008). “Facultative Cheater Mutants Reveal the Genetic Complexity of Cooperation in Social Amoebae.” *Nature* 451: 1107–1110. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature06558.**  Demonstrates multiple genetic routes to context-dependent cheating. It supports diverse mutation operators and invasion tests rather than a single fixed “cheater allele.”

57. **Aktipis, C. A. (2004). “Know When to Walk Away: Contingent Movement and the Evolution of Cooperation.” *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 231: 249–260. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2004.06.020.**  Shows that leaving defectors can stabilize cooperation without sophisticated reciprocity. It is directly relevant to movement-mediated partner choice and the cheater-resistance ladder.

58. **Stallen, M., Snijder, L. L., Gross, J., et al. (2023). “Partner Choice and Cooperation in Social Dilemmas Can Increase Resource Inequality.” *Nature Communications* 14: 6432. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-42128-2.**  Shows that a cooperation-supporting mechanism can have distributional side effects. Genesis should therefore measure inequality, exclusion, and access rather than optimizing cooperation rate alone.

59. **Inaba, M., and Akiyama, E. (2023). “Evolution of Cooperation in Multiplex Networks through Asymmetry between Interaction and Replacement.” *Scientific Reports* 13: 9814. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-37074-4.**  Demonstrates that interaction and reproductive/replacement networks can have nontrivial asymmetric effects. It reinforces the need to log and manipulate social encounters and demographic replacement separately.

60. **Su, Q., McAvoy, A., and Plotkin, J. B. (2023). “Strategy Evolution on Dynamic Networks.” *Nature Computational Science* 3: 763–776. https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-023-00509-z.**  Provides modern theory for coevolving strategies and dynamic social networks. It is especially relevant because Genesis groups and partner networks should be endogenous rather than fixed graphs.

61. **Lee, N., Chae, S., Baek, S. K., et al. (2023). “Social Dilemma in Foraging Behavior and Evolution of Cooperation by Learning.” *Scientific Reports* 13: 21948. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-49368-8.**  An embodied spatial model in which cooperative group chasing faces exploitation and learning changes the evolutionary outcome. It provides a contemporary example of why biological and cultural transmission can favor different strategies.

### 20.5 Territoriality, resource defense, residence, and spatial memory

62. **Brown, J. L. (1964). “The Evolution of Diversity in Avian Territorial Systems.” *Wilson Bulletin* 76: 160–169.**  A foundational resource-defense perspective linking territorial systems to ecological benefits and costs. It supports treating defendability as conditional rather than assuming all valuable resources produce territory.

63. **Emlen, S. T., and Oring, L. W. (1977). “Ecology, Sexual Selection, and the Evolution of Mating Systems.” *Science* 197: 215–223. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.327542.**  Connects spatial and temporal distribution of resources and mates to monopolization potential. Relevant to reproductive opportunity, resource concentration, and territorial contest value.

64. **Maher, C. R., and Lott, D. F. (1995). “Definitions of Territoriality Used in the Study of Variation in Vertebrate Spacing Systems.” *Animal Behaviour* 49: 1581–1597. https://doi.org/10.1016/0003-3472(95)90080-2.**  Documents variation in territoriality definitions. It supports the report’s component-based metrics and caution against binary observer labels.

65. **Maher, C. R., and Lott, D. F. (2000). “A Review of Ecological Determinants of Territoriality within Vertebrate Species.” *American Midland Naturalist* 143: 1–29. https://doi.org/10.1674/0003-0031(2000)143[0001:AROEDO]2.0.CO;2.**  Reviews within-species ecological predictors and finds territoriality highly conditional. It is a core source for crossing resource abundance, distribution, renewal, and defensibility.

66. **Araya-Salas, M., Gonzalez-Gomez, P., Wojczulanis-Jakubas, K., et al. (2018). “Spatial Memory Is as Important as Weapon and Body Size for Territorial Ownership in a Lekking Hummingbird.” *Scientific Reports* 8: 2001. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-20441-x.**  Connects cognition and spatial memory to territory acquisition. It supports learned place expectations as a minimally scripted territorial mechanism.

67. **Koch, F., Signer, J., Kappeler, P. M., and Fichtel, C. (2016). “The Role of the Residence-Effect on the Outcome of Intergroup Encounters in Verreaux’s Sifakas.” *Scientific Reports* 6: 28457. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep28457.**  Finds that encounter location and intensive area use predicted outcomes better than numerical advantage in the studied groups. It demonstrates that territorial value and residence can outweigh simple coalition size.

68. **Morrison, R. E., Dunn, J. C., Illera, G., et al. (2020). “Western Gorilla Space Use Suggests Territoriality.” *Scientific Reports* 10: 3692. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-60504-6.**  Uses avoidance and core-area use to argue for a territoriality continuum in western gorillas. It is useful precisely because active defense was not directly observed: spatial evidence can suggest but not fully establish territorial defense.

69. **Backwell, P. R. Y., and Jennions, M. D. (2004). “Coalition among Male Fiddler Crabs.” *Nature* 430: 417. https://doi.org/10.1038/430417a.**  Shows strategic assistance to a territorial neighbor, consistent with avoiding a more costly future neighbor. It provides a simple route from territorial familiarity to coalition behavior without kinship or complex faction identity.

70. **Crofoot, M. C. (2013). “The Cost of Defeat: Capuchin Groups Travel Further, Faster and Later after Losing Conflicts with Neighbours.” *American Journal of Physical Anthropology* 152: 79–85. https://doi.org/10.1002/ajpa.22307.**  Measures post-conflict spatial costs rather than only winners and losers. It motivates logging displacement, travel, lost access, and recovery after conflict.

71. **Markham, A. C., Alberts, S. C., and Altmann, J. (2012). “Intergroup Conflict: Ecological Predictors of Winning and Consequences of Defeat in a Wild Primate Population.” *Animal Behaviour* 84: 399–403. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2012.05.009.**  Links ecology, group composition, and post-encounter consequences in baboons. It supports treating outcome and consequence as separate dimensions.

72. **Temeles, E. J. (1994). “The Role of Neighbours in Territorial Systems: When Are They ‘Dear Enemies’?” *Animal Behaviour* 47: 339–350. https://doi.org/10.1006/anbe.1994.1047.**  Reviews neighbor–stranger discrimination and conditions under which familiar neighbors receive reduced aggression. It motivates learned individual recognition, stable boundaries, and migrant/intruder playback tests.

### 20.6 Coalition formation, intergroup aggression, and organized conflict

73. **Wrangham, R. W. (1999). “Evolution of Coalitionary Killing.” *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*, Supplement 29: 1–30. https://doi.org/10.1002/%28SICI%291096-8644%281999%29110%3A29%2B%3C1%3A%3AAID-AJPA2%3E3.0.CO%3B2-E.**  Develops the imbalance-of-power hypothesis: attackers can gain through coalitionary killing when numerical asymmetry makes personal risk low. This is a central testable hypothesis for Genesis, not a sufficient-condition claim.

74. **Wilson, M. L., and Wrangham, R. W. (2003). “Intergroup Relations in Chimpanzees.” *Annual Review of Anthropology* 32: 363–392. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.anthro.32.061002.120046.**  Reviews chimpanzee intergroup hostility, patrols, and demographic/ecological variation. It provides a broad empirical basis for separating boundary behavior, encounter response, and lethal attack.

75. **Wilson, M. L., Hauser, M. D., and Wrangham, R. W. (2001). “Does Participation in Intergroup Conflict Depend on Numerical Assessment, Range Location, or Rank for Wild Chimpanzees?” *Animal Behaviour* 61: 1203–1216. https://doi.org/10.1006/anbe.2000.1706.**  Tests predictors of participation and response in intergroup contexts. It directly motivates ally-number, location, rank, and opportunity controls.

76. **Mitani, J. C., Watts, D. P., and Amsler, S. J. (2010). “Lethal Intergroup Aggression Leads to Territorial Expansion in Wild Chimpanzees.” *Current Biology* 20: R507–R508. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2010.04.021.**  Links a long sequence of lethal attacks to later territorial expansion. It is unusually relevant to the requirement that conflict consequences be measured materially rather than inferred from attack frequency.

77. **Wilson, M. L., Boesch, C., Fruth, B., et al. (2014). “Lethal Aggression in *Pan* Is Better Explained by Adaptive Strategies than Human Impacts.” *Nature* 513: 414–417. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature13727.**  Comparative analysis across long-term chimpanzee and bonobo sites found lethal aggression associated with demographic variables and not the measured human-impact variables. The paper supports adaptive hypotheses while not establishing one universal motive for every attack.

78. **Samuni, L., Crockford, C., and Wittig, R. M. (2021). “Group-Level Cooperation in Chimpanzees Is Shaped by Strong Social Ties.” *Nature Communications* 12: 539. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-20709-9.**  Finds that strong social relationships are associated with participation in risky collective intergroup encounters. It supports multiplex social-memory models in which affiliative ties affect coalition participation.

79. **Massaro, A. P., Gilby, I. C., Deschner, T., et al. (2022). “Correlates of Individual Participation in Boundary Patrols by Male Chimpanzees.” *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 377: 20210151. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0151.**  Uses three decades of Gombe data and finds high participation with predictors including gregariousness, age, and hunting, challenging a simple free-rider account. It shows why participation opportunity and broad mutual benefits must be measured.

80. **Rosenbaum, S., Vecellio, V., and Stoinski, T. (2016). “Observations of Severe and Lethal Coalitionary Attacks in Wild Mountain Gorillas.” *Scientific Reports* 6: 37018. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep37018.**  Reports rare whole-group attacks arising after demographic changes produced large multimale groups, despite low food-resource stress. It is strong evidence against treating resource scarcity as necessary for coalitionary violence and highlights social structure and risk dilution.

81. **Packer, C., Gilbert, D. A., Pusey, A. E., and O’Brien, S. J. (1991). “A Molecular Genetic Analysis of Kinship and Cooperation in African Lions.” *Nature* 351: 562–565. https://doi.org/10.1038/351562a0.**  Combines genetic relatedness and coalition behavior in lions. It supports measuring both kinship and direct coalition benefits rather than assuming one excludes the other.

82. **Bygott, J. D., Bertram, B. C. R., and Hanby, J. P. (1979). “Male Lions in Large Coalitions Gain Reproductive Advantages.” *Nature* 282: 839–841. https://doi.org/10.1038/282839a0.**  Links coalition size to territorial and reproductive outcomes. It motivates reproductive-skew and coalition-size experiments while leaving open how participation is stabilized.

83. **Packer, C., and Pusey, A. E. (1982). “Cooperation and Competition within Coalitions of Male Lions: Kin Selection or Game Theory?” *Nature* 296: 740–742. https://doi.org/10.1038/296740a0.**  Explicitly compares kin-selected and strategic explanations. It exemplifies why Genesis should log relatedness, coalition payoff, and within-coalition competition together.

84. **Smith, J. E., Van Horn, R. C., Powning, K. S., et al. (2010). “Evolutionary Forces Favoring Intragroup Coalitions among Spotted Hyenas and Other Animals.” *Behavioral Ecology* 21: 284–303. https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arp181.**  Reviews coalition benefits, partner choice, rank, and kinship across animals. It offers a framework for distinguishing support, intervention, and coalition formation within as well as between groups.

85. **Dugatkin, L. A. (1998). “A Model of Coalition Formation in Animals.” *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 265: 2121–2125. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1998.0548.**  A theoretical treatment of coalition partner choice and payoff. Useful for generating falsifiable expectations about coalition stability without creating coalition objects in the engine.

86. **Kitchen, D. M., and Beehner, J. C. (2007). “Factors Affecting Individual Participation in Group-Level Aggression among Non-Human Primates.” *Behaviour* 144: 1551–1581. https://doi.org/10.1163/156853907782512074.**  Reviews sex, rank, kinship, group size, location, and expected benefit as predictors of participation. It is a compact guide to the covariates Genesis must log before attributing collective action.

87. **Cant, M. A., Otali, E., and Mwanguhya, F. (2002). “Fighting and Mating between Groups in a Cooperatively Breeding Mammal, the Banded Mongoose.” *Ethology* 108: 541–555. https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1439-0310.2002.00795.x.**  Connects intergroup encounters with both aggression and mating. It supports treating reproductive opportunities, migration, and resource competition as potentially simultaneous motives.

88. **Tan, J., Ariely, D., and Hare, B. (2017). “Bonobos Respond Prosocially toward Members of Other Groups.” *Scientific Reports* 7: 14733. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-15320-w.**  Demonstrates prosocial behavior toward out-group individuals in bonobos. It is an important counterexample to assumptions that group boundaries inherently produce hostility.

89. **Pisor, A. C., and Surbeck, M. (2019). “The Evolution of Intergroup Tolerance in Nonhuman Primates and Humans.” *Evolutionary Anthropology* 28: 210–223. https://doi.org/10.1002/evan.21793.**  Reviews conditions supporting tolerant intergroup interaction. It broadens the design space beyond conflict and reinforces measurement of cross-group exchange and affiliation.

90. **Bowles, S. (2009). “Did Warfare among Ancestral Hunter-Gatherers Affect the Evolution of Human Social Behaviors?” *Science* 324: 1293–1298. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1168112.**  Models whether estimated intergroup mortality and between-group differentiation could favor parochial altruism. The estimates and historical inferences are debated; the paper should be treated as a quantitative hypothesis, not a settled account of human evolution.

91. **Fry, D. P., and Söderberg, P. (2013). “Lethal Aggression in Mobile Forager Bands and Implications for the Origins of War.” *Science* 341: 270–273. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1235675.**  Reports that most lethal events in the sampled mobile forager societies were interpersonal rather than organized warfare. It provides a direct caution against inferring war from aggregate lethal-violence rates.

92. **Kelly, R. C. (2005). “The Evolution of Lethal Intergroup Violence.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 102: 15294–15298. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0505955102.**  Argues that war depends on a social logic of substitutability and group responsibility, not merely individual aggression. This underscores how far recurrent harmful encounters can remain from human institutional warfare.

93. **Wiessner, P. (2006). “From Spears to M-16s: Testing the Imbalance of Power Hypothesis among the Enga.” *Journal of Anthropological Research* 62: 165–191. https://doi.org/10.3998/jar.0521004.0062.203.**  Examines how weapon changes and political organization altered conflict dynamics. It demonstrates that damage technology interacts with social institutions and cannot be analyzed as an isolated lethality parameter.

94. **Mirazón Lahr, M., Rivera, F., Power, R. K., et al. (2016). “Inter-Group Violence among Early Holocene Hunter-Gatherers of West Turkana, Kenya.” *Nature* 529: 394–398. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16477.**  Interprets the Nataruk remains as evidence of intergroup violence among hunter-gatherers. It is relevant both as archaeological evidence and as a case whose contextual interpretation was subsequently disputed.

95. **Stojanowski, C. M., Seidel, A. C., Fulginiti, L. C., Johnson, K. M., and Buikstra, J. E. (2016). “Contesting the Massacre at Nataruk.” *Nature* 539: E8–E10. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature19778.**  Challenges aspects of the Nataruk massacre interpretation, illustrating how spatial context, deposition, and sample reconstruction affect claims about organized violence.

96. **Crevecoeur, I., Dias-Meirinho, M.-H., Zazzo, A., et al. (2021). “New Insights on Interpersonal Violence in the Late Pleistocene Based on the Nile Valley Cemetery of Jebel Sahaba.” *Scientific Reports* 11: 9991. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-89386-y.**  Reanalysis suggests repeated episodes of violence rather than one simple massacre event. It reinforces the distinction between recurrent violence and a single organized conflict episode.

97. **Meyer, C., Knipper, C., Nicklisch, N., et al. (2018). “Early Neolithic Executions Indicated by Clustered Cranial Trauma in the Mass Grave of Halberstadt.” *Nature Communications* 9: 2472. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-04773-w.**  Combines trauma, demographic, and isotopic evidence to interpret organized killing. It shows the multi-evidence burden required before assigning social organization to skeletal violence.

98. **Wrangham, R. W., and Glowacki, L. (2012). “Intergroup Aggression in Chimpanzees and War in Nomadic Hunter-Gatherers: Evaluating the Chimpanzee Model.” *Human Nature* 23: 5–29. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-012-9132-1.**  Compares chimpanzee raiding with small-scale human warfare while noting differences. It is useful for defining matched operational variables without collapsing the phenomena into one category.

### 20.7 Social learning, traditions, and cultural evolution

99. **Whiten, A., Goodall, J., McGrew, W. C., et al. (1999). “Cultures in Chimpanzees.” *Nature* 399: 682–685. https://doi.org/10.1038/21415.**  Catalogues community-level behavioral variation and argues that a substantial subset is consistent with culture after ecological and genetic exclusions. It is foundational but also illustrates the limits of the method of exclusion when communities differ in many ways.

100. **Whiten, A., Horner, V., and de Waal, F. B. M. (2005). “Conformity to Cultural Norms of Tool Use in Chimpanzees.” *Nature* 437: 737–740. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04047.**  Seeds alternative tool techniques in different groups and observes persistent group-typical variants. It motivates arbitrary-equivalent variant experiments and exposure-aware conformity tests.

101. **Laland, K. N., and Janik, V. M. (2006). “The Animal Cultures Debate.” *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 21: 542–547. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2006.06.005.**  Reviews disputes over definitions and evidence for animal culture. It supports graded claims and explicit exclusion of genetic and ecological alternatives.

102. **Hoppitt, W., and Laland, K. N. (2013). *Social Learning: An Introduction to Mechanisms, Methods, and Models*. Princeton University Press.**  A comprehensive treatment of social-learning mechanisms and inference. It informs demonstrator controls, diffusion analysis, and the distinction between stimulus enhancement, emulation, imitation, and teaching.

103. **Franz, M., and Nunn, C. L. (2009). “Network-Based Diffusion Analysis: A New Method for Detecting Social Learning.” *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 276: 1829–1836. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2008.1824.**  Introduces a method that tests whether behavioral acquisition follows a social network. It is highly relevant to Genesis, but endogenous network formation still requires randomized or counterfactual validation where possible.

104. **Hoppitt, W., Boogert, N. J., and Laland, K. N. (2010). “Detecting Social Transmission in Networks.” *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 263: 544–555. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2010.01.004.**  Extends network diffusion methods and clarifies model assumptions. It supports event-level exposure logging and explicit asocial learning rates.

105. **Hobaiter, C., Poisot, T., Zuberbühler, K., Hoppitt, W., and Gruber, T. (2014). “Social Network Analysis Shows Direct Evidence for Social Transmission of Tool Use in Wild Chimpanzees.” *PLoS Biology* 12: e1001960. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1001960.**  Tracks a novel tool-use behavior spreading along social-network pathways in the wild. It is a model for combining behavioral innovation, opportunity networks, and diffusion analysis.

106. **Gruber, T., Muller, M. N., Strimling, P., Wrangham, R., and Zuberbühler, K. (2011). “Community-Specific Evaluation of Tool Affordances in Wild Chimpanzees.” *Scientific Reports* 1: 128. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep00128.**  Finds community differences in how a standardized object’s affordances were evaluated. It motivates testing whether traditions alter attention and affordance learning, not merely action frequencies.

107. **Musgrave, S., Morgan, D., Lonsdorf, E., Mundry, R., and Sanz, C. (2016). “Tool Transfers Are a Form of Teaching among Chimpanzees.” *Scientific Reports* 6: 34783. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep34783.**  Reports costly tool transfers that improve learners’ performance under a functional definition of teaching. It provides measurable criteria—teacher cost, learner benefit, and behavior contingent on learner presence—without requiring mental-state attribution.

108. **van Leeuwen, E. J. C., DeTroy, S. E., Haun, D. B. M., et al. (2024). “Chimpanzees Use Social Information to Acquire a Skill They Fail to Innovate.” *Nature Human Behaviour* 8: 891–902. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01836-5.**  Demonstrates acquisition of a complex task after trained models were introduced following an extended unsuccessful baseline. It is strong evidence for socially enabled acquisition beyond immediate independent innovation and a useful template for seeded capability tests.

109. **Bandini, E., and Tennie, C. (2023). “Naïve, Adult, Captive Chimpanzees Do Not Socially Learn How to Make and Use Sharp Stone Tools.” *Scientific Reports* 13: 22733. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-49780-0.**  A valuable negative result showing that demonstrations do not make every complex behavior socially learnable. It reinforces the need for falsification and for distinguishing observational access from representational capacity.

110. **Boyd, R., and Richerson, P. J. (2009). “Culture and the Evolution of Human Cooperation.” *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 364: 3281–3288. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0134.**  Reviews how norms, social learning, and cultural group differences may support large-scale cooperation. Genesis can test simpler components, but should not import norm enforcement or cultural groups as primitives.

111. **Mathew, S., and Perreault, C. (2015). “Behavioural Variation in 172 Small-Scale Societies Indicates That Social Learning Is the Main Mode of Human Adaptation.” *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 282: 20150061. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2015.0061.**  Uses cross-cultural behavioral variation to argue for a large role of social learning. It is relevant to comparing within- and between-group variation, while the observational design does not by itself identify every transmission mechanism.

112. **Henrich, J., and Boyd, R. (1998). “The Evolution of Conformist Transmission and the Emergence of Between-Group Differences.” *Evolution and Human Behavior* 19: 215–241. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1090-5138(98)00018-X.**  Models how conformist transmission can maintain group differences despite migration. It motivates neutral-variant tests that separate majority bias from repeated exposure and payoff bias.

113. **Whiten, A. (2019). “Cultural Evolution in Animals.” *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics* 50: 27–48. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ecolsys-110218-025040.**  Reviews animal traditions, diffusion experiments, conformity, and cumulative culture. It provides a broad evidence map and emphasizes variation in cultural complexity across taxa.

### 20.8 Artificial life, evolutionary robotics, swarm systems, and multi-agent emergence

114. **Waibel, M., Keller, L., and Floreano, D. (2009). “Genetic Team Composition and Level of Selection in the Evolution of Cooperation.” *IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation* 13: 648–660. https://doi.org/10.1109/TEVC.2008.2011741.**  Evolves cooperation in robot teams under different genetic compositions and selection regimes. It directly demonstrates that assortment and selection architecture alter cooperative outcomes in embodied agents.

115. **Waibel, M., Floreano, D., and Keller, L. (2011). “A Quantitative Test of Hamilton’s Rule for the Evolution of Altruism.” *PLoS Biology* 9: e1000615. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1000615.**  Uses evolving robots to test relatedness, benefit, and cost predictions. It is a strong precedent for measuring realized actor/recipient effects in an artificial embodied system.

116. **Mitri, S., Floreano, D., and Keller, L. (2009). “The Evolution of Information Suppression in Communicating Robots with Conflicting Interests.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 106: 15786–15790. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903152106.**  Shows that communication can become deceptive or suppressed when sender and receiver interests diverge. It supports separate signal-production and response genes and explicit payoff analysis.

117. **Floreano, D., and Keller, L. (2010). “Evolution of Adaptive Behaviour in Robots by Means of Darwinian Selection.” *PLoS Biology* 8: e1000292. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1000292.**  Reviews evolutionary robotics as an experimental tool for behavior and social evolution. It supports the use of embodied, evolvable controllers while emphasizing the consequences of selection design.

118. **Werfel, J., Petersen, K., and Nagpal, R. (2014). “Designing Collective Behavior in a Termite-Inspired Robot Construction Team.” *Science* 343: 754–758. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1245842.**  Demonstrates decentralized construction using local sensing and stigmergic rules. Although engineered rather than evolved, it establishes that persistent structures can coordinate agents without explicit group commands.

119. **Pini, G., Brutschy, A., Frison, M., Roli, A., Dorigo, M., and Birattari, M. (2014). “Task Partitioning in a Robot Swarm: Object Retrieval as a Sequence of Subtasks with Direct Object Transfer.” *Artificial Life* 20: 291–317. https://doi.org/10.1162/ARTL_a_00134.**  Studies decentralized task partitioning and direct object handoff in a robot swarm. It informs physical transfer, division-of-labor metrics, and coordination costs.

120. **Garbus, J., and Pollack, J. (2024). “Emergent Resource Exchange and Tolerated Theft Behavior Using Multiagent Reinforcement Learning.” *Artificial Life* 30: 28–47. https://doi.org/10.1162/artl_a_00423.**  Reports emergent resource exchange through dropping and pickup around a congregation-promoting campfire, without a dedicated punishment or theft mechanism. It is unusually aligned with Genesis’s preference for neutral physical affordances and highlights the causal role of shared sites and downtime.

121. **Ichinose, G., Miyagawa, D., Chiba, E., and Sayama, H. (2023). “How Lévy Flights Triggered by the Presence of Defectors Affect Evolution of Cooperation in Spatial Games.” *Artificial Life* 29: 187–197. https://doi.org/10.1162/artl_a_00382.**  Shows that movement and sensitivity to defectors interact with density to shape cooperation. It reinforces the point that mobility is a social mechanism, not merely a transport detail.

122. **Groß, R., and Dorigo, M. (2008). “Evolution of Solitary and Group Transport Behaviors for Autonomous Robots Capable of Self-Assembling.” *Adaptive Behavior* 16: 285–305. https://doi.org/10.1177/1059712308090537.**  Evolves group transport in robots that can grasp objects and one another, including cases where effective cooperation emerges without explicit awareness of partners. It is relevant to distinguishing true social contingency from compatible individual mechanics that happen to produce joint success.

123. **Epstein, J. M. (2002). “Modeling Civil Violence: An Agent-Based Computational Approach.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 99(Suppl. 3): 7243–7250. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.092080199.**  A well-known agent-based model of civil violence and repression. Its predefined categories and grievance/risk variables make it unsuitable as a Genesis emergence template, but useful as a contrast between explanatory social simulation and open-ended artificial life.

124. **Axelrod, R. (1997). “The Dissemination of Culture: A Model with Local Convergence and Global Polarization.” *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 41: 203–226. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022002797041002001.**  Shows how local similarity-biased interaction can generate global cultural regions. It is useful for observer hypotheses, but its explicit cultural feature vectors and homophily should not be adopted uncritically as organism inputs.

125. **Trianni, V., Nolfi, S., and Dorigo, M. (2008). “Evolution, Self-Organization and Swarm Robotics.” In *Swarm Intelligence*, 163–191. Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-74089-6_5.**  Reviews the relationship among evolutionary design, local interaction, and self-organized swarm behavior. It provides a useful taxonomy for reactive collective mechanisms while clarifying that engineered swarm objectives differ from open-ended evolution.

126. **Bedau, M. A., McCaskill, J. S., Packard, N. H., et al. (2000). “Open Problems in Artificial Life.” *Artificial Life* 6: 363–376. https://doi.org/10.1162/106454600300103683.**  Identifies major challenges including open-ended evolution, emergent organization, and transitions in individuality. It frames the broader ambition but also the difficulty of making complexity persist and expand.

127. **Taylor, T., Bedau, M., Channon, A., et al. (2016). “Open-Ended Evolution: Perspectives from the OEE Workshop in York.” *Artificial Life* 22: 408–423. https://doi.org/10.1162/ARTL_a_00210.**  Surveys conceptual and methodological views on open-ended evolution. It supports treating social complexity as a possibility requiring evidence rather than an expected stage sequence.

128. **Packard, N., Bedau, M. A., Channon, A., et al. (2019). “An Overview of Open-Ended Evolution: Editorial Introduction to the Open-Ended Evolution II Special Issue.” *Artificial Life* 25: 93–133. https://doi.org/10.1162/artl_a_00291.**  Reviews open-endedness, novelty, and evolving complexity across artificial systems. It is relevant to maintaining variation and avoiding finite designer-specified social objectives.

129. **Sayama, H., and Nehaniv, C. L. (2025). “Self-Reproduction and Evolution in Cellular Automata: 25 Years after Evoloops.” *Artificial Life* 31: 81–95. https://doi.org/10.1162/artl_a_00451.**  Reviews deterministic spatial artificial-life systems and their evolutionary limitations. While not focused on social behavior, it informs the broader challenge of preserving emergent organization without external scripting.

### 20.9 Methods, null models, causal inference, and deterministic computation

130. **Farine, D. R. (2017). “A Guide to Null Models for Animal Social Network Analysis.” *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 8: 1309–1320. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12772.**  Explains why randomization must preserve data-generating constraints and interaction opportunity. It directly supports opportunity-matched nulls for grouping and coordination.

131. **Bejder, L., Fletcher, D., and Bräger, S. (1998). “A Method for Testing Association Patterns of Social Animals.” *Animal Behaviour* 56: 719–725. https://doi.org/10.1006/anbe.1998.0802.**  Presents permutation-based testing of association patterns. It is a useful foundation for distinguishing preferred partnerships from shared space and sampling effects.

132. **Hobson, E. A., Silk, M. J., Fefferman, N. H., et al. (2021). “A Guide to Choosing and Implementing Reference Models for Social Network Analysis.” *Biological Reviews* 96: 2716–2734. https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.12775.**  Emphasizes that reference models must correspond to the biological question and data process. This is directly applicable to every Genesis claim based on temporal networks.

133. **Butts, C. T. (2008). “Social Network Analysis: A Methodological Introduction.” *Asian Journal of Social Psychology* 11: 13–41. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-839X.2007.00241.x.**  Provides a broad methodological overview of network representation and inference. It is useful for maintaining distinctions among nodes, ties, observations, and latent social structure.

134. **Salmon, J. K., Moraes, M. A., Dror, R. O., and Shaw, D. E. (2011). “Parallel Random Numbers: As Easy as 1, 2, 3.” In *Proceedings of the International Conference for High Performance Computing, Networking, Storage and Analysis*. https://doi.org/10.1145/2063384.2063405.**  Introduces counter-based random-number generation suitable for parallel deterministic simulations. It directly supports named, key-addressable RNG streams that do not depend on traversal order.

135. **Monniaux, D. (2008). “The Pitfalls of Verifying Floating-Point Computations.” *ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems* 30(3): Article 12. https://doi.org/10.1145/1353445.1353446.**  Explains how compiler, architecture, and extended-precision behavior can undermine apparently exact floating-point reasoning. It supports fixed-point authoritative state or tightly specified floating-point profiles.

136. **Sandve, G. K., Nekrutenko, A., Taylor, J., and Hovig, E. (2013). “Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Computational Research.” *PLoS Computational Biology* 9: e1003285. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003285.**  A concise reproducibility framework covering provenance, versioning, raw data, and automation. It supports manifests, immutable event streams, and rerunnable observer pipelines.

137. **Peng, R. D. (2011). “Reproducible Research in Computational Science.” *Science* 334: 1226–1227. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1213847.**  Frames reproducibility as a minimum scientific standard for computation. Genesis goes further with exact deterministic replay, but the same provenance principles apply to offline analyses.

138. **Pearl, J. (2009). *Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference*, 2nd ed. Cambridge University Press.**  Provides the formal distinction between association, intervention, and counterfactual reasoning. It underlies the recommendation to branch from checkpoints and define causal estimands rather than rely on correlations.

139. **Hernán, M. A., and Robins, J. M. (2020). *Causal Inference: What If*. Chapman & Hall/CRC.**  A practical treatment of causal questions, confounding, and target estimands. It is useful for distinguishing short-horizon intervention effects from long-run evolutionary treatment effects.

140. **Gelman, A., Hill, J., and Vehtari, A. (2020). *Regression and Other Stories*. Cambridge University Press.**  Provides practical guidance on multilevel modeling, prediction, and uncertainty. It supports seed-level inference and hierarchical treatment of organisms, dyads, groups, and episodes.

141. **Lakens, D. (2022). “Sample Size Justification.” *Collabra: Psychology* 8: 33267. https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.33267.**  Reviews defensible ways to justify sample size beyond arbitrary conventions. For Genesis, simulation-based power and precision targets should determine independent seed counts.

142. **Nosek, B. A., Ebersole, C. R., DeHaven, A. C., and Mellor, D. T. (2018). “The Preregistration Revolution.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 115: 2600–2606. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708274114.**  Explains how preregistration separates confirmatory and exploratory analysis. This is especially important in visually rich simulations with many possible thresholds and narratives.

### 20.10 Recent 2026 developments

143. **De Dreu, C. K. W. (2026). “Coalitionary Conflict as Ultimate Cause or By-Product of Cooperation.” *Small Group Research* 57(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/10464964251387353.**  Argues that coalitionary conflict need not be the evolutionary cause of cooperation and may instead exploit prior capacities for cooperation and parochial solidarity. The article represents a current theoretical position rather than a settled resolution; for Genesis, its main value is to require experiments on both causal directions and to avoid assuming pre-existing groups or out-group hostility.

144. **Morozov, A. V., and Feigel, A. (2026). “Emergence of Cooperation Due to Opponent-Specific Responses in Prisoner’s Dilemma.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 123(21): e2513282123. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2513282123.**  Shows theoretically that high cooperation can evolve when behavior is conditioned on opponent identity without imposed genetic or spatial structure or an explicit reciprocity rule. Because the mechanism still requires consistent opponent recognition across repeated encounters, it supports bounded perceptual identity as a minimal Genesis treatment while leaving embodiment, error, migration, and ecological robustness as open tests.

### 20.11 Bibliographic synthesis for Genesis

Across these sources, the strongest recurring conclusions are conditional rather than universal:

- local interaction can create visually coherent groups with almost no cognition;
- stable differentiated relationships require repeated opportunity, recognition, memory, or shared dependence;
- kin bias can arise from assortment without active recognition, while active recognition requires evolvable cues;
- cooperation has multiple routes, and mechanisms often interact nonadditively;
- territoriality depends more on defendability, site value, memory, and contest cost than on scarcity alone;
- damage enables injury but does not create coalitions, persistent groups, opponent recognition, or recurrent organized conflict;
- coalitionary aggression becomes more plausible when attackers can coordinate and reduce individual risk through numerical asymmetry;
- social learning can stabilize group-specific behavior, but genetic, ecological, artifact-mediated, and independent-learning alternatives must be tested;
- artificial systems readily produce target behavior when tags, teams, rewards, or tasks encode it directly, so neutral affordances and observer separation are essential;
- exact deterministic replay is a necessary engineering property, not a substitute for controls, independent seeds, or valid causal definitions.

The bibliography therefore does not imply a single canonical path from aggregation to conflict. It supports the staged, ablation-driven program in this report: establish each prerequisite independently, retain peaceful alternatives, and admit stronger social labels only when their operational and causal criteria are met.
